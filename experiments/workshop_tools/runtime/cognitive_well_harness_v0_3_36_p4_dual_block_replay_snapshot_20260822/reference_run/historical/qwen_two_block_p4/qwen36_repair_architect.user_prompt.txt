## Problem
IMO 2026 Problem 4. Shan-Yu and Mulan are playing a game. Let theta be an angle with 0 degrees < theta < 180 degrees, known to both players. Initially, Shan-Yu makes a paper triangle T with measurements of his choice. Then they repeatedly perform the following steps. If T has at least one angle measuring exactly theta, the game stops and Mulan wins. Otherwise, Mulan chooses a point P on the perimeter of T, different from its three vertices, and makes a straight cut from P to the opposite vertex of T, splitting it into two triangles. Shan-Yu discards one of the two triangles; the remaining triangle becomes the new T. For which real values of theta can Mulan guarantee victory in finitely many steps, no matter how Shan-Yu plays?

## Candidate proofa
To determine the values of $\theta$ for which Mulan can guarantee victory, we analyze the game using the concept of a "winning set" of triangles.

**1. Definition of the Winning Set**
Let $W$ be the set of triangles from which Mulan can force a win in finitely many steps. A triangle $T$ is in $W$ if:
(i) $T$ has at least one angle equal to $\theta$.
(ii) There exists a cut from a vertex to the opposite side that divides $T$ into two triangles $T_1$ and $T_2$ such that both $T_1 \in W$ and $T_2 \in W$.

**2. The Case where $180^\circ/\theta = n$ for some integer $n \ge 2$**
Assume $\theta = 180^\circ/n$. Let $S = \{ k\theta \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}^+, 0 < k\theta < 180^\circ \}$. We claim that any triangle containing an angle $\alpha \in S$ is in $W$.
We prove this by induction on $k$ for an angle $\alpha = k\theta$:
- **Base case ($k=1$):** If $T$ has an angle $\theta$, then $T \in W$ by definition.
- **Inductive step:** Assume any triangle with an angle $m\theta$ for $1 \le m < k$ is in $W$. Consider a triangle $T$ with an angle $k\theta$. Mulan can cut this angle into $\theta$ and $(k-1)\theta$. The two resulting triangles, $T_1$ and $T_2$, will have angles $\theta$ and $(k-1)\theta$ respectively. Since $\theta \in S$ and $(k-1)\theta \in S$ (and $k-1 < k$), both $T_1, T_2 \in W$ by the inductive hypothesis. Thus, $T \in W$.

Now we show that any triangle $T(A, B, C)$ is in $W$ if $180^\circ/\theta = n$.
Mulan chooses a vertex, say $C$, and makes a cut to the opposite side $AB$. Let $\phi$ be the angle $\angle APC$ (where $P$ is the cut point). The range of $\phi$ is $(A, A+C)$. The length of this interval is $C$.
- If any angle of $T$ is in $S$, then $T \in W$ by the previous lemma.
- If no angle is in $S$, Mulan can win if she can choose $\phi$ such that both $\phi \in S$ and $180^\circ - \phi \in S$.
If $\phi = k\theta$, then $180^\circ - \phi = 180^\circ - k\theta = (n-k)\theta$. Both $k\theta$ and $(n-k)\theta$ are in $S$. Thus, if $\phi = k\theta$, then $T_1 \in W$ and $T_2 \in W$.
Since at least one angle of $T$ must be $\ge 60^\circ$, and $n \ge 2$ implies $\theta \le 90^\circ$, at least one angle (say $C$) satisfies $C \ge \theta$ unless $n=2$ (where $C$ could be $< 90^\circ$). In any case, for $n \ge 2$, the interval $(A, A+C)$ contains at least one multiple $k\theta$ unless $A$ is already a multiple of $\theta$. Specifically, if $C \ge \theta$, the interval $(A, A+C)$ must contain a multiple of $\theta$ unless $A = m\theta$, in which case $T$ already contains an angle in $S$. 
Thus, Mulan can always force a win if $180^\circ/\theta$ is an integer $n \ge 2$.

**3. The Case where $180^\circ/\theta$ is not an integer**
If $180^\circ/\theta \notin \mathbb{Z}$, then there are no positive integers $k, m$ such that $k\theta + m\theta = 180^\circ$. 
Let $S = \{ k\theta \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}^+ \}$. We claim that if $T \in W$, then $T$ must have an angle in $S$.
- If $T$ has an angle $\theta$, then it has an angle in $S$.
- If $T$ does not have an angle in $S$, for $T$ to be in $W$, Mulan must split it into $T_1, T_2 \in W$. By the inductive assumption, both $T_1$ and $T_2$ must contain an angle in $S$. Since $T$ had no such angle, these angles must be the ones created by the cut: $\phi$ and $180^\circ - \phi$.
- This requires $\phi = k\theta$ and $180^\circ - \phi = m\theta$, which implies $180^\circ = (k+m)\theta$, contradicting that $180^\circ/\theta$ is not an integer.
Thus, if $180^\circ/\theta \notin \mathbb{Z}$, Mulan can only win if $T$ initially has an angle in $S$. Since Shan-Yu chooses $T$, he can choose a triangle (e.g., equilateral) with no angles in $S$ (unless $\theta = 60^\circ$ or $120^\circ$, but these divide $180^\circ$ evenly).

The values of $\theta$ are those for which $180^\circ/\theta = n$ for $n \in \{2, 3, 4, \dots\}$.
