The candidate verification is correct: \(f\equiv 0\) and \(f(x)=-x+c\) do satisfy the equation.

The argument for \(c=f(0)\ne 0\) is essentially valid. From
\[
f(c)=0,\qquad f(c+f(b))=b-c,
\]
one obtains injectivity. Then \(P(a,a)\) gives \(f(a+f(a))=0\) for \(a\ne0\), so injectivity and \(f(c)=0\) imply \(a+f(a)=c\); the case \(a=0\) follows directly from \(f(0)=c\). Thus \(f(a)=-a+c\).

However, the entire case \(f(0)=0\) is not handled. In particular, \(f(0)=0\) does not imply \(f(f(a))=0\) for every \(a\); for example, the valid solution \(f(a)=-a\) has \(f(f(a))=a\). The assertion that the composition-zero case “previously” leads to \(f\equiv0\) is also not proved and does not exhaust the \(f(0)=0\) case.

A complete treatment would derive from \(P(a,0)\) that
\[
f(f(a))=-f(a),
\]
then use the original equation and \(P(b,b)\) to conclude that either \(f\equiv0\) or \(f(b)=-b\) for all \(b\). Since this major branch is missing, the proof is not almost complete. It does, however, establish exactly the partial-credit result that either \(f(0)=0\) or \(f(x)=-x+c\).
