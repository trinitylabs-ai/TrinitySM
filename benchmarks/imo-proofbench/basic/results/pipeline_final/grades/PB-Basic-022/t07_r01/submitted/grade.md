The proposed classification is correct, and taking \(n=0\) implicitly gives the useful observation that \(P\) has an integer root. However, the necessity proof has several major gaps:

- From
  \[
  x_n=C_jn^{k/d}+D_j+o(1)
  \]
  on an arbitrary infinite subset \(S_j\), it does not follow that \(k/d\) is an integer. For example, \(n^{1/2}\) is an integer on the infinite set of squares.
- Even if \(x_n-Q(n)\to0\) with \(x_n\in\mathbb Z\), eventual equality \(x_n=Q(n)\) does not follow; \(Q(n)=n+1/n\) is a simple counterexample. Hence the crucial identity \(P(Q(x))=x^k\) is unsupported.
- The claim that \(c\in\mathbb Q\) and \(c^d=1/a_d\) with \(a_d\in\mathbb Z\) forces \(c\in\mathbb Z\) is false: \(c=1/2\), \(a_d=2^d\) is a counterexample.

Thus the correct answer is reached through fundamentally unjustified steps, not merely minor mistakes. The implicit integer-root observation merits only the specified partial credit.
