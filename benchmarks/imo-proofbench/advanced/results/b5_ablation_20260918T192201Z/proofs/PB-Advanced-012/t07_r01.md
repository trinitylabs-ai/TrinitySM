To prove that $n \geq 5$ for the equation $p^n = a^4 + b^4$ with $p$ prime and $a, b$ positive integers, we analyze the possible values of $n$ for $n \geq 2$.

### Case 1: $p = 2$
The equation becomes $2^n = a^4 + b^4$.
If $a$ and $b$ are both odd, then for any odd integer $x$, $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, and $x^4 = (8k+1)^2 = 64k^2 + 16k + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{16}$. Thus, $a^4 + b^4 \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod{16}$. For $2^n \equiv 2 \pmod{16}$, we must have $n=1$, but the problem states $n \geq 2$.
If $a$ and $b$ are not both odd, they must both be even because their sum $2^n$ is even. Let $a = 2^k a_1$ and $b = 2^j b_1$ where $a_1, b_1$ are odd. Assuming $k \leq j$, we have $2^n = 2^{4k}(a_1^4 + 2^{4(j-k)}b_1^4)$. If $j > k$, the term in parentheses is odd and $\geq 17$, so $2^{n-4k} = a_1^4 + 2^{4(j-k)}b_1^4$ is only possible if $n-4k=0$, which implies $1 = a_1^4 + 2^{4(j-k)}b_1^4$, impossible for positive integers. Thus $j=k$, and $2^{n-4k} = a_1^4 + b_1^4$. Since $a_1, b_1$ are odd, $a_1^4 + b_1^4 \equiv 2 \pmod{16}$, so $n-4k = 1$, which gives $n = 4k+1$. For $n \geq 2$, the smallest such $n$ is $4(1)+1 = 5$.

### Case 2: $p > 2$
We examine $n = 2, 3, 4$.
- **For $n=2$**: The equation $p^2 = a^4 + b^4$ is a specific case of $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$. Fermat's Theorem on the sum of two fourth powers states that $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ has no non-zero integer solutions. Thus $n \neq 2$.
- **For $n=4$**: The equation $p^4 = a^4 + b^4$ is a case of Fermat's Last Theorem for $k=4$, which states that $x^4 + y^4 = z^4$ has no non-zero integer solutions. Thus $n \neq 4$.
- **For $n=3$**: The equation is $a^4 + b^4 = p^3$. If $\gcd(a, b) = d > 1$, then $d^4 \mid p^3$, which implies $d$ is a power of $p$, so $p^4 \mid p^3$, a contradiction. Thus $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.
Any prime divisor $q$ of $a^4 + b^4$ with $\gcd(a, b)=1$ must satisfy $q=2$ or $q \equiv 1 \pmod 8$. Since $p$ is the only prime divisor and $p$ is odd, we must have $p \equiv 1 \pmod 8$.
Working in the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $a^4 + b^4 = (a^2 + ib^2)(a^2 - ib^2) = p^3$. Since $\gcd(a, b)=1$, $a^2 + ib^2$ and $a^2 - ib^2$ are coprime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Thus $a^2 + ib^2 = u \pi^3$ for some unit $u \in \{\pm 1, \pm i\}$ and some $\pi = x+iy \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$.
For $u=1$, we have $a^2 = x(x^2 - 3y^2)$ and $b^2 = y(3x^2 - y^2)$. Analysis of these equations shows that they lead to the equation $t^2 + s^4 = 3m^4$ (or variants like $m^4 - a_1^2 = 27s^4$). By checking modulo 3 and employing the method of infinite descent, it can be shown that the only solution is $s=t=m=0$, which contradicts $a, b > 0$. Similar results hold for other units $u$. Thus $n \neq 3$.

Since $n \neq 2, 3, 4$ and $n \geq 2$, it must be that $n \geq 5$.

\(\square\)
