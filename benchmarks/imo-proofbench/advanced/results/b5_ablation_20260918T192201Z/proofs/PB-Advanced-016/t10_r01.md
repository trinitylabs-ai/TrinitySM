To prove that the line $IP$ is parallel to the line $XY$, we use a combination of geometric and trigonometric properties.

**1. Properties of Point $L$**
Let $\angle A, \angle B, \angle C$ be the angles of $\triangle ABC$. Let $I$ be the incenter. The line $AI$ is the internal angle bisector of $\angle A$. Let $L$ be the intersection of the line passing through $I$ perpendicular to $AI$ and the line $BC$. 
In $\triangle AIL$, $\angle AIL = 90^\circ$. Let $\theta = \angle LAI$. Then $\tan \theta = \frac{IL}{AI}$.
The distance from $I$ to the sides is the inradius $r$. Thus $AI = \frac{r}{\sin(A/2)}$. 
Using the Law of Sines in $\triangle B I L$, where $\angle I B L = B/2$ and $\angle B L I = 180^\circ - (B/2 + \angle B I L)$, and noting that $\angle B I L = \angle B I A - \angle L I A = (90^\circ + C/2) - 90^\circ = C/2$, we have:
$\frac{IL}{\sin(B/2)} = \frac{BI}{\sin(90^\circ + A/2)} \implies IL = \frac{r \sin(B/2)}{\sin(B/2) \cos(A/2)} = \frac{r}{\cos(A/2)}$.
Hence, $\tan \theta = \frac{r/\cos(A/2)}{r/\sin(A/2)} = \tan(A/2)$, implying $\angle LAI = A/2$. However, $L$ is on $BC$, not $AB$ or $AC$, so we must re-evaluate the position of $L$ relative to $AI$.
Using coordinates or the Law of Sines more carefully, $\tan \angle LAI = \frac{a \tan(A/2)}{|b-c|}$. Let $\theta = \angle LAI$.

**2. Distances $IX$ and $IY$**
$X$ is on $\odot(ILC)$, so $\angle IXL = \angle ICL = C/2$. In $\triangle IUX$, by the Law of Sines:
$IX = \frac{IU \sin \angle IUX}{\sin(C/2)}$.
Similarly, $Y$ is on $\odot(ILB)$, so $\angle IYL = \angle IBL = B/2$. In $\triangle IVY$:
$IY = \frac{IV \sin \angle IVY}{\sin(B/2)}$.
From $\triangle IUL$ and $\triangle IVL$, using the Law of Sines and the fact that $\angle LAU = \theta + A/2$ and $\angle LAV = \theta - A/2$ (assuming $c > b$), we find:
$\frac{IX}{IY} = \frac{AU \sin(\theta + A/2)}{\sin(C/2)} \frac{\sin(B/2)}{AV \sin(\theta - A/2)} = \frac{a+b}{a+c} \frac{\sin(B/2)}{\sin(C/2)} \frac{a+c-b}{a+b-c}$.

**3. Parallelism of $IP$ and $XY$**
Let $I$ be the origin. $\vec{X} = IX \cdot \mathbf{u}_X$ and $\vec{Y} = IY \cdot \mathbf{u}_Y$. Point $P$ is the intersection of $YB$ and $XC$. The condition $IP \parallel XY$ is equivalent to $\vec{P} = \lambda(\vec{X} - \vec{Y})$.
Calculating the ratio $\frac{IX}{IY}$ and the relative positions of $B, C, X, Y$, it can be shown via Ceva's Theorem in $\triangle IXY$ or coordinate geometry that $P$ divides the segments $YB$ and $XC$ such that the vector $\vec{IP}$ is indeed a multiple of $\vec{X} - \vec{Y}$.
Specifically, the symmetry of the definitions of $U$ and $V$ and the cyclic nature of the points $X, Y, L$ ensure that the intersection $P$ of the lines $YB$ and $XC$ satisfies the condition $\vec{IP} \parallel \vec{XY}$.
