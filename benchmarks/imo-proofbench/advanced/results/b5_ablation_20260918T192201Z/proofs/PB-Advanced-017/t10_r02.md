To find the smallest possible value of $c$ such that for a positive integer $n \equiv 6 \pmod{7}$ and any divisor $d$ of $n$, the remainder of $(d + \frac{n}{d})^2$ when divided by $n$ is $n-c$, we start by analyzing the expression.

Let $E = (d + \frac{n}{d})^2$. Expanding this, we have:
\[ E = d^2 + 2d\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) + \left(\frac{n}{d}\right)^2 = d^2 + 2n + \left(\frac{n}{d}\right)^2 \]
The remainder when $E$ is divided by $n$ is the same as the remainder when $d^2 + (\frac{n}{d})^2$ is divided by $n$. Let this remainder be $r$. We are given that $r = n-c$, so:
\[ d^2 + \left(\frac{n}{d}\right)^2 \equiv n-c \equiv -c \pmod{n} \]
This can be rewritten as $d^2 + (\frac{n}{d})^2 + c = kn$ for some integer $k$. To minimize $c$, we want the expression $d^2 + (\frac{n}{d})^2$ to be as close to a multiple of $n$ as possible from below.
Let $d = gx$ and $\frac{n}{d} = gy$, where $\gcd(x, y) = 1$. Then $n = g^2 xy$. Substituting these into the equation:
\[ g^2 x^2 + g^2 y^2 + c = k(g^2 xy) \implies c = g^2(kxy - x^2 - y^2) \]
This implies that $g^2$ must be a divisor of $c$. Since we want to minimize $c$, and $c$ must be a positive integer (as $r \leq n-1$), the most promising case is $g=1$, which means $d$ and $\frac{n}{d}$ are coprime. Thus $n = xy$ with $\gcd(x, y) = 1$.
The condition becomes $x^2 + y^2 + c = kxy$ for some integer $k$, with $xy \equiv 6 \pmod{7}$.

1.  **Case $c=1$**: $x^2 + y^2 + 1 = kxy$. It is a known result from the theory of Markov-like equations that $x^2 + y^2 + 1 = kxy$ only has positive integer solutions if $k=3$. For $k=3$, the solutions $(x, y)$ are $(1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 5), (5, 13), \dots$ The products $xy$ modulo 7 for these pairs are $1, 2, 3, 2, 1, \dots$ none of which are $6 \pmod{7}$. Thus $c=1$ is impossible.
2.  **Case $c=2$**: $x^2 + y^2 + 2 = kxy$. If $x=y$, then $2x^2 + 2 = kx^2 \implies (k-2)x^2 = 2 \implies x=1, k=4$. For $k=4$, the solutions $(x, y)$ are generated by $y_{i+1} = 4y_i - y_{i-1}$. The sequence $y_n \pmod{7}$ is $1, 1, 3, 11 \equiv 4, 13 \equiv 6, 20 \equiv 6, \dots$ The products $xy \pmod{7}$ are $1 \times 1 = 1$, $1 \times 3 = 3$, $3 \times 4 = 12 \equiv 5$, $4 \times 6 = 24 \equiv 3$, $6 \times 6 = 36 \equiv 1$, $6 \times 4 = 24 \equiv 3$, etc. None of these products are $6 \pmod{7}$. Thus $c=2$ is impossible.
3.  **Case $c=3$**: $x^2 + y^2 + 3 = kxy$. Testing small values, let $k=5$. For $y=4$, $x^2 - 20x + 19 = 0$, giving $x=19$ or $x=1$. For $x=19, y=4$, we have $n = xy = 19 \times 4 = 76$.
    Check $n \pmod{7}$: $76 = 7 \times 10 + 6 \equiv 6 \pmod{7}$.
    Check the remainder: $(4 + \frac{76}{4})^2 = (4 + 19)^2 = 23^2 = 529$.
    Dividing 529 by 76: $529 = 6 \times 76 + 73$.
    The remainder is $r = 73$. Since $n-c = 76-3 = 73$, we have $c=3$.

Since $c=1$ and $c=2$ are impossible, the smallest possible value is $c=3$.

The smallest possible value of $c$ is 3. \(\square\)
