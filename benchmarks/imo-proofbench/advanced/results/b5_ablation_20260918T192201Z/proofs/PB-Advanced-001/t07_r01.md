To prove that there are infinitely many positive integers $n$ such that $A_n$ divides $n + 2024$, we analyze the growth and distribution of perfect powers.

**1. Definitions and Initial Estimates**
A perfect power is an integer of the form $a^b$ where $a \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $b \in \mathbb{Z}, b \ge 2$. Let $p_1, p_2, p_3, \dots$ be the sequence of perfect powers in increasing order. By definition, $A_n = k$ if and only if $p_k \le n < p_{k+1}$. This means that for any fixed $k$, the value of $A_n$ is constant for all $n$ in the interval $[p_k, p_{k+1}-1]$.

The number of perfect powers up to $x$ is dominated by the number of squares. Specifically,
\[ A_x = \lfloor \sqrt{x} \rfloor + A_x^{(3)}, \]
where $A_x^{(3)}$ is the number of perfect powers $\le x$ that are not squares. A perfect power $a^b$ is not a square if and only if $b$ is odd. Thus,
\[ A_x^{(3)} = \sum_{b=3, b \text{ odd}}^{\lfloor \log_2 x \rfloor} |\{ a \in \mathbb{Z}^+ : a^b \le x \text{ and } a^b \text{ is not a square} \}| \le \sum_{b=3}^{\lfloor \log_2 x \rfloor} x^{1/b}. \]
The sum is dominated by the first term $x^{1/3}$, so $A_x^{(3)} = O(x^{1/3} \log x)$.

**2. Analysis of the Interval $[p_k, p_{k+1}-1]$**
For a fixed $k$, we want to find $n \in [p_k, p_{k+1}-1]$ such that $n \equiv -2024 \pmod{k}$. Such an $n$ exists if the length of the interval, $L_k = p_{k+1} - p_k$, is at least $k$.

Let $m$ be a large integer and consider $p_k = m^2$. Then $k = A_{m^2} = m + A_{m^2}^{(3)}$.
The next perfect power $p_{k+1}$ is at least the next square $(m+1)^2$, unless there is a perfect power $a^b$ with $b \ge 3$ in the interval $(m^2, (m+1)^2)$.
If no such perfect power exists, then $p_{k+1} = (m+1)^2$, and the length of the interval is:
\[ L_k = (m+1)^2 - m^2 = 2m + 1. \]
We require $L_k \ge k$, which means $2m + 1 \ge m + A_{m^2}^{(3)}$, or $m + 1 \ge A_{m^2}^{(3)}$.
Since $A_{m^2}^{(3)} = O(m^{2/3} \log m)$, this inequality holds for all sufficiently large $m$.

**3. Existence of Infinitely Many Such $m$**
We must show that there are infinitely many $m$ such that the interval $(m^2, (m+1)^2)$ contains no perfect power $a^b$ with $b \ge 3$.
Let $S^{(3)} = \{ a^b : a \in \mathbb{Z}^+, b \ge 3 \}$. The number of elements in $S^{(3)}$ up to $X$ is $N^{(3)}(X) = O(X^{1/3})$.
For $m \in \{1, 2, \dots, M\}$, the number of intervals $(m^2, (m+1)^2)$ that contain at least one element of $S^{(3)}$ is at most the total number of such elements up to $(M+1)^2$, which is $O((M+1)^{2/3})$.
As $M \to \infty$, the ratio $O(M^{2/3})/M$ tends to 0. Thus, for almost all $m$, the interval $(m^2, (m+1)^2)$ contains no perfect powers other than squares.

**4. Conclusion**
For any such $m$ (where $m$ is sufficiently large), we have $p_k = m^2$, $p_{k+1} = (m+1)^2$, and $L_k = 2m+1 \ge k$. The interval $[m^2, (m+1)^2-1]$ therefore contains at least one integer $n$ such that $n \equiv -2024 \pmod{k}$. For this $n$, $A_n = k$, and $k$ divides $n + 2024$. Since there are infinitely many such $m$, and the intervals $[m^2, (m+1)^2-1]$ are disjoint, there are infinitely many such $n$.

$\square$
