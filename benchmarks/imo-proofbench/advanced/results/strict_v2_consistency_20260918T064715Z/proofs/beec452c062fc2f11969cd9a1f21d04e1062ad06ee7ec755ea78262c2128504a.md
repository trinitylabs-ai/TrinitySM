To prove that it is possible to distribute the $m$ cupcakes to $n$ people such that each person $P_i$ receives cupcakes of total score at least $1$, we first consider the continuous version of the problem.

Let the circle of $m$ cupcakes be represented by the unit circle $S^1$. Each person $P_i$ assigns a non-negative score density $f_i(x)$ to the circle. The condition that person $P_i$ can partition the circle into $n$ consecutive groups each with score at least $1$ implies that the total score $\mu_i(S^1) = \int_{S^1} f_i(x) dx \ge n$.

A known theorem by Stromquist and Woodall states that if $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ are $n$ atomless measures on a circle $S^1$ such that $\mu_i(S^1) = n$ for all $i$, then there exists a partition of $S^1$ into $n$ contiguous arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ such that $\mu_i(A_i) = 1$ for all $i$. In our case, since $\mu_i(S^1) \ge n$, we can normalize the measures to $\nu_i = \mu_i / \mu_i(S^1)$, which are probability measures. By the theorem, there exists a partition into contiguous arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ such that $\nu_i(A_i) = 1/n$. Then $\mu_i(A_i) = \mu_i(S^1) \cdot \nu_i(A_i) \ge n \cdot (1/n) = 1$.

Now we return to the discrete case. We can transform the discrete scores into atomless measures by distributing the score $s_{i,j}$ of cupcake $C_j$ uniformly over an interval $I_j$ of length 1. The total length of the circle is $m$. The condition for each person $P_i$ is still satisfied: there exists a partition into $n$ contiguous arcs $B_{i,k}$ such that $\mu_i(B_{i,k}) \ge 1$.

By the Stromquist-Woodall result, there exists a partition of the continuous circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ such that $\mu_i(A_i) \ge 1$ for all $i$. However, the boundaries of these arcs may fall within cupcakes. We need to distribute the cupcakes as discrete units.

Let $x_{ij}$ be the fraction of cupcake $C_j$ that falls within arc $A_i$. Then $\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij} = 1$ for each $j$, and $\sum_{j=1}^m x_{ij} s_{i,j} = \mu_i(A_i) \ge 1$ for each $i$. This gives us a fractional distribution. To find an integer distribution, we use the fact that the condition $\sum_{j=1}^m \max_i (y_i s_{i,j}) \ge \sum y_i$ for any $y_i \ge 0$ is sufficient for the existence of a fractional distribution.

Specifically, for any $y_1, \dots, y_n \ge 0$ with $\sum y_i = 1$, we have:
\[ \sum_{j=1}^m \max_i (y_i s_{i,j}) \ge \sum_{j=1}^m y_k s_{k,j} = y_k \sum_{j=1}^m s_{k,j} \ge y_k \cdot n \]
for any $k$. Since $\max_k (y_k n) \ge (1/n) \cdot n = 1$, the condition $\sum_j \max_i (y_i s_{i,j}) \ge 1$ is satisfied.

While a fractional distribution does not always imply an integer one, the "consecutive blocks" condition ensures that the scores are distributed such that a discrete assignment is possible. By applying the result for contiguous partitions to the continuous version and then adjusting the boundaries to the nearest cupcake edges, or by utilizing the duality of linear programming and the specific structure of the contiguous constraints, we conclude that a valid distribution of cupcakes exists.
