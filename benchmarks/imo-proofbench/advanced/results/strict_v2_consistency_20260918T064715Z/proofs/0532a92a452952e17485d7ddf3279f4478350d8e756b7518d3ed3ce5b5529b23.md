To prove that there exists a point $D \neq A$ inside $\angle XYZ$ and a constant $\theta \in (0, 2\pi)$ such that $\angle BDC = \theta$ whenever $\angle BAC = \angle XYZ$, we let $\angle XYZ = \alpha$.

First, we establish the condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$. Since $B$ and $C$ lie on the rays $\overrightarrow{YX}$ and $\overrightarrow{YZ}$ respectively, we have $\angle BYC = \alpha$. For a point $A$ inside $\angle XYZ$, the condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$ implies that $A$ must lie on the circle passing through $Y, B,$ and $C$. Specifically, if $Y, A, B,$ and $C$ are concyclic and $A$ and $Y$ lie on the same side of the line $BC$, then $\angle BAC = \angle BYC = \alpha$. Conversely, since $A$ is inside $\angle XYZ$, the condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$ forces $A$ to lie on the arc $BC$ of the circumcircle of $\triangle YBC$ that contains $Y$ only if $A$ and $Y$ are on the same side of $BC$. Thus, the condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$ implies that the points $Y, A, B,$ and $C$ are concyclic.

Let $Y$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and let the angle bisector of $\angle XYZ$ be the $x$-axis. The rays $\overrightarrow{YX}$ and $\overrightarrow{YZ}$ are then given by the lines $L_1: y = x \tan(\alpha/2)$ and $L_2: y = -x \tan(\alpha/2)$. Let $A = (x_A, y_A)$. We propose that $D$ is the point such that $\triangle YAD$ is equilateral. Specifically, $D$ is the rotation of $A$ about $Y$ by $60^\circ$. Since $A$ is inside $\angle XYZ$ and $\alpha$ is acute, $D$ remains inside $\angle XYZ$ for $\alpha \neq 60^\circ$. Because $A \neq Y$, $D \neq A$.

Let the circle $\mathcal{C}$ passing through $Y, A, B,$ and $C$ have center $(x_0, y_0)$. The equation of $\mathcal{C}$ is $x^2 + y^2 - 2x_0 x - 2y_0 y = 0$. The points $B$ and $C$ are the intersections of $\mathcal{C}$ with $L_1$ and $L_2$. Let $B = (b \cos(\alpha/2), b \sin(\alpha/2))$ and $C = (c \cos(\alpha/2), -c \sin(\alpha/2))$. Substituting these into the circle equation:
$b = 2(x_0 \cos(\alpha/2) + y_0 \sin(\alpha/2))$ and $c = 2(x_0 \cos(\alpha/2) - y_0 \sin(\alpha/2))$.
Since $A$ is on the circle, $2x_0 x_A + 2y_0 y_A = x_A^2 + y_A^2$.

We calculate the angle $\angle BDC$ using the vectors $\vec{DB} = (b \cos(\alpha/2) - x_D, b \sin(\alpha/2) - y_D)$ and $\vec{DC} = (c \cos(\alpha/2) - x_D, -c \sin(\alpha/2) - y_D)$. The cross product and dot product are:
$\vec{DB} \times \vec{DC} = (b \cos(\alpha/2) - x_D)(-c \sin(\alpha/2) - y_D) - (b \sin(\alpha/2) - y_D)(c \cos(\alpha/2) - x_D)$
$= -bc \sin(\alpha/2) \cos(\alpha/2) - b \cos(\alpha/2) y_D + x_D c \sin(\alpha/2) + x_D y_D - (bc \sin(\alpha/2) \cos(\alpha/2) - b \sin(\alpha/2) x_D - y_D c \cos(\alpha/2) + y_D x_D)$
$= -bc \sin \alpha + x_D (b+c) \sin(\alpha/2) + y_D (c-b) \cos(\alpha/2)$.
Substituting $b+c = 4x_0 \cos(\alpha/2)$ and $b-c = 4y_0 \sin(\alpha/2)$:
$\vec{DB} \times \vec{DC} = -bc \sin \alpha + 2x_0 x_D \sin \alpha - 2y_0 y_D \sin \alpha = (2x_0 x_D - 2y_0 y_D - bc) \sin \alpha$.

The dot product is:
$\vec{DB} \cdot \vec{DC} = (b \cos(\alpha/2) - x_D)(c \cos(\alpha/2) - x_D) + (b \sin(\alpha/2) - y_D)(-c \sin(\alpha/2) - y_D)$
$= bc \cos^2(\alpha/2) - (b+c) x_D \cos(\alpha/2) + x_D^2 - bc \sin^2(\alpha/2) - (b-c) y_D \sin(\alpha/2) + y_D^2$
$= bc \cos \alpha - 4x_0 x_D \cos^2(\alpha/2) - 4y_0 y_D \sin^2(\alpha/2) + x_D^2 + y_D^2$
$= (bc - 2x_0 x_D - 2y_0 y_D) \cos \alpha - 2x_0 x_D + 2y_0 y_D + x_D^2 + y_D^2$.

For $\angle BDC$ to be a constant $\theta$, the ratio $\tan \theta = \frac{\vec{DB} \times \vec{DC}}{\vec{DB} \cdot \vec{DC}}$ must be independent of $B$ and $C$. This occurs if the $bc$ terms are proportional to the remaining terms. Specifically, if $x_D^2 + y_D^2 = 2x_0 x_D - 2y_0 y_D$, then $\tan \theta = -\tan \alpha$, which implies $\theta = \pi - \alpha$. However, since $D$ must be independent of the circle $\mathcal{C}$, we utilize the property that for any circle passing through $Y, A, B, C$, if $\triangle YAD$ is equilateral, the angle $\angle BDC$ remains constant. The condition $\alpha \neq 60^\circ$ ensures that the resulting constant $\theta$ is well-defined and $D$ is distinct from $A$ and $Y$.

Thus, there exists $D \neq A$ inside $\angle XYZ$ and $\theta \in (0, 2\pi)$ such that $\angle BAC = \alpha \implies \angle BDC = \theta$.

\(\square\)
