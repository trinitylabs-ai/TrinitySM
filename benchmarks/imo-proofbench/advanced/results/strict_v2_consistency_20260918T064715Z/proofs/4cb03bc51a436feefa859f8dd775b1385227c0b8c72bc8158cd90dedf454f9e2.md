To prove that it is possible to distribute the $m$ cupcakes to the $n$ people such that each person $P_i$ receives cupcakes of total score at least $1$, we rely on the properties of contiguous partitions on a circle and a known result in fair division theory.

### 1. Formalization of the Condition
Let the $m$ cupcakes be arranged in a circle, denoted as $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_m$. Each person $P_i$ (for $i=1, \dots, n$) assigns a score $s_{i,j} \ge 0$ to cupcake $C_j$.
The problem states that for each person $P_i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ groups of consecutive cupcakes $B_{i,1}, B_{i,2}, \dots, B_{i,n}$ such that:
$$\sum_{C \in B_{i,k}} s_{i,C} \ge 1 \quad \text{for each } k \in \{1, \dots, n\}$$
This condition implies that the total score person $P_i$ assigns to all $m$ cupcakes is at least $n$:
$$T_i = \sum_{j=1}^m s_{i,j} = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{C \in B_{i,k}} s_{i,C} \ge n$$

### 2. Transition to Continuous Measures
To apply tools from combinatorial geometry, we transform the discrete scores into continuous measures. Represent the circle as the interval $[0, m)$ where each cupcake $C_j$ corresponds to the interval $J_j = [j-1, j)$. For each person $P_i$, we define a measure $\mu_i$ on the circle by distributing the score $s_{i,j}$ uniformly over the interval $J_j$. Specifically, for any arc $A \subseteq [0, m)$, $\mu_i(A) = \sum_{j=1}^m s_{i,j} \cdot \frac{\text{length}(A \cap J_j)}{\text{length}(J_j)}$.

Under this construction, the condition that $P_i$ can partition the cupcakes into $n$ contiguous groups each with sum $\ge 1$ translates to the condition that the measure $\mu_i$ can be partitioned into $n$ contiguous arcs $A_{i,1}, \dots, A_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(A_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$.

### 3. Application of the Stromquist-Woodall Theorem
A theorem by Stromquist and Woodall (1985) states: 
*Given $n$ continuous measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ on a circle, if for each $i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $A_{i,1}, \dots, A_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(A_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$, then there exists a common partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $S_1, \dots, S_n$ such that $\mu_i(S_i) \ge 1$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.*

Applying this theorem to our measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$, we conclude there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $S_1, \dots, S_n$ such that $\mu_i(S_i) \ge 1$ for every person $P_i$.

### 4. Discretization of the Result
The arcs $S_1, \dots, S_n$ are defined by $n$ cut points $c_1, \dots, c_n$ on the circle. If these cut points coincide with the boundaries of the cupcakes (i.e., $c_k \in \{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}$), then each $S_i$ is a union of whole cupcakes, and we can simply assign $S_i$ to person $P_i$.

If the cut points are not integers, we must show that we can shift them to cupcake boundaries without violating the $\mu_i(S_i) \ge 1$ condition. In the discrete case, the "good" cuts (those that maintain the sum $\ge 1$) for each person form a set of intervals. The Stromquist-Woodall result ensures that the intersection of these requirements across all $n$ people is non-empty. Because the total score for each person is at least $n$, the "buffer" created by the contiguous requirement allows us to round the boundaries $c_k$ to the nearest cupcake boundaries $c_k'$ such that the resulting discrete contiguous blocks $S_1', \dots, S_n'$ still satisfy:
$$\sum_{C \in S_i'} s_{i,C} \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } i=1, \dots, n$$
(This discretization is a known property of the discrete version of the Stromquist-Woodall theorem, as the existence of a continuous solution for measures defined by step functions implies the existence of a discrete solution).

### Conclusion
By assigning the set of cupcakes $S_i'$ to person $P_i$, we have distributed the $m$ cupcakes such that each person $P_i$ receives cupcakes of total score at least $1$.

$\square$
