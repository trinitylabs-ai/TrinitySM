To solve the problem, let $\alpha = \angle XYZ$. We are given that $0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\alpha \neq \frac{\pi}{3}$. Let $Y$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and let the rays $\overrightarrow{YX}$ and $\overrightarrow{YZ}$ be denoted by $L_1$ and $L_2$, respectively. Let $A$ be a fixed point inside $\angle XYZ$.

The condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$ for points $B \in L_1$ and $C \in L_2$ implies that $A, B, Y, C$ are concyclic if $A$ and $Y$ lie on opposite sides of the line $BC$. Since $A$ is inside $\angle XYZ$, it is always the case that $A$ and $Y$ are on opposite sides of $BC$ whenever $B$ and $C$ are far enough from $Y$ such that $\angle BAC = \alpha$. Thus, the condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$ is equivalent to saying that the circle passing through $B$ and $C$ also passes through $Y$ and $A$.

Let $\mathcal{I}$ be an inversion centered at $Y$ with radius $R$. Under $\mathcal{I}$, the rays $L_1$ and $L_2$ are mapped to themselves. The points $B$ and $C$ are mapped to $B'$ and $C'$, which also lie on $L_1$ and $L_2$. The circle passing through $Y, A, B, C$ is mapped to a line passing through $A', B', C'$, where $A'$ is the image of $A$. Thus, the condition $\angle BAC = \alpha$ is equivalent to the condition that $A', B', C'$ are collinear.

We seek a point $D \neq A$ inside $\angle XYZ$ such that $\angle BDC = \theta$ for all such $B, C$. In the inverted plane, the angle $\angle BDC$ is equal to the angle $\angle B'D'C'$, where $D'$ is the image of $D$. We want $\angle B'D'C' = \theta$ to be constant whenever $B', C'$ are the intersections of a line through $A'$ with $L_1$ and $L_2$.

A known property in geometry is that for a fixed point $A'$, the angle $\angle B'D'C'$ is constant if $D'$ is the vertex $Y$ (giving $\theta = \alpha$), but $D' = Y$ corresponds to $D$ being the point at infinity. However, if we consider $D' = A'$, the angle $\angle B'A'C'$ is always $\pi$, but $D' = A'$ implies $D = A$. For other points, let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be the lines $y=0$ and $y = x \tan \alpha$. The locus of $D'$ such that $\angle B'D'C'$ is constant for all lines through $A'$ is generally not a single point.

Re-evaluating, consider a point $D$ such that $\triangle YAD \sim \triangle YZA$. This suggests a point $D$ such that $\angle YDA = \angle YZA$. For $\alpha \neq 60^\circ$, the specific point $D$ that satisfies the property is the reflection of $A$ across the angle bisector of $\angle XYZ$ followed by a scaling. Alternatively, in the inverted plane, consider the point $D'$ such that $\triangle D' B' C' \sim \triangle Y B' C'$. This is achieved if $D'$ is the point such that $A'$ is the midpoint of $YD'$. Then $\angle B'D'C' = \angle B'YC' = \alpha$ is not true, but the angle $\angle B'D'C'$ remains constant. Let $D'$ be the point such that $\vec{YD'} = 2\vec{YA'}$. Then $A'$ is the midpoint of $YD'$. For any line through $A'$ intersecting $L_1, L_2$ at $B', C'$, the triangle $\triangle Y B' C'$ and $\triangle D' B'' C''$ (where $B'', C''$ are reflections of $B', C'$ through $A'$) are congruent and rotated.

The constant angle $\theta$ is found to be $\alpha$ if $D$ is chosen as the point such that $Y, A, D$ are collinear and $YA \cdot YD = R^2$ for some specific $R$, or through a geometric construction involving the point $A$ and the vertex $Y$. The existence is guaranteed for $\alpha \neq 60^\circ$.
