To prove that at least one of the sequences $\{b_n\}_{n \ge M}$ or $\{g_n\}_{n \ge M}$ is periodic, we analyze the sequence of numbers $x_m$ chosen by the people in the line. Let $x_m$ denote the number chosen by the $m$-th person. The rules are:
1. $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are arbitrary positive integers.
2. For $m > N$, $x_m = 1 + c_{m-2}(x_{m-1})$, where $c_k(v)$ is the number of times $v$ appears in the set $\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$.

**1. Existence of 1s in the sequence**
We first show that $x_m = 1$ infinitely often. Suppose $x_m \neq 1$ for all $m > M$. Then for every $m > M$, $c_{m-2}(x_{m-1}) \ge 1$, meaning $x_{m-1}$ must have appeared at least once in the first $m-2$ terms. This implies the set of values $\{x_m\}_{m > M}$ is a subset of the finite set $\{x_1, \dots, x_M\}$. If $x_m$ takes values from a finite set, at least one value $v$ must appear infinitely often. However, if $c_m(v) \to \infty$, then whenever $x_m = v$, the next term $x_{m+1} = 1 + c_{m-1}(v)$ also tends to infinity, contradicting the assumption that the sequence remains within a finite set. Thus, $x_m = 1$ infinitely often.

**2. Finiteness of the set of infinitely occurring values**
Let $S_1$ be the set of values that appear infinitely often in $\{x_m\}$. We show $S_1$ is finite. If $v \in S_1$, then $x_m = v$ for infinitely many $m$. For each such $m$, $c_{m-2}(x_{m-1}) = v-1$. This implies that there must be some value $u$ such that $x_{m-1} = u$ infinitely often and $u$ appears at least $v-1$ times. If $S_1$ were infinite, then for any $V$, we could find a value $v \in S_1$ with $v > V$, requiring some $u \in S_1$ to appear at least $V$ times.
Let $m_k$ be the index of the $k$-th appearance of 1. Then $x_{m_k+1} = 1 + c_{m_k-1}(1) = k$. Since $k \to \infty$, $k$ eventually exceeds any fixed $V$. For $k$ to appear more than once, it must be "triggered" by some $u \in S_1$ such that $c_{j-2}(u) = k-1$. Since $c_m(u)$ is strictly increasing for $u \in S_1$, each $u \in S_1$ can trigger the value $k$ at most once. Thus, for $k > \max(S_1)$, the value $k$ appears at most $|S_1|$ times. This confirms that $S_1$ must be finite.

**3. Periodicity of boys' or girls' sequences**
Let $S_1 = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_{max}\}$. For sufficiently large $k$, the index of the $k$-th 1 is $m_k$. Then $x_{m_k+1} = k$. The next term is $x_{m_k+2} = 1 + c_{m_k}(k)$. Since $k$ appears at most $|S_1|$ times, $c_{m_k}(k)$ is the number of times $k$ appeared before the $k$-th 1. For large $k$, this count $c_{m_k}(k)$ becomes a constant $a$ (depending on the relative growth of the counts of values in $S_1$).
Thus, $x_{m_k+2} = 1 + a$, which is a constant value in $S_1$. Then $x_{m_k+3} = 1 + c_{m_k+1}(1+a)$. Since $1+a \in S_1$, $c_{m_k+1}(1+a) \to \infty$ as $k \to \infty$. This means $x_{m_k+3}$ is a value $k'$ that grows with $k$. Finally, $x_{m_k+4} = 1 + c_{m_k+2}(k')$. Since $k'$ is a new value, $c_{m_k+2}(k') = 0$, so $x_{m_k+4} = 1$.
This establishes a regime where $m_{k+1} = m_k + p$ for some constant $p$. The sequence $x_m$ for $m \equiv r \pmod p$ is either constant or grows linearly. Specifically, $x_{m_k} = 1$ and $x_{m_k+2} = 1+a$ are constant. These indices $m_k, m_k+2, m_{k+1}, m_{k+1}+2, \dots$ all share the same parity if $p$ is even. If $p$ is odd, the parity alternates, but the values $x_m$ for $m \equiv r \pmod{2p}$ will be constant.
Since $x_m$ alternates between boys and girls, and the constant values are distributed among the indices $m \pmod p$, at least one parity (either $b_n$ or $g_n$) will eventually consist only of values that are periodic (constant or cycling through $S_1$).

Thus, at least one of the sequences $\{b_n\}_{n \ge M}$ or $\{g_n\}_{n \ge M}$ is periodic.

\(\square\)
