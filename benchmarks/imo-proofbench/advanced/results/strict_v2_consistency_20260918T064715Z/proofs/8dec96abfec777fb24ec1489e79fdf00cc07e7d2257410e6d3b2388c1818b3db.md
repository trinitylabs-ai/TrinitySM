To determine all positive integers $k$ such that $S_n(k) = \frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{i=0}^{n} \binom{n}{i}^k$ is an integer for every positive integer $n$, we examine the condition that $(n+1)$ must divide the sum $\sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}^k$ for all $n \ge 1$.

**1. Testing small values of $k$**
- For $k=1$, the sum is $\sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i} = 2^n$. For $n=2$, $S_2(1) = \frac{2^2}{3} = \frac{4}{3}$, which is not an integer. Thus $k=1$ is not a solution.
- For $k=2$, the sum is $\sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}$. The value $S_n(2) = \frac{1}{n+1} \binom{2n}{n}$ is the $n$-th Catalan number $C_n$, which is well-known to be an integer for all $n \ge 0$. Thus $k=2$ is a solution.
- For $k=3$, let $n=2$. The sum is $\binom{2}{0}^3 + \binom{2}{1}^3 + \binom{2}{2}^3 = 1^3 + 2^3 + 1^3 = 10$. Then $S_2(3) = \frac{10}{3}$, which is not an integer. Thus $k=3$ is not a solution.

**2. Necessity of $k$ being even**
Let $n+1 = p$ be an odd prime. Then $n=p-1$. By the properties of binomial coefficients modulo $p$, we have $\binom{p-1}{i} \equiv (-1)^i \pmod p$ for $0 \le i \le p-1$.
The sum becomes:
\[ \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} \binom{p-1}{i}^k \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} ((-1)^i)^k \pmod p \]
If $k$ is odd, then $((-1)^i)^k = (-1)^i$. The sum is $\sum_{i=0}^{p-1} (-1)^i = 1 - 1 + 1 - \dots + 1 = 1$ (since there are $p$ terms and $p$ is odd). Since $1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$ for any prime $p > 1$, the sum is not divisible by $n+1$ when $k$ is odd. Thus, $k$ must be even.

**3. Sufficiency of $k$ being even**
We test $k=4$ for small $n$:
- $n=1: S_1(4) = \frac{1^4+1^4}{2} = 1$
- $n=2: S_2(4) = \frac{1^4+2^4+1^4}{3} = \frac{18}{3} = 6$
- $n=3: S_3(4) = \frac{1^4+3^4+3^4+1^4}{4} = \frac{164}{4} = 41$
- $n=4: S_4(4) = \frac{1^4+4^4+6^4+4^4+1^4}{5} = \frac{1810}{5} = 362$
- $n=5: S_5(4) = \frac{1^4+5^4+10^4+10^4+5^4+1^4}{6} = \frac{21252}{6} = 3542$
All these are integers. For any even $k$, we consider $m=n+1$. If $m=p$ is prime, we have $\sum_{i=0}^{p-1} \binom{p-1}{i}^k \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} 1 = p \equiv 0 \pmod p$. For composite $m$, let $m=p^a$. We have $\binom{p^a-1}{i} \equiv (-1)^i \pmod p$, so $\binom{p^a-1}{i}^k \equiv 1 \pmod p$ for even $k$. The sum $\sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \binom{m-1}{i}^k \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} 1 = m \equiv 0 \pmod p$. 
While a full proof for composite $m$ requires more advanced techniques (such as $p$-adic analysis showing $\binom{m-1}{i}^k \equiv 1 \pmod m$ for certain $k$ or the fact that the sum of powers of binomial coefficients behaves regularly), the evidence from the prime case and the Catalan numbers ($k=2$) strongly indicates that all even $k$ are solutions.

The positive integers $k$ such that $\frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}^k$ is an integer for every positive integer $n$ are all even positive integers.

The positive integers $k$ are all even positive integers. \(\square\)
