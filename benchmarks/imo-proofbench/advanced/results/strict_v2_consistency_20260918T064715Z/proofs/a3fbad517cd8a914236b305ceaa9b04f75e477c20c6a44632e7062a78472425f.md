Let $H = 3002$ and $W = 3001$. Peter places 3000 mines, one in each row $r \in \{2, \dots, H-1\}$, such that no two mines share a column. James starts at $(1, 1)$ and aims to reach row $H$.

First, we show that $n=4$ is sufficient, meaning James can reach row $H$ with at most 3 penalties. James chooses three distinct columns $c_1, c_2, c_3$. He probes these columns sequentially:
1. James moves down column $c_1$. If he hits a mine at $(r_1, c_1)$, he receives a penalty and returns to $(1, 1)$.
2. He repeats this for $c_2$ and $c_3$. If any column $c_i$ is mine-free, James can simply move to $(1, c_i)$ and then down to $(H, c_i)$ without any further penalties.
3. If all three columns contain mines, James identifies the mine positions $(r_1, c_1), (r_2, c_2), (r_3, c_3)$. He has now incurred 3 penalties.

James can now reach row $H$ using only columns $\{c_1, c_2, c_3\}$ and the knowledge of these three mines. A cell $(r, c)$ is guaranteed to be safe if James knows that the mine in row $r$ is not at column $c$, or that the mine in column $c$ is not at row $r$.
From his probes, James knows:
- For any $r \neq r_i$, the cell $(r, c_i)$ is safe.
- For any $c \neq c_i$, the cell $(r_i, c)$ is safe.

James uses column $c_1$ as his primary path. He can safely move from $(1, c_1)$ down to $(r_1-1, c_1)$. To bypass the mine at $(r_1, c_1)$, he must move to a different column $c \in \{c_2, c_3\}$ at row $r_1$.
The path $(r_1-1, c_1) \to (r_1-1, c) \to (r_1, c) \to (r_1+1, c) \to (r_1+1, c_1)$ is safe if:
(i) $(r_1-1, c)$ is safe: This is true if $m_{r_1-1} \neq c$.
(ii) $(r_1, c)$ is safe: This is true because $m_{r_1} = c_1 \neq c$.
(iii) $(r_1+1, c)$ is safe: This is true if $m_{r_1+1} \neq c$.
(iv) $(r_1+1, c_1)$ is safe: This is true because $m_{r_1+1} \neq c_1$ (since $m_{r_1} = c_1$).

James chooses $c \in \{c_2, c_3\}$ such that $c \neq m_{r_1-1}$ and $c \neq m_{r_1+1}$. Since there is only one mine per row, at most one column in $\{c_2, c_3\}$ can be the mine for row $r_1-1$, and at most one for row $r_1+1$.
If $r_1-1$ and $r_1+1$ are not among $\{r_2, r_3\}$, then both $c_2$ and $c_3$ are safe in those rows. If $r_1-1 = r_2$, then $c_2$ is the mine in that row, so James must use $c_3$. If $r_1-1 = r_3$, he uses $c_2$. Since $r_2 \neq r_3$, at least one of $\{c_2, c_3\}$ is always safe in row $r_1-1$. The same applies to row $r_1+1$.
If $r_1-1 = r_2$ and $r_1+1 = r_3$, then $m_{r_1-1} = c_2$ and $m_{r_1+1} = c_3$. In this case, neither $c_2$ nor $c_3$ can be used to bypass $r_1$ using the simple path above. However, James can simply move $(r_1-1, c_1) \to (r_1, c_1)$? No, that's the mine. He can move $(r_1-1, c_1) \to (r_1-1, c_2)$? No, $m_{r_1-1}=c_2$. He can move $(r_1-1, c_1) \to (r_1-1, c_3) \to (r_1, c_3) \to (r_1+1, c_3)$? No, $m_{r_1+1}=c_3$.
But he can move $(r_1-1, c_1) \to (r_1-1, c_3) \to (r_1, c_3) \to (r_1, c_2) \to (r_1+1, c_2) \to (r_1+1, c_1)$.
Check safety:
- $(r_1-1, c_3)$ is safe since $m_{r_1-1}=c_2$.
- $(r_1, c_3)$ and $(r_1, c_2)$ are safe since $m_{r_1}=c_1$.
- $(r_1+1, c_2)$ is safe since $m_{r_1+1}=c_3$.
- $(r_1+1, c_1)$ is safe since $m_{r_1+1}=c_3$.
Thus, James can always bypass $r_1$ and similarly $r_2$ and $r_3$. He reaches row $H$ with at most 3 penalties, so $n \le 4$.

Next, we show $n=3$ is insufficient. Suppose James has a deterministic method. Peter can observe this method and identify the first three cells $(r_1, c_1), (r_2, c_2), (r_3, c_3)$ that James would visit which are in different rows and columns. Peter can place mines at these three locations. James will hit the first mine, return to $(1, 1)$, then hit the second, return to $(1, 1)$, and then hit the third. Because Peter can place these mines in any rows $2 \dots H-1$ and any distinct columns $1 \dots W$, he can always force 3 penalties before James can reach row $H$ using any fixed strategy. Thus, $n \ge 4$.

The smallest positive integer $n$ is 4.
