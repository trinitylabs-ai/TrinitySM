To determine all positive integers $k$ such that $S_n(k) = \frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}^k$ is an integer for every positive integer $n$, we examine the condition that $n+1$ must divide the sum $T_n(k) = \sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}^k$ for all $n \ge 1$.

**1. Necessity of $k$ being even**
Let $n+1 = p$ be an odd prime. Then $n=p-1$. By the properties of binomial coefficients modulo $p$:
\[ \binom{p-1}{i} = \frac{(p-1)(p-2)\dots(p-i)}{i!} \equiv \frac{(-1)(-2)\dots(-i)}{i!} = (-1)^i \pmod p \]
The sum becomes:
\[ T_{p-1}(k) = \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} \binom{p-1}{i}^k \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{p-1} ((-1)^i)^k \pmod p \]
If $k$ is odd, then $((-1)^i)^k = (-1)^i$. The sum is $\sum_{i=0}^{p-1} (-1)^i = 1 - 1 + 1 - \dots + 1 = 1$ (since there are $p$ terms and $p$ is odd). Since $1 \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$ for any prime $p > 1$, $T_{p-1}(k)$ is not divisible by $n+1$ when $k$ is odd. Thus, $k$ must be even.

**2. Sufficiency of $k$ being even**
Let $k$ be an even positive integer. We show that $T_{m-1}(k) = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \binom{m-1}{i}^k$ is divisible by $m$ for all $m \ge 2$. It suffices to show $T_{m-1}(k) \equiv 0 \pmod{p^a}$ for every prime power $p^a$ dividing $m$.
Using the identity $\binom{m-1}{i} = \prod_{j=1}^i \frac{m-j}{j} = (-1)^i \prod_{j=1}^i (1 - \frac{m}{j})$, and since $k$ is even:
\[ \binom{m-1}{i}^k = \prod_{j=1}^i \left(1 - \frac{m}{j}\right)^k \]
We expand this as a power series in $m$ in the ring of $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$. For $i < p^a$, $v_p(j) < a$, so $\frac{m}{j} \in \mathbb{Z}_p$.
\[ \binom{m-1}{i}^k = 1 - km H_i + m^2 \left( \frac{k^2}{2} H_i^2 - \frac{k}{2} H_i^{(2)} \right) + \sum_{r=3}^\infty m^r C_{i,r} \]
where $H_i^{(s)} = \sum_{j=1}^i j^{-s}$ are generalized harmonic numbers. Summing over $i=0, \dots, m-1$:
\[ T_{m-1}(k) = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} 1 - km \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i + m^2 \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} \left( \frac{k^2}{2} H_i^2 - \frac{k}{2} H_i^{(2)} \right) + \sum_{r=3}^\infty m^r \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} C_{i,r} \]
We analyze the $p$-adic valuation of each term for $m=p^a$:
- **Constant Term**: $\sum_{i=0}^{m-1} 1 = m \equiv 0 \pmod{p^a}$.
- **Linear Term**: We use $\sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i = m H_{m-1} - (m-1)$. Thus:
  $-km(m H_{m-1} - m + 1) = -km^2 H_{m-1} + km(m-1)$.
  Since $v_p(H_{m-1}) \ge -(a-1)$, $v_p(m^2 H_{m-1}) \ge 2a - (a-1) = a+1$. Thus, this term is $0 \pmod{p^a}$.
- **Quadratic Term**: We use $\sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i^2 = m H_{m-1}^2 - (2m-1) H_{m-1} + 2(m-1)$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i^{(2)} = m H_{m-1}^{(2)} - H_{m-1}$.
  $v_p(m^2 \sum H_i^2) \ge \min(3a - 2(a-1), 2a - (a-1), 2a) = a+1$.
  $v_p(m^2 \sum H_i^{(2)}) \ge \min(3a - 2(a-1), 2a - (a-1)) = a+1$.
  Thus, the quadratic term is $0 \pmod{p^a}$.
- **Higher-Order Terms**: The terms $m^r \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} C_{i,r}$ are combinations of $m^r \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i^{s}$ for $s \le r$. We prove $v_p(m^r \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i^r) \ge a$ by induction on $r$.
  For $r=1$, $v_p(m \sum H_i) \ge a$ as shown above.
  Assume it holds for $r-1$. Using $\sum_{i=1}^{n} H_i^r = (n+1) H_n^r - \sum_{i=1}^n i (H_i^r - H_{i-1}^r)$, we have:
  $m^r \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} H_i^r = m^{r+1} H_{m-1}^r - \sum_{j=1}^r \binom{r}{j} m^r \sum_{i=1}^{m-1} \frac{H_{i-1}^{r-j}}{i^{j-1}}$.
  The term $j=1$ is $r m^r \sum H_{i-1}^{r-1}$, which is $0 \pmod{p^a}$ by the induction hypothesis.
  For $j \ge 2$, $v_p(m^r \frac{H_{i-1}^{r-j}}{i^{j-1}}) \ge ra - (r-j)(a-1) - (j-1)(a-1) = ra - (r-1)(a-1) = a+r-1 \ge a$.
  Thus, all higher-order terms are $0 \pmod{p^a}$.

Therefore, $T_{m-1}(k) \equiv 0 \pmod{p^a}$ for all prime powers $p^a$ dividing $m$, so $T_{m-1}(k) \equiv 0 \pmod m$.

The positive integers $k$ such that $\frac{1}{n+1} \sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}^k$ is an integer for every positive integer $n$ are all even positive integers.
