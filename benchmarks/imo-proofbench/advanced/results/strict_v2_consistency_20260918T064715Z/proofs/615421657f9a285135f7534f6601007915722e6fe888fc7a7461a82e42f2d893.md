To prove that $P(x)$ has a non-real root, we proceed by contradiction. Suppose $P(x)$ has only real roots $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$. Since $P(x)$ has no repeated roots, the $r_i$ are distinct. Since the constant term of $P(x)$ is nonzero, $P(0) \neq 0$, which implies $r_i \neq 0$ for all $i=1, \dots, n$.

Any polynomial $Q(x) = a_k x^k + \dots + a_0$ that divides $P(x)$ must have its roots as a subset $S \subset \{r_1, \dots, r_n\}$ of size $k$. The coefficients of $Q(x)$ are given by $a_j = a_k (-1)^{k-j} e_{k-j}(S)$, where $e_m(S)$ is the $m$-th elementary symmetric polynomial of the roots in $S$. The condition $a_0 a_1 \dots a_k = 0$ implies that for any $k$-subset $S$, there exists some $j \in \{0, 1, \dots, k\}$ such that $e_j(S) = 0$. Since $\deg Q = k$, $a_k \neq 0$, so $e_0(S) = 1 \neq 0$. Since $P(0) \neq 0$, $a_0 \neq 0$, so $e_k(S) \neq 0$. Thus, for every $k$-subset $S$, there exists $j \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $e_j(S) = 0$.

We first establish a lemma: a polynomial $f(x) = \sum_{i=0}^m a_i x^i$ with $m$ distinct real roots and $a_0 \neq 0$ cannot have two consecutive coefficients $a_j, a_{j+1}$ equal to zero for $0 \le j \le m-1$.
Proof: If $f(x)$ has $m$ distinct real roots, then by Rolle's Theorem, its $j$-th derivative $f^{(j)}(x)$ has $m-j$ distinct real roots. If $a_j = 0$ and $a_{j+1} = 0$, then $f^{(j)}(0) = j! a_j = 0$ and $f^{(j+1)}(0) = (j+1)! a_{j+1} = 0$. This implies that $x=0$ is a root of $f^{(j)}(x)$ with multiplicity at least 2, which contradicts the fact that $f^{(j)}(x)$ has $m-j$ distinct real roots.

Now, consider any $k+1$ roots from the set $\{r_1, \dots, r_n\}$. Let this subset be $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_{k+1}\}$. There are $k+1$ subsets of $X$ of size $k$, namely $S_i = X \setminus \{x_i\}$ for $i=1, \dots, k+1$. By the given condition, for each $i$, there exists $j_i \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $e_{j_i}(S_i) = 0$. Let $m_j$ be the number of indices $i$ such that $e_j(S_i) = 0$. Then $\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} m_j \ge k+1$.

Suppose $m_j \ge 2$ for some $j \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$. Then there exist distinct indices $i_1, i_2$ such that $e_j(S_{i_1}) = 0$ and $e_j(S_{i_2}) = 0$. Let $C = X \setminus \{x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}\}$. Then $C$ is a subset of size $k-1$. We can write:
$$e_j(S_{i_1}) = e_j(C) + x_{i_2} e_{j-1}(C) = 0$$
$$e_j(S_{i_2}) = e_j(C) + x_{i_1} e_{j-1}(C) = 0$$
Subtracting these equations gives $(x_{i_2} - x_{i_1}) e_{j-1}(C) = 0$. Since the roots are distinct, $x_{i_1} \neq x_{i_2}$, so $e_{j-1}(C) = 0$. Substituting this back into either equation gives $e_j(C) = 0$. Thus, the polynomial $Q_C(x) = \prod_{r \in C} (x-r)$ has $k-1$ distinct real roots and two consecutive coefficients $a_{k-1-j} = (-1)^j e_j(C)$ and $a_{k-j} = (-1)^{j-1} e_{j-1}(C)$ that are both zero. Since $e_0(C) = 1 \neq 0$ and $e_{k-1}(C) = \prod_{r \in C} r \neq 0$, this contradicts the lemma.

Therefore, we must have $m_j \le 1$ for all $j \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$. This implies:
$$\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} m_j \le k-1$$
However, we established that $\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} m_j \ge k+1$. This is a contradiction since $k-1 < k+1$.

Thus, the assumption that $P(x)$ has only real roots must be false. $P(x)$ must have at least one non-real root.
