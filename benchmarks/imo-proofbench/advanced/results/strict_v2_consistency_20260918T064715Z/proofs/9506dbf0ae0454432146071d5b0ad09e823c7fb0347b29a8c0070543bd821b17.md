Let $R = 3002$ and $C = 3001$. Peter selects a set $S = \{(r, c_r) : r = 2, 3, \dots, R-1\}$ of 3000 cells, one from each row $r \in \{2, \dots, 3001\}$, such that all $c_r$ are distinct. James starts at $(1, 1)$ and aims to reach row $R$. If he enters a cell in $S$, he receives a penalty and resets to $(1, 1)$.

**1. Upper Bound:**
James can guarantee reaching row $R$ with at most 3 penalties. He employs the following strategy:
- **Step 1:** James attempts the path $P_1: (1, 1) \to (1, 1) \to (2, 1) \to \dots \to (R, 1)$.
If $P_1$ is safe, he wins with 0 penalties. If $P_1$ hits a trap at $(r_1, 1)$, he now knows $c_{r_1} = 1$. Since all $c_r$ are distinct, he knows that for all $r \neq r_1$, the cell $(r, 1)$ is safe.

- **Step 2:** James attempts to cross row $r_1$ using column $k=2$. He follows the path $P_2$:
$(1, 1) \to (r_1-1, 1) \to (r_1-1, 2) \to (r_1, 2) \to (r_1+1, 2) \to (r_1+1, 1) \to (R, 1)$.
(If $r_1=2$, he starts at $(1, 1) \to (1, 2) \to (2, 2) \to (3, 2) \to (3, 1) \to (R, 1)$. If $r_1=R-1$, he ends at $(R-2, 2) \to (R-1, 2) \to (R, 2) \to (R, 1)$).
The only cells in $P_2$ that could be traps are $(r_1-1, 2)$ and $(r_1+1, 2)$, because $c_r = 1$ for $r=r_1$ and $c_r \neq 1$ for $r \neq r_1$.
If $P_2$ is safe, he wins with 1 penalty. If $P_2$ hits a trap at $(r_2, 2)$, he now knows $c_{r_2} = 2$, where $r_2 \in \{r_1-1, r_1+1\}$.

- **Step 3:** James attempts to cross row $r_1$ using column $k=3$. He follows the path $P_3$:
$(1, 1) \to (r_1-1, 1) \to (r_1-1, 3) \to (r_1, 3) \to (r_1+1, 3) \to (r_1+1, 1) \to (R, 1)$.
Similar to $P_2$, the only potential traps are $(r_1-1, 3)$ and $(r_1+1, 3)$. However, since he knows $c_{r_2} = 2$, the cell $(r_2, 3)$ cannot be a trap. Thus, only one cell in $P_3$ (the one in row $r_3 \in \{r_1-1, r_1+1\} \setminus \{r_2\}$) can be a trap.
If $P_3$ is safe, he wins with 2 penalties. If $P_3$ hits a trap at $(r_3, 3)$, he now knows $c_{r_3} = 3$.

- **Step 4:** James attempts to cross row $r_1$ using column $k=4$. He follows the path $P_4$:
$(1, 1) \to (r_1-1, 1) \to (r_1-1, 4) \to (r_1, 4) \to (r_1+1, 4) \to (r_1+1, 1) \to (R, 1)$.
Now he knows $c_{r_1}=1, c_{r_1-1} \in \{2, 3\}, c_{r_1+1} \in \{2, 3\}$. Thus, none of the cells in $P_4$ can be traps. He wins with at most 3 penalties.
Thus, $n \le 4$.

**2. Lower Bound:**
We show that Peter can force at least 3 penalties for any strategy James uses.
Let James's strategy be $\mathcal{S}$. Peter can choose $S$ adaptively based on the paths James chooses:
1. James chooses $P_1$. Peter picks any $(r_1, c_1) \in P_1$ and sets $c_{r_1} = c_1$. James hits this trap and resets.
2. James now knows $c_{r_1} = c_1$ and chooses $P_2$. To avoid $(r_1, c_1)$, $P_2$ must at some point move to a cell $(r, c)$ where $c \neq c_1$. Peter picks such a cell $(r_2, c_2) \in P_2$ with $r_2 \neq r_1$ and sets $c_{r_2} = c_2$. James hits this trap and resets.
3. James now knows $c_{r_1} = c_1$ and $c_{r_2} = c_2$ and chooses $P_3$. To avoid both traps, $P_3$ must again visit some cell $(r_3, c_3)$ where $r_3 \notin \{r_1, r_2\}$ and $c_3 \notin \{c_1, c_2\}$. Since $P_3$ must cross all rows $2, \dots, 3001$, and there are 3001 columns, Peter can always pick such a cell $(r_3, c_3) \in P_3$ and set $c_{r_3} = c_3$.
Thus, Peter can force James to receive 3 penalties.

The smallest $n$ such that James can guarantee success before $n$ penalties is $n = 4$.
