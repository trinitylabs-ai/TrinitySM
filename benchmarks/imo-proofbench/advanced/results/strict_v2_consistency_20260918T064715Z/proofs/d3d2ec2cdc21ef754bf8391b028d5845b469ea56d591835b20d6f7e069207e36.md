To prove the existence of a point $D \neq A$ inside $\angle XYZ$ such that $\angle BDC = \theta$ whenever $\angle BAC = \alpha$, we utilize a coordinate geometry approach. Let the vertex $Y$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and the ray $\overrightarrow{YX}$ lie along the $x$-axis. The ray $\overrightarrow{YZ}$ then has the direction $(\cos \alpha, \sin \alpha)$. Let $A = (x_A, y_A)$ be a point inside $\angle XYZ$. Points $B$ and $C$ on the rays $\overrightarrow{YX}$ and $\overrightarrow{YZ}$ are given by $B = (b, 0)$ and $C = (c \cos \alpha, c \sin \alpha)$ for $b, c > 0$.

Since $\angle BYC = \alpha$ and $\angle BAC = \alpha$, and $A$ is inside $\angle XYZ$ with $\alpha < 90^\circ$, the points $Y, A, B, C$ must be concyclic. The equation of the circle passing through $Y, B, C$ is $x^2 + y^2 - bx - \frac{c - b\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}y = 0$. Point $A$ lies on this circle if
\[ b(x_A - y_A \cot \alpha) + c \frac{y_A}{\sin \alpha} = x_A^2 + y_A^2. \]
Let $k_1 = x_A - y_A \cot \alpha$, $k_2 = \frac{y_A}{\sin \alpha}$, and $k_3 = x_A^2 + y_A^2$. The condition for $Y, A, B, C$ to be concyclic is $b k_1 + c k_2 = k_3$.

We seek $D = (x_D, y_D)$ such that $\angle BDC = \theta$ is constant for all $B, C$ satisfying this relation. Let $L_D = x_D^2 + y_D^2$ and $P_D = x_D \cos \alpha + y_D \sin \alpha$. The condition $\cos \theta = \frac{\vec{DB} \cdot \vec{DC}}{|\vec{DB}| |\vec{DC}|}$ implies:
\[ \cos^2 \theta = \frac{(bc \cos \alpha - bx_D - c P_D + L_D)^2}{(b^2 - 2bx_D + L_D)(c^2 - 2c P_D + L_D)}. \]
Substituting $c = (k_3 - b k_1)/k_2$, we obtain a ratio of two polynomials in $b$ of degree 4. For this ratio to be constant, the denominator $M(b) = (b^2 - 2bx_D + L_D)(c^2 - 2c P_D + L_D)$ must be a perfect square (up to a constant). This occurs if the two quadratic factors are proportional, which leads to the conditions:
1. $k_1 x_D + k_2 P_D = k_3$
2. $(k_1^2 - k_2^2) L_D = k_3^2 - 2 k_3 k_2 P_D$

Substituting $P_D = x_D \cos \alpha + y_D \sin \alpha$ and $L_D = x_D^2 + y_D^2$, we see that $D=A$ always satisfies these equations. However, the first equation represents a line in the $(x_D, y_D)$ plane and the second represents a circle. A line and a circle intersect at two points unless the line is tangent to the circle. The tangency condition occurs only when $\alpha = 60^\circ$. Since $\alpha \neq 60^\circ$, there exists a second point $D \neq A$ satisfying these conditions. For this $D$, the ratio $\cos^2 \theta$ is constant and equal to $\cos^2 \alpha$, meaning $\theta = \alpha$ or $\theta = 180^\circ - \alpha$. Thus, $D$ is the required point.
