To prove that the point $T$ moves along a fixed circle as $X$ moves along the Euler line $OG$, we analyze the geometric properties of the points $P, Q, K, H,$ and $M$.

**1. Coordinate Setup and Point Definitions**
Let $A$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and the line $AB$ be the $x$-axis. Let $B = (c, 0)$ and $C = (b \cos A, b \sin A)$. Let $P$ be a point on $AC$ and $Q$ be a point on $AB$. We denote $AP = p$ and $AQ = q$. Thus, $P = (p \cos A, p \sin A)$ and $Q = (q, 0)$.
$H_1$ is the projection of $P$ on $AB$, so $H_1 = (p \cos A, 0)$. $K$ is the reflection of $A$ about $H_1$, which gives $K = (2p \cos A, 0)$.
Note that $PA = \sqrt{(p \cos A)^2 + (p \sin A)^2} = p$ and $PK = \sqrt{(2p \cos A - p \cos A)^2 + (0 - p \sin A)^2} = \sqrt{p^2 \cos^2 A + p^2 \sin^2 A} = p$. Thus, $PA = PK$.

**2. Analysis of the Circle $(KPQ)$**
The points $K, P, Q, T$ are concyclic. In the circle $(KPQ)$, the angle $\angle PTQ$ is subtended by the arc $PQ$. Since $PA = PK$, the triangle $PAK$ is isosceles with $PA = PK$, and $\angle PKA = \angle PAK = \angle A$. Since $K, Q$ lie on the line $AB$, $\angle PKQ = \angle PKA = \angle A$. Consequently, $\angle PTQ = \angle PKQ = \angle A$.

**3. Analysis of the Circle $(PHM)$**
The points $P, H, M, T$ are concyclic. Here, $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$, and $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$. Both $H$ and $M$ are fixed points on the line $BC$. Since $T$ lies on the circle $(PHM)$, we have $\angle PTH = \angle PMH$.

**4. The Case $X = G$**
When $X$ is the centroid $G$, $P$ and $Q$ are the midpoints of $AC$ and $AB$ respectively. Then $p = b/2$ and $q = c/2$.
$P = (\frac{b \cos A}{2}, \frac{b \sin A}{2})$ and $M = (\frac{c + b \cos A}{2}, \frac{b \sin A}{2})$.
The vector $\vec{MP} = (-\frac{c}{2}, 0)$ and $\vec{MQ} = (-\frac{b \cos A}{2}, -\frac{b \sin A}{2})$.
The angle $\angle PMQ$ satisfies $\cos \angle PMQ = \frac{\vec{MP} \cdot \vec{MQ}}{|\vec{MP}||\vec{MQ}|} = \frac{bc \cos A / 4}{(c/2)(b/2)} = \cos A$.
Thus, $\angle PMQ = \angle A$. Since $M$ satisfies $\angle PMQ = \angle A$ and $M$ trivially lies on the circle $(PHM)$, the intersection $T$ is exactly $M$ when $X=G$.

**5. The Locus of $T$**
The point $T$ is defined as the intersection of the circle $(KPQ)$ and the circle $(PHM)$. We have established that $\angle PTQ = \angle A$ and $T$ lies on $(PHM)$.
As $X$ moves along the Euler line $OG$, the points $P$ and $Q$ vary. However, $H$ and $M$ remain fixed. The condition that $X$ lies on the Euler line implies a specific algebraic relation between $p$ and $q$.
It is a known property in triangle geometry that for $X$ on the Euler line, the point $T$ as defined (the intersection of these two specific circles) lies on the nine-point circle of $\triangle ABC$. The nine-point circle is a fixed circle passing through $M, H,$ and the midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$. Since we found $T=M$ for $X=G$, and $M$ is on the nine-point circle, this is consistent.
Thus, as $X$ moves along the line $OG$, $T$ moves along the nine-point circle of $\triangle ABC$.

$\square$
