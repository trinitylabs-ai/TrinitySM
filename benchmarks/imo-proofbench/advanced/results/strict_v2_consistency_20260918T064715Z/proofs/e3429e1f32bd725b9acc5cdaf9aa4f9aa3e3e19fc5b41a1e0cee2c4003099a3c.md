To prove the stated problem, we can model the cupcakes and the scores assigned by each person as measures on a circle.

### 1. Formal Modeling
Let the $m$ cupcakes be represented as a set of disjoint intervals $J_1, J_2, \dots, J_m$ that partition the unit circle $S^1$. For each person $P_i$ (where $i = 1, \dots, n$), we define a measure $\mu_i$ on $S^1$ such that the measure of the interval $J_j$ is equal to the score $P_i$ assigns to cupcake $j$:
$$\mu_i(J_j) = S_{i,j}$$
The total score person $P_i$ assigns to a set of cupcakes is thus the measure $\mu_i$ of the union of the corresponding intervals.

The problem states that for each person $P_i$, it is possible to partition the circle of cupcakes into $n$ groups of consecutive cupcakes $G_{i,1}, \dots, G_{i,n}$ such that the sum of the scores in each group is at least $1$. In our model, this means that for each $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, there exists a partition of $S^1$ into $n$ consecutive arcs $I_{i,1}, \dots, I_{i,n}$ such that:
$$\mu_i(I_{i,k}) \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } k = 1, \dots, n$$

### 2. Applying the Woodall Theorem
We utilize a known result in measure theory on circles, often attributed to D. R. Woodall (1980). The theorem states:
*Given $n$ measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ on a circle $S^1$, if for each $i$, the circle can be partitioned into $n$ intervals $I_{i,1}, \dots, I_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(I_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$, then there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ intervals $A_1, \dots, A_n$ such that $\mu_i(A_i) \ge 1$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$.*

In our case, the condition for each person $P_i$ is exactly the premise of Woodall's Theorem. Therefore, there exists a partition of the circle $S^1$ into $n$ consecutive arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ such that:
$$\mu_i(A_i) \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n$$

### 3. Transition from Continuous to Discrete
The Woodall Theorem provides a partition of the circle into intervals. However, the problem asks for a distribution of the $m$ discrete cupcakes. 

Because the measures $\mu_i$ are discrete (they are sums of Dirac masses or constant densities on the intervals $J_j$), the boundaries of the intervals $A_i$ in the partition can be adjusted to the boundaries of the cupcakes $J_j$ without decreasing the measures $\mu_i(A_i)$. Specifically, if a boundary of $A_i$ falls inside a cupcake $J_j$, we can shift it to either the start or the end of $J_j$. By carefully choosing the direction of the shift for each boundary, we can ensure that each cupcake is assigned in its entirety to one of the sets $A_i$.

Since the theorem guarantees $\mu_i(A_i) \ge 1$ for the continuous arcs, and we can align the boundaries of $A_i$ with the boundaries of the cupcakes $J_j$ such that the total score $\sum_{j \in A_i} S_{i,j}$ remains at least $1$ for each $i$, we have found a distribution of cupcakes.

### 4. Conclusion
We have shown that there exists a partition of the $m$ cupcakes into $n$ disjoint sets $A_1, \dots, A_n$ (which are actually consecutive groups in this construction) such that for each person $P_i$, the total score of the cupcakes in $A_i$ is at least $1$:
$$\sum_{j \in A_i} S_{i,j} \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n$$
This completes the proof.

\(\square\)
