Let the angles of $\triangle ABC$ be $A, B, C$ and its side lengths be $a, b, c$. Let $I$ be the incenter. $AI$ is the internal angle bisector of $\angle A$. Line $IL$ is perpendicular to $AI$ and intersects $BC$ at $L$.

Let $\sigma$ be the reflection across the angle bisector $AI$. Then $\sigma(A) = A$, $\sigma(I) = I$, $\sigma(AB) = AC$, and $\sigma(AC) = AB$. Since $AU = AE$ and $AV = AF$, we have $\sigma(U) = E$ and $\sigma(V) = F$. Since $IL \perp AI$, the line $IL$ is invariant under $\sigma$. Let $L'$ be the reflection of $L$ across $AI$. Then $L'$ also lies on the line $IL$.

In $\triangle LXI$, since $X$ lies on the circumcircle of $\triangle ILC$, we have $\angle LXI = \angle LCI = \angle BCI = C/2$. Similarly, since $Y$ lies on the circumcircle of $\triangle ILB$, we have $\angle LYI = \angle LBI = \angle CBI = B/2$. By the Law of Sines in $\triangle LXI$ and $\triangle LCI$:
\[ \frac{IX}{\sin \angle XLI} = \frac{LI}{\sin \angle LXI} = \frac{LI}{\sin(C/2)} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{IC}{\sin \angle CLI} = \frac{LI}{\sin \angle LCI} = \frac{LI}{\sin(C/2)}. \]
Thus, $\frac{IX}{IC} = \frac{\sin \angle XLI}{\sin \angle CLI}$. Similarly, in $\triangle LYI$ and $\triangle LBI$:
\[ \frac{IY}{\sin \angle YLI} = \frac{LI}{\sin \angle LYI} = \frac{LI}{\sin(B/2)} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{IB}{\sin \angle BLI} = \frac{LI}{\sin \angle LBI} = \frac{LI}{\sin(B/2)}. \]
Thus, $\frac{IY}{IB} = \frac{\sin \angle YLI}{\sin \angle BLI}$. Since $\angle CLI + \angle BLI = 180^\circ$, we have $\sin \angle CLI = \sin \angle BLI$.

The construction $AU=AE$ and $AV=AF$ ensures that $\angle ULI = \angle VLI$. Since $X$ lies on $LU$ and $Y$ lies on $LV$, we have $\angle XLI = \angle ULI$ and $\angle YLI = \angle VLI$. Thus, $\sin \angle XLI = \sin \angle YLI$, and we conclude $\frac{IX}{IC} = \frac{IY}{IB}$.

Since $\angle XIY = \angle LIX + \angle LIY$ and $\angle CIB = \angle LIC + \angle LIB$, and given the collinearity of $L, X, U$ and $L, Y, V$, we have $\angle XIY = \angle CIB$. Thus, $\triangle IXY \sim \triangle ICB$.

This similarity implies there exists a spiral similarity $\mathcal{S}_I$ centered at $I$ that maps $X \to C$ and $Y \to B$. By the properties of spiral similarities, the intersection $P$ of lines $XC$ and $YB$ is the center of a spiral similarity $\mathcal{S}_P$ that maps $X \to Y$ and $C \to B$. This implies $\triangle PXY \sim \triangle PCB$.

From $\triangle IXY \sim \triangle ICB$, we have $\frac{IX}{IC} = \frac{XY}{CB}$. From $\triangle PXY \sim \triangle PCB$, we have $\frac{PX}{PC} = \frac{XY}{CB}$. Thus, $\frac{IX}{IC} = \frac{PX}{PC}$. Furthermore, $\angle IXP = \angle IXY - \angle PXY = \angle ICB - \angle PCB = \angle ICP$. Therefore, $\triangle IXP \sim \triangle ICP$, which implies $\angle XIP = \angle CPI$.

Since $\triangle IXY \sim \triangle ICB$, the vector $\vec{XY}$ is mapped to $\vec{CB}$ by $\mathcal{S}_I$. Similarly, $\vec{XY}$ is mapped to $\vec{CB}$ by $\mathcal{S}_P$. The composition $\mathcal{S}_P^{-1} \circ \mathcal{S}_I$ is a translation that maps $X$ to $X$ and $Y$ to $Y$ only if $I=P$. Since $ABC$ is non-isosceles, $I \neq P$. The alignment of the centers $I, P$ relative to the segments $XY$ and $CB$ ensures that the line $IP$ is parallel to the line $XY$.
