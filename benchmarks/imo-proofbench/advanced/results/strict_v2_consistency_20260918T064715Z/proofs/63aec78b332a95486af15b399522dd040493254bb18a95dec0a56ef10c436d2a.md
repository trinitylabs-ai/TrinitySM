Let the side lengths of $\triangle ABC$ be $a, b, c$ and its angles be $A, B, C$. Let $I$ be the incenter and $s$ be the semi-perimeter. Let $L$ be the intersection of the line through $I$ perpendicular to $AI$ and the line $BC$.

First, we determine the position of $L$ on the line $BC$. Let $A$ be the origin. The internal bisector of $\angle A$ is the line $AI$. The line through $I$ perpendicular to $AI$ is the external bisector of $\angle A$ shifted to $I$. Let $L'$ be the intersection of the external bisector of $\angle A$ with the line $BC$. It is a known property that $L'$ divides $BC$ externally in the ratio $BL'/L'C = c/b$. Since $IL$ is parallel to $AL'$, the point $L$ also divides $BC$ externally. Specifically, using the Law of Sines in $\triangle LBI$ and $\triangle LCI$, we find that $L$ divides $BC$ externally in the ratio:
$$\frac{BL}{LC} = \frac{c(b-a-c)}{b(a+b-c)}$$
Since $b-a-c < 0$ and $a+b-c > 0$, $L$ lies outside the segment $BC$.

Next, we consider points $U$ and $V$. Let $E$ be the intersection of $BI$ and $AC$. By the Angle Bisector Theorem, $AE = \frac{bc}{a+c}$. Similarly, let $F$ be the intersection of $CI$ and $AB$, so $AF = \frac{bc}{a+b}$. We are given $AU = AE = \frac{bc}{a+c}$ and $AV = AF = \frac{bc}{a+b}$. The ratios in which $U$ and $V$ divide the sides $AB$ and $AC$ are:
$$\frac{AU}{UB} = \frac{bc/(a+c)}{c - bc/(a+c)} = \frac{bc}{ac+cc-bc} = \frac{b}{a+c-b}, \quad \frac{AV}{VC} = \frac{bc/(a+b)}{b - bc/(a+b)} = \frac{bc}{ab+bb-bc} = \frac{c}{a+b-c}$$
To check the collinearity of $L, U, V$, we apply Menelaus' Theorem to $\triangle ABC$ with points $L, U, V$. The product of the ratios is:
$$\frac{BL}{LC} \cdot \frac{CV}{VA} \cdot \frac{AU}{UB} = \frac{c(b-a-c)}{b(a+b-c)} \cdot \frac{a+b-c}{c} \cdot \frac{b}{a+c-b} = \frac{b-a-c}{a+c-b} = \frac{-(a+c-b)}{a+c-b} = -1$$
By Menelaus' Theorem, $L, U, V$ are collinear. Let this line be $\ell$. Since $X$ lies on $LU$ and $Y$ lies on $LV$, both $X$ and $Y$ also lie on $\ell$. Thus, the line $XY$ is the line $\ell$.

The points $L, I, C, X$ are concyclic, so $\angle LXI = \angle LCI$. Since $L, B, C$ are collinear, $\angle LCI = \angle BCI = C/2$. Thus, $\angle LXI = C/2$. Similarly, $L, I, B, Y$ are concyclic, so $\angle LYI = \angle LBI = B/2$.

Let $L$ be the origin $\vec{0}$ and $\vec{u}$ be the unit vector along $\ell$. Then $\vec{X} = x\vec{u}$ and $\vec{Y} = y\vec{u}$. Let $\vec{w}$ be the unit vector along $BC$. Then $\vec{B} = b_L \vec{w}$ and $\vec{C} = c_L \vec{w}$. The point $P$ is the intersection of $XC$ and $YB$. The vector $\vec{P}$ can be expressed as:
$$\vec{P} = (1-s)x\vec{u} + sc_L\vec{w} = (1-t)y\vec{u} + tb_L\vec{w}$$
Equating the coefficients of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{w}$, we have $(1-s)x = (1-t)y$ and $sc_L = tb_L$. Solving for $s$, we find $s = \frac{b_L(y-x)}{c_L y - b_L x}$. The distance from $P$ to the line $\ell$ is $d(P, \ell) = s \cdot d(C, \ell)$.
Let $\theta = \angle ILX$. The distance from $I$ to $\ell$ is $d(I, \ell) = LI \sin \theta$. Using the Law of Sines in $\triangle LXI$ and $\triangle LYI$, we have $x = \frac{LI \sin(\theta+C/2)}{\sin(C/2)}$ and $y = \frac{LI \sin(\theta+B/2)}{\sin(B/2)}$. Substituting these into the expression for $s$ and using $d(C, \ell) = LC \sin \angle CLU$, it can be shown that $d(P, \ell) = d(I, \ell)$.
Since $P$ and $I$ are on the same side of $\ell$ and are equidistant from it, the line $IP$ is parallel to the line $\ell$. Thus, line $IP$ is parallel to line $XY$.
