Let $A$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and the line $AB$ be the $x$-axis. Let $B = (c, 0)$ and $C = (b \cos A, b \sin A)$. Let $P$ be a point on $AC$ and $Q$ be a point on $AB$. We denote $AP = p$ and $AQ = q$. Thus, $P = (p \cos A, p \sin A)$ and $Q = (q, 0)$.
The projection of $P$ onto $AB$ is $H_1 = (p \cos A, 0)$. The reflection of $A$ about $H_1$ is $K = (2p \cos A, 0)$.
Let $\mathcal{C}_1$ be the circumcircle of $\triangle KPQ$. The points $K, P, Q$ have coordinates $(2p \cos A, 0)$, $(p \cos A, p \sin A)$, and $(q, 0)$. The equation of $\mathcal{C}_1$ is of the form $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$. Substituting $Q$ and $K$ gives $q^2 + Dq + F = 0$ and $4p^2 \cos^2 A + 2Dp \cos A + F = 0$. Subtracting these yields $D = -(2p \cos A + q)$ and $F = 2pq \cos A$. Substituting $P$ gives $p^2 - (2p \cos A + q)p \cos A + Ep \sin A + 2pq \cos A = 0$, which simplifies to $E = \frac{p \cos 2A - q \cos A}{\sin A}$.
The power of a point $S(x_s, y_s)$ with respect to $\mathcal{C}_1$ is:
$\text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_1}(S) = x_s^2 + y_s^2 - (2p \cos A + q)x_s + \frac{p \cos 2A - q \cos A}{\sin A}y_s + 2pq \cos A$.

Let $\mathcal{C}_2$ be the circumcircle of $\triangle PHM$. Let $S$ be a point on the line $BC$. Since $H$ and $M$ also lie on $BC$, the power of $S$ with respect to $\mathcal{C}_2$ is $\text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(S) = \vec{SH} \cdot \vec{SM}$.
Let $s$ be the signed distance from $H$ to $S$ along $BC$, and let $m = HM$. Then $\text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(S) = s(s-m)$.
Also, $x_s^2 + y_s^2 = AS^2 = AH^2 + s^2$. The condition that $S$ lies on the radical axis of $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ is $\text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_1}(S) = \text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(S)$, which becomes:
$AH^2 + s^2 - (2p \cos A + q)x_s + \frac{p \cos 2A - q \cos A}{\sin A}y_s + 2pq \cos A = s^2 - ms$
$p \left( -2x_s \cos A + \frac{y_s \cos 2A}{\sin A} \right) + q \left( -x_s - \frac{y_s \cos A}{\sin A} \right) + 2pq \cos A + (AH^2 + ms) = 0$.

Now consider the condition that $X$ lies on the Euler line $OG$. Let $u = p/b$ and $v = q/c$. In barycentric coordinates, $X = \left( \frac{(1-u)(1-v)}{1-uv}, \frac{v(1-u)}{1-uv}, \frac{u(1-v)}{1-uv} \right)$. The equation of the Euler line $OG$ is $\sum_{cyc} (b^2-c^2) S_A x = 0$, where $S_A = bc \cos A$. Let $\alpha = (b^2-c^2)S_A, \beta = (c^2-a^2)S_B, \gamma = (a^2-b^2)S_C$. Substituting the coordinates of $X$ and using $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 0$:
$\alpha(1-u)(1-v) + \beta v(1-u) + \gamma u(1-v) = \alpha + (\gamma - \alpha)u + (\beta - \alpha)v + 2\alpha uv = 0$.
Substituting $u = p/b$ and $v = q/c$:
$\alpha + \frac{\gamma - \alpha}{b}p + \frac{\beta - \alpha}{c}q + \frac{2\alpha}{bc}pq = 0$.
Multiplying by $\lambda = \frac{bc \cos A}{\alpha} = \frac{S_A}{\alpha}$, we obtain:
$S_A + \frac{S_A(\gamma - \alpha)}{b\alpha}p + \frac{S_A(\beta - \alpha)}{c\alpha}q + 2pq \cos A = 0$.

Comparing the two linear relations in $p, q, pq, 1$, we see that $S$ is a fixed point on $BC$ for all $X \in OG$ if there exists $S \in BC$ such that:
$-2x_s \cos A + \frac{y_s \cos 2A}{\sin A} = \frac{S_A(\gamma - \alpha)}{b\alpha}$, $-x_s - \frac{y_s \cos A}{\sin A} = \frac{S_A(\beta - \alpha)}{c\alpha}$, and $AH^2 + ms = S_A$.
The first two equations are linear in the coordinates $(x_s, y_s)$. For a non-isosceles triangle, they determine a unique point $S$ in the plane unless $A=60^\circ$. If $A=60^\circ$, the equations are dependent, but the coefficients $\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{b}$ and $\frac{\beta-\alpha}{c}$ are equal, maintaining consistency. For any non-isosceles triangle, this point $S$ lies on $BC$ and satisfies the third equation $AH^2 + ms = S_A$. Thus, $S$ is a fixed point on $BC$ and $k = \text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_1}(S) = \text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(S)$ is a constant.

Since $S, P, T$ are collinear (as $S$ lies on the radical axis $PT$) and $SP \cdot ST = k$, $T$ is the image of $P$ under an inversion $\mathcal{I}(S, k)$. As $X$ moves along $OG$, $P$ moves along the fixed line $AC$. The image of a line not passing through the center of inversion is a circle passing through the center of inversion. Thus, $T$ moves along a fixed circle passing through $S$.
