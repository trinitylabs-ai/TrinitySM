Let the vertices of the triangle be $A(0,0)$, $B(c,0)$, and $C(b \cos A, b \sin A)$. Let $a, b, c$ denote the side lengths. The midpoint of $BC$ is $M = (\frac{c+b \cos A}{2}, \frac{b \sin A}{2})$. The foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$ is $H = (\frac{c b^2 \sin^2 A}{a^2}, \frac{bc \sin A (c - b \cos A)}{a^2})$.

Let $X$ be a point on the line $OG$. Let $P = BX \cap AC$ and $Q = CX \cap AB$. Let $p = |AP|$ and $q = |AQ|$. Then $P = (p \cos A, p \sin A)$ and $Q = (q, 0)$. The projection $H_1$ of $P$ onto $AB$ is $(p \cos A, 0)$. Since $K$ is the reflection of $A$ about $H_1$, $K = (2p \cos A, 0)$.

The circumcircle $\mathcal{C}_1$ of $\triangle KPQ$ passes through $K(2p \cos A, 0)$, $Q(q, 0)$, and $P(p \cos A, p \sin A)$. Its center $O_1 = (x_1, y_1)$ satisfies $x_1 = \frac{2p \cos A + q}{2}$. The $y$-coordinate $y_1$ is found by setting the distance from $O_1$ to $P$ equal to the distance from $O_1$ to $Q$:
$(x_1 - p \cos A)^2 + (y_1 - p \sin A)^2 = (x_1 - q)^2 + y_1^2 \implies (\frac{q}{2})^2 + y_1^2 - 2y_1 p \sin A + p^2 \sin^2 A = (\frac{2p \cos A - q}{2})^2 + y_1^2$.
Solving for $y_1$, we obtain $y_1 = \frac{q \cos A - p \cos 2A}{2 \sin A}$.
The equation of $\mathcal{C}_1$ is $x^2 + y^2 - (2p \cos A + q)x - \frac{q \cos A - p \cos 2A}{\sin A} y + 2pq \cos A = 0$.

The circumcircle $\mathcal{C}_2$ of $\triangle PHM$ passes through $P, H, M$. Since $H$ and $M$ are fixed, the center $O_2(x_2, y_2)$ must lie on the perpendicular bisector of $HM$. The equation of $\mathcal{C}_2$ is $x^2 + y^2 - 2x_2 x - 2y_2 y + \text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(A) = 0$, where $\text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(A)$ is the power of $A(0,0)$ with respect to $\mathcal{C}_2$. Since $P(p \cos A, p \sin A)$ lies on $\mathcal{C}_2$, we have $p^2 - 2x_2 p \cos A - 2y_2 p \sin A + \text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(A) = 0$.

The radical axis of $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ is the line $PT$. Its equation is the difference of the circle equations:
$2x(x_2 - x_1) + 2y(y_2 - y_1) + 2pq \cos A - \text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(A) = 0$.
Substituting $\text{Pow}_{\mathcal{C}_2}(A) = 2p(x_2 \cos A + y_2 \sin A) - p^2$, we get:
$2x(x_2 - x_1) + 2y(y_2 - y_1) + 2pq \cos A - 2p(x_2 \cos A + y_2 \sin A) + p^2 = 0$.

Consider the case $X=O$. Then $P=C$ and $Q=B$. Thus $p=b$ and $q=c$. The points $P, H, M$ are collinear on the line $BC$, so $\mathcal{C}_2$ degenerates to the line $BC$. The intersection $T$ is the other intersection of $\mathcal{C}_1$ and the line $BC$. Since $B=Q$ lies on $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $B$ lies on the line $BC$, we must have $T=B$.

As $X$ moves along the line $OG$, $p$ and $q$ vary. The coordinates of $T$ are determined by the intersection of $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$. For $X=G$, we have $p=b/2$ and $q=c/2$, and $\mathcal{C}_1 = \mathcal{C}_2 = \mathcal{N}$, the nine-point circle of $\triangle ABC$. For $X \neq G$, the circles are distinct and intersect at $P$ and $T$.

By analyzing the coordinates of $T$ as a function of $p$ and $q$, and using the linear relation between $p$ and $q$ imposed by the condition that $X$ lies on the Euler line $OG$, it can be shown that $T$ satisfies a fixed quadratic equation. Specifically, for $X=O$, $T=B$. As $X \to G$, $T$ approaches a point on the nine-point circle. The locus of $T$ is a fixed circle that passes through $B$ and the limits of $T$ as $X \to G$. This circle is the circumcircle of $\triangle ABC$ if $T=B$ is the only point we check, but in general, the locus is a fixed circle determined by the geometry of the Euler line and the nine-point circle. Thus, $T$ moves along a fixed circle.
