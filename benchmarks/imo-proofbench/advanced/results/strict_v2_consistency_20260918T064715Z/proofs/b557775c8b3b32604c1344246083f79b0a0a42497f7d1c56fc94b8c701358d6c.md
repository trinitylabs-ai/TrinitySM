To determine which player has a winning strategy, we analyze the conditions under which roads are constructed between cities.

Let the points in the plane be represented by complex numbers. For any three points $U, V, W$, the condition that $\triangle UVW$ is directly similar to $\triangle XYZ$ is given by the equation $\frac{w-u}{v-u} = \frac{z-x}{y-x}$. Alice chooses points $P, Q$ and a subset $S$ of the plane. Let $S'$ be the set of complex numbers $\alpha = \frac{r-p}{q-p}$ for $r \in S$. A road exists between cities $A$ and $B$ if and only if for every other city $C$, there exists $R \in S$ such that $\triangle PQR$ is directly similar to either $\triangle ABC$ or $\triangle BAC$.

Translating this into complex numbers, the road $AB$ exists if and only if for every city $C \notin \{A, B\}$,
\[ \frac{c-a}{b-a} \in S' \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{c-b}{a-b} \in S'. \]
Let $z_{ABC} = \frac{c-a}{b-a}$. Note that $\frac{c-b}{a-b} = \frac{c-a+a-b}{a-b} = \frac{c-a}{a-b} + 1 = 1 - z_{ABC}$. Thus, the condition for a road $AB$ to exist is that for every city $C \notin \{A, B\}$, $z_{ABC} \in S' \cup (1-S')$. Let $T = S' \cup (1-S')$.

Bob wins if the resulting graph is either disconnected or non-planar. We consider two cases for the set $T$:

Case 1: $T \neq \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$.
If $T$ is not the set of all non-real complex numbers, there exists some $w \in (\mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}) \setminus T$. Bob can place cities $C_1, C_2, \dots$ inductively to ensure no roads exist. He picks $C_1, C_2$ arbitrarily. For every pair $(i, j)$, he can pick a city $C_k$ (with $k$ not yet used) such that $z_{C_i C_j C_k} = w$. Since $w \notin \mathbb{R}$, $C_k$ is not collinear with $C_i$ and $C_j$. By picking $w$ and the initial points carefully (or by choosing different $w_{ij} \notin T \cup \mathbb{R}$ for each pair), Bob can ensure that all cities are at distance $>1$ and no three are collinear. Because for every pair $(i, j)$, there exists a city $C_k$ such that $z_{C_i C_j C_k} \notin T$, no road $C_i C_j$ can ever be formed. A graph with infinitely many vertices and no roads is disconnected, so Bob wins.

Case 2: $T = \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$.
If $T$ consists of all non-real complex numbers, then for any three cities $C_i, C_j, C_k$, the value $z_{C_i C_j C_k}$ is necessarily in $T$ because Bob is forbidden from placing three cities in a collinear arrangement (which would make $z_{C_i C_j C_k} \in \mathbb{R}$). Consequently, every pair of cities is connected by a road, and the resulting graph is the complete graph $K_\infty$. A complete graph with infinitely many vertices is not planar (for instance, any four cities forming a convex quadrilateral will have crossing diagonals). Thus, Bob wins.

In all possible choices of $P, Q$, and $S$ by Alice, Bob has a strategy to ensure the resulting graph is either disconnected or non-planar.

Bob has a winning strategy. \(\square\)
