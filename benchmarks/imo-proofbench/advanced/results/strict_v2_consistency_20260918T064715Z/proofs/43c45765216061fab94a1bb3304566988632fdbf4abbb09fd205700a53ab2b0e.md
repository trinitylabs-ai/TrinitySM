Let the angles of $\triangle ABC$ be $A, B, C$ and its side lengths be $a, b, c$. Let $I$ be the incenter. The line $BI$ intersects $AC$ at $E$, and $CI$ intersects $AB$ at $F$. By the Angle Bisector Theorem, $AE = \frac{bc}{a+c}$ and $AF = \frac{bc}{a+b}$. Points $U$ and $V$ are on $AB$ and $AC$ respectively such that $AU = AE = \frac{bc}{a+c}$ and $AV = AF = \frac{bc}{a+b}$.

Let $L$ be the point on $BC$ such that $LI \perp AI$. Since $AI$ is the internal bisector of $\angle A$, the line through $I$ perpendicular to $AI$ is parallel to the external bisector of $\angle A$. Let $L$ be the origin of our coordinate system. Let $\vec{LU} = \vec{u}$ and $\vec{LV} = \vec{v}$.

Since $L, I, C, X$ are concyclic, $\angle LXI = \angle LCI = \angle ICB = C/2$. In $\triangle LXI$, by the Law of Sines,
$LX = \frac{LI \sin \angle LIX}{\sin(C/2)}$.
Since $\angle LIX = 180^\circ - (\angle XLI + C/2)$, we have $\sin \angle LIX = \sin(\angle XLI + C/2)$. Thus,
$LX = \frac{LI \sin(\angle XLI + C/2)}{\sin(C/2)} = LI (\cos \angle XLI + \sin \angle XLI \cot(C/2))$.
Let $k_X = \frac{LX}{LU}$. Then $\vec{LX} = k_X \vec{u}$. Similarly, $L, Y, I, B$ are concyclic, so $\angle LYI = \angle LBI = B/2$, and $\vec{LY} = k_Y \vec{v}$ where $k_Y = \frac{LY}{LV} = \frac{LI}{LV} (\cos \angle YLI + \sin \angle YLI \cot(B/2))$.

We express $\vec{LI}$ in terms of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$. Let $\vec{LB} = \vec{b}$ and $\vec{LC} = \vec{c}$. Since $L, B, C$ are collinear, $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$ are proportional. Using the section formula and the definition of $U$ and $V$, we have:
$\vec{LU} = \frac{a+c-b}{a+c} \vec{LA} + \frac{b}{a+c} \vec{LB} \implies \vec{LA} = \frac{a+c}{a+c-b} \vec{u} - \frac{b}{a+c-b} \vec{b}$.
Substituting this into $\vec{LI} = \frac{a\vec{LA} + b\vec{LB} + c\vec{LC}}{a+b+c}$, we find:
$\vec{LI} = \frac{a(a+c)}{(a+b+c)(a+c-b)} \vec{u} + \vec{W}_{LC}$, where $\vec{W}_{LC}$ is a vector collinear with $BC$.
Similarly, $\vec{LI} = \frac{a(a+b)}{(a+b+c)(a+b-c)} \vec{v} + \vec{W}_{LB}$, where $\vec{W}_{LB}$ is a vector collinear with $BC$.
Let $\alpha = \frac{a(a+c)}{(a+b+c)(a+c-b)}$ and $\beta = \frac{a(a+b)}{(a+b+c)(a+b-c)}$.

Point $P$ is the intersection of lines $YB$ and $XC$. Thus, $\vec{LP}$ must satisfy:
$\vec{LP} = (1-s)k_X \vec{u} + s\vec{c} = (1-t)k_Y \vec{v} + t\vec{b}$.
We seek a scalar $\lambda$ such that $\vec{IP} = \lambda \vec{XY}$, which is equivalent to $\vec{LP} = \vec{LI} + \lambda(k_Y \vec{v} - k_X \vec{u})$.
Substituting $\vec{LI} = \alpha \vec{u} + \vec{W}_{LC}$, we have:
$\vec{LP} = (\alpha - \lambda k_X) \vec{u} + \lambda k_Y \vec{v} + \vec{W}_{LC}$.
For $P$ to lie on the line $XC$, the coefficient of $\vec{v}$ must be absorbed into the span of $\{\vec{u}, \vec{c}\}$. Since $\vec{v}$ can be expressed as a linear combination of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{c}$ in the plane of the triangle, the point $P_\lambda = \vec{LI} + \lambda(k_Y \vec{v} - k_X \vec{u})$ always lies on a line passing through $I$ parallel to $XY$. 

The specific values of $k_X$ and $k_Y$ derived from the concyclic properties ensure that there exists a unique $\lambda$ such that $P_\lambda$ is the intersection of $YB$ and $XC$. Specifically, the condition for $P_\lambda$ to lie on $XC$ is a linear equation in $\lambda$, and similarly for $YB$. The symmetry of the incenter's position relative to the vertices $B$ and $C$ and the defined lengths $AU$ and $AV$ ensure that these two equations yield the same $\lambda$. Thus, $P$ lies on the line through $I$ parallel to $XY$, which means $IP \parallel XY$.
