To determine the values of $\theta \in (0^\circ, 180^\circ)$ for which Mulan can guarantee victory, we analyze the conditions under which she can force an angle of size $\theta$ to appear in the triangle $T$.

Let the angles of the current triangle $T$ be $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$. Mulan's move consists of choosing a vertex (say $A$) and a point $P$ on the opposite side $BC$. This splits $\angle A$ into $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ (where $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \alpha$). The two resulting triangles $T_1$ and $T_2$ have angle sets:
$S_1 = \{\alpha_1, \beta, 180^\circ - \alpha_1 - \beta\}$ and $S_2 = \{\alpha_2, \gamma, 180^\circ - \alpha_2 - \gamma\}$.
Shan-Yu chooses which triangle to keep. Mulan wins if she can force $\theta$ to be an angle of $T$.

**1. Forcing a $90^\circ$ angle**
Mulan can force a $90^\circ$ angle regardless of the initial triangle $T_0$. Let the angles of $T$ be $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$.
If $\beta < 90^\circ$ and $\gamma < 90^\circ$, Mulan chooses vertex $A$ and splits $\alpha$ into $\alpha_1 = 90^\circ - \beta$ and $\alpha_2 = 90^\circ - \gamma$. Then $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 180^\circ - (\beta + \gamma) = \alpha$.
The angles of $T_1$ are $\{90^\circ - \beta, \beta, 180^\circ - (90^\circ - \beta) - \beta\} = \{90^\circ - \beta, \beta, 90^\circ\}$.
The angles of $T_2$ are $\{90^\circ - \gamma, \gamma, 180^\circ - (90^\circ - \gamma) - \gamma\} = \{90^\circ - \gamma, \gamma, 90^\circ\}$.
Both triangles contain $90^\circ$, so Mulan forces a $90^\circ$ angle in one move.
If $\beta \ge 90^\circ$, then $\alpha < 90^\circ$ and $\gamma < 90^\circ$. Mulan can apply the same logic to vertex $B$, splitting $\beta$ into $\beta_1 = 90^\circ - \alpha$ and $\beta_2 = 90^\circ - \gamma$. Then $\beta_1 + \beta_2 = 180^\circ - (\alpha + \gamma) = \beta$, and both resulting triangles will contain $90^\circ$.
Thus, Mulan can always force a $90^\circ$ angle in one move.

**2. Forcing $\theta = 90^\circ/k$**
If $\theta = 90^\circ/k$ for some $k \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, Mulan can guarantee victory:
- For $k=1$, $\theta = 90^\circ$, and Mulan wins in one move.
- For $k > 1$, Mulan first forces a $90^\circ$ angle. Once she possesses an angle $\phi$, she can force the angle $\phi/m$ for any integer $m \ge 2$ as follows:
  She splits $\phi$ into $\phi/m$ and $(m-1)\phi/m$. If Shan-Yu keeps the triangle with $\phi/m$, she has achieved the angle. If he keeps the triangle with $(m-1)\phi/m$, she now possesses an angle $(m-1)\phi/m$. She repeats this process, splitting the remaining angle into $\phi/m$ and $(m-2)\phi/m$, and so on. After at most $m-1$ steps, she will either have Shan-Yu keep a triangle with $\phi/m$ or reach a state where she splits $2\phi/m$ into $\phi/m$ and $\phi/m$, forcing the angle $\phi/m$ regardless of Shan-Yu's choice.
By applying this to $\phi = 90^\circ$ and $m=k$, Mulan can force the angle $90^\circ/k$.

**3. Other values of $\theta$**
If $\theta \neq 90^\circ/k$ for any $k \in \mathbb{N}$, Shan-Yu can prevent Mulan's victory. Mulan can force an angle $\theta$ in one move if and only if $\theta = 90^\circ$ or if the triangle has an angle $\alpha = 2\theta$ (or if $\theta$ is already present). This is because for $\theta \in S_1$ and $\theta \in S_2$ to hold without $\theta$ being $\beta$ or $\gamma$, we must have $\alpha_1 \in \{\theta, 180^\circ-\beta-\theta\}$ and $\alpha_2 \in \{\theta, 180^\circ-\gamma-\theta\}$. The only valid combinations for $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \alpha$ are $\alpha = 2\theta$ or $\theta = 90^\circ$.
Consequently, any angle $\phi$ that Mulan can force from any initial triangle must be reachable from $90^\circ$ through the operation of taking integer divisors. The set of all such angles is $\{90^\circ/k \mid k \in \mathbb{N}\}$.
By choosing the initial triangle $T_0$ such that its angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are not related to $\theta$ by any such process (e.g., choosing them to be transcendental or linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $\theta$ and $180^\circ$), Shan-Yu ensures that $\theta$ can never be forced.

Mulan can guarantee victory if and only if $\theta = \frac{90^\circ}{k}$ for some $k \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.
