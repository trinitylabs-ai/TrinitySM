To determine the largest number $c$ such that Liu Bang can guarantee a total length of at least $c$ regardless of Xiang Yu's play, we analyze the game's mechanics based on the number of points $n$.

Let the final pieces be $l_1 \ge l_2 \ge \dots \ge l_N$, where $N$ is the total number of pieces. Liu and Xiang take turns claiming pieces greedily, so Liu receives $l_1, l_3, l_5, \dots$ and Xiang receives $l_2, l_4, l_6, \dots$. Liu's total length is $S = \sum_{i \text{ odd}} l_i$ and Xiang's is $T = \sum_{i \text{ even}} l_i$. Since $l_1 \ge l_2, l_3 \ge l_4$, and so on, we always have $S \ge T$. Since $S + T = 1$, it follows that $S \ge 1/2$ for any $n$.

**Case 1: $n = 1$**
Liu marks one point at $x = 1/3$. Xiang then marks one point at $y$.
If $y < 1/3$, the resulting pieces have lengths $y, 1/3 - y$, and $1 - 1/3 = 2/3$. The median length $l_2$ is $\max(y, 1/3 - y)$. Since $y \in (0, 1/3)$, the maximum value of $l_2$ occurs as $y \to 0$ or $y \to 1/3$, where $l_2 \to 1/3$. Thus, $l_2 \le 1/3$ for all $y < 1/3$.
If $y > 1/3$, the resulting pieces have lengths $1/3, y - 1/3$, and $1 - y$. The median length $l_2$ is $\text{median}(1/3, y - 1/3, 1 - y)$. For $l_2$ to be different from $1/3$, either two values must be strictly greater than $1/3$ or two must be strictly less than $1/3$.
1. If $y - 1/3 > 1/3$ and $1 - y > 1/3$, then $y > 2/3$ and $y < 2/3$, which is impossible.
2. If $y - 1/3 < 1/3$ and $1 - y < 1/3$, then $y < 2/3$ and $y > 2/3$, which is impossible.
Thus, $l_2$ is always $1/3$ for any $y \in (1/3, 1)$.

In both subcases, $l_2 \le 1/3$. Liu's total length is $S = l_1 + l_3 = 1 - l_2 \ge 1 - 1/3 = 2/3$.
To show $c=2/3$, we check if Xiang can force $S \le 2/3$ for any $x$ Liu chooses. If Liu marks $x \le 1/2$, Xiang can pick $y = (1+x)/2 > x$. The pieces are $x, (1-x)/2, (1-x)/2$. The median is $l_2 = \max(x, (1-x)/2)$. For $x=1/3$, $l_2=1/3$ and $S=2/3$. For $x \neq 1/3$, $l_2 > 1/3$ and $S < 2/3$. If $x > 1/2$, Xiang can pick $y = x/2 < x$, and the pieces are $x/2, x/2, 1-x$. The median is $l_2 = \max(x/2, 1-x)$, which is $\ge 1/3$ (since $x > 1/2 \implies x/2 > 1/4$ and $1-x < 1/2$; specifically, if $x=2/3$, $l_2=1/3$). In all cases, Xiang can ensure $l_2 \ge 1/3$, so $S \le 2/3$. Thus, $c = 2/3$ for $n=1$.

**Case 2: $n \ge 2$**
Liu can always guarantee $S \ge 1/2$ by marking no points. In this case, Xiang marks $m \le n$ points, creating $N = m + 1 \le n + 1$ pieces. As established, $S \ge 1/2$ always.
We now check if Xiang can force $S$ to be arbitrarily close to $1/2$. If Liu marks no points, Xiang can mark points at $y_1 = 1/2$ and $y_2 = 1/2 + \epsilon$, creating pieces of length $1/2, \epsilon, 1/2 - \epsilon$. The sorted pieces are $l_1 = 1/2, l_2 = 1/2 - \epsilon, l_3 = \epsilon$. Liu's score is $S = l_1 + l_3 = 1/2 + \epsilon$. As $\epsilon \to 0$, $S \to 1/2$.

If Liu marks $k$ points ($1 \le k \le n$), the stick is divided into $k+1$ pieces $p_1 \ge p_2 \ge \dots \ge p_{k+1}$. Xiang can use his $n$ points to subdivide these pieces. To minimize $S$, Xiang aims to make $l_1 \approx l_2$ and $l_3, l_5, \dots$ as small as possible.
Since $n \ge 2$, Xiang can pick the largest piece $p_1$ and mark two points $y_1, y_2$ inside it to split $p_1$ into three pieces: $\epsilon, (p_1 - \epsilon)/2, (p_1 - \epsilon)/2$. The resulting collection of pieces is $\{(p_1 - \epsilon)/2, (p_1 - \epsilon)/2, \epsilon, p_2, \dots, p_{k+1}\}$.
If $p_1$ is large (e.g., $p_1 \to 1$), then $p_2, \dots, p_{k+1} \to 0$. The sorted pieces are $l_1 \approx 1/2, l_2 \approx 1/2$, and $l_i \approx 0$ for $i \ge 3$. Thus $S = l_1 + l_3 + \dots \to 1/2$.
If $p_1$ is not large, Xiang can still force $S \to 1/2$ by splitting the largest piece into two nearly equal halves and using the remaining points to further subdivide the resulting largest pieces or create very small pieces. For example, if $p_1=p_2=p_3=1/3$ and $n=2$, Xiang splits $p_1$ into $\epsilon, 1/6, 1/6$. The pieces are $1/3, 1/3, 1/6, 1/6, \epsilon$. The sorted pieces are $l_1=1/3, l_2=1/3, l_3=1/6, l_4=1/6, l_5=\epsilon$. Liu's score is $S = 1/3 + 1/6 + \epsilon = 1/2 + \epsilon$.

In general, by using his $n$ points to create pairs of pieces of nearly equal length from the largest available segments, Xiang can ensure $l_1 \approx l_2, l_3 \approx l_4, \dots$, which forces $S$ to be arbitrarily close to $1/2$.

Thus, for $n \ge 2$, the largest value Liu can guarantee is $1/2$.

The largest number $c$ is $2/3$ for $n=1$ and $1/2$ for $n \ge 2$.
