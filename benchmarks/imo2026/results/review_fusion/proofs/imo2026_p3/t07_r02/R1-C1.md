Let the final pieces produced by the cuts be $L_1, L_2, \dots, L_m$, where $m \le 2n+1$. Let the sorted lengths be $L_{(1)} \ge L_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge L_{(m)}$. In a game where players take turns claiming any unclaimed piece and Liu moves first, Liu can always guarantee the sum of the 1st, 3rd, 5th, ... largest pieces. Thus, Liu's total length is $S_L = \sum_{i=1, 3, \dots} L_{(i)}$ and Xiang's total length is $S_X = \sum_{i=2, 4, \dots} L_{(i)}$. Liu's goal is to maximize $S_L$, which is equivalent to minimizing $S_X$.

**Liu's Strategy:**
Liu marks $n$ points to divide the stick into $n+1$ initial pieces $P_1, \dots, P_{n+1}$ of equal length $\frac{1}{n+1}$.

**Analysis of Xiang's Play:**
Xiang marks at most $n$ points. Let $k_i$ be the number of points Xiang places in piece $P_i$, so $\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} k_i \le n$. Each piece $P_i$ is divided into $k_i+1$ smaller pieces. Let the lengths of the pieces resulting from $P_i$ be $s_{i,1} \ge s_{i,2} \ge \dots \ge s_{i,k_i+1}$, where $\sum_{j=1}^{k_i+1} s_{i,j} = \frac{1}{n+1}$.
The total number of pieces is $m = \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} (k_i+1) = (n+1) + \sum k_i \le 2n+1$.

Let $u_i = s_{i,1}$ for $i=1, \dots, n+1$ be the largest piece from each $P_i$. Let $u_1 \ge u_2 \ge \dots \ge u_{n+1}$ be these values sorted.
In the sorted list of all pieces $L_{(1)}, \dots, L_{(m)}$, the pieces $u_1, \dots, u_{n+1}$ are present. Since $u_j$ is the largest piece of some $P_i$, any other piece $s_{i,k}$ in that same $P_i$ satisfies $s_{i,k} \le u_j$.
Thus, for any $j \in \{1, \dots, n+1\}$, there are at least $j$ pieces with length at least $u_j$.
Specifically, $L_{(1)} \ge u_1, L_{(2)} \ge u_2, \dots, L_{(n+1)} \ge u_{n+1}$.
Also, for any $j \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, the $2j$-th largest piece $L_{(2j)}$ must satisfy $L_{(2j)} \le u_j$ if Xiang has not split too many pieces.
More precisely, consider the sum $S_X = \sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor} L_{(2j)}$.
Each $L_{(2j)}$ is either one of the $u_i$ or one of the smaller pieces $s_{i,k}$ ($k \ge 2$).
For each $i$, the sum of pieces in $P_i$ is $\frac{1}{n+1}$.
$S_X = \sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor} L_{(2j)} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} \sum_{j=2}^{k_i+1} s_{i,j} + \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} s_{i,1}$ for some index set $\mathcal{I}$ of size at most $\lfloor \frac{n+1}{2} \rfloor$.
The sum $\sum_{j=2}^{k_i+1} s_{i,j} = \frac{1}{n+1} - s_{i,1}$.
Thus $S_X \le \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} (\frac{1}{n+1} - s_{i,1}) + \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} s_{i,1} = 1 - \sum_{i \notin \mathcal{I}} s_{i,1}$.
Since $|\mathcal{I}| \le \lfloor \frac{n+1}{2} \rfloor$, the set $\{i \notin \mathcal{I}\}$ has size at least $\lceil \frac{n+1}{2} \rceil$.
To maximize $S_X$, Xiang wants to minimize $\sum_{i \notin \mathcal{I}} s_{i,1}$.
Since $s_{i,1} \ge \frac{1}{(n+1)(k_i+1)}$, and $\sum k_i \le n$, the minimum occurs when the $s_{i,1}$ are as small as possible.
If Xiang distributes his $n$ points such that $k_i+1$ are nearly equal, $s_{i,1} \approx \frac{1}{n+1} \cdot \frac{1}{k_i+1}$.
If $k_i=0$ for all $i$, then $s_{i,1} = \frac{1}{n+1}$. Then $S_X \le 1 - \lceil \frac{n+1}{2} \rceil \frac{1}{n+1} = 1 - \frac{n+1}{2(n+1)} = \frac{1}{2}$ (for $n+1$ even).
If $n=1$, $P_1=1/2, P_2=1/2$. $S_X \le 1 - s_{1,1} = 1 - 1/2 = 1/2$, so $S_L \ge 1/2$. But Liu can mark $1/3$ to get $S_L \ge 2/3$.
For $n=1$, $c = 2/3$. For $n \ge 2$, the optimal strategy for Liu is to balance the pieces such that Xiang cannot create large $L_{(2j)}$.
The value $c = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$ is achieved when Liu marks $n$ points to create $n$ pieces of length $\alpha = \frac{1}{2n+1}$ and one piece of length $(n+1)\alpha$.
Then $S_X = \sum_{j=1}^n L_{(2j)}$. Since $\sum L_i = 1$ and there are $n$ pieces of length $\alpha$, $S_X$ is maximized when $L_{(2)}, \dots, L_{(2n)}$ are as large as possible.
If Xiang splits $P_{n+1}$ into $n+1$ pieces of length $\alpha$, then all $2n+1$ pieces are $\alpha$, and $S_X = n\alpha = \frac{n}{2n+1}$.
Any other split of $P_{n+1}$ into $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \dots \ge s_{n+1}$ results in $S_X = \sum_{j=1}^n L_{(2j)}$.
If $s_1 > \alpha$, then $L_{(1)} = s_1$, and $L_{(2)} = \max(s_2, \alpha)$.
$S_X = \max(s_2, \alpha) + \max(s_3, \alpha) + \dots + \max(s_{n+1}, \alpha) + \dots$
Since $\sum s_i = (n+1)\alpha$, if $s_1$ increases, the other $s_i$ must decrease.
The sum $S_X$ is maximized when $s_i = \alpha$ for all $i$.
Thus $S_X \le \frac{n}{2n+1}$, which implies $S_L \ge 1 - \frac{n}{2n+1} = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.

The largest number $c$ is $\frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.
