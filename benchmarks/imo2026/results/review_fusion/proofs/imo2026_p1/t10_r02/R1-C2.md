### Part 1: Termination and the Number of Integers Greater than 1

Let the integers on the blackboard be $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N$ where $N=2026$. A move consists of choosing two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ from different positions and replacing them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $h = \frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)}$.

We first prove that this process terminates. For any prime $p$, let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $x$. Let $S$ be the total sum of all exponents of all primes across all integers on the blackboard:
\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_p v_p(a_i) \]
In a move $(m, n) \to (g, h)$, let $e = v_p(m)$ and $f = v_p(n)$. The new exponents for $p$ are $v_p(g) = \min(e, f)$ and $v_p(h) = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(g) = \max(e, f) - \min(e, f) = |e - f|$.
The sum of exponents for prime $p$ changes from $e + f$ to $\min(e, f) + |e - f| = \max(e, f)$. Since $\max(e, f) \le e + f$, the total sum $S$ is non-increasing. Specifically:
- If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then for at least one prime $p$, $\min(e, f) > 0$, so $S$ strictly decreases.
- If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, $S$ remains constant. However, the new integer $g = \gcd(m, n) = 1$, so the number of integers $k$ greater than 1 on the blackboard decreases by at least 1.

Thus, the pair $(S, k)$ decreases lexicographically with each move. Since $S$ and $k$ are non-negative integers, the process must terminate. Termination occurs when no two integers $m, n > 1$ can be chosen, meaning at most one integer is greater than 1.

We now prove that exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains. Suppose $m > 1$ and $n > 1$. The resulting integers are $g$ and $h$. If both $g=1$ and $h=1$, then $\text{lcm}(m, n) = \gcd(m, n)$, which implies $m=n$. But if $m=n$, then $g = m > 1$, a contradiction. Thus, it is impossible for both resulting integers to be 1. Consequently, the number of integers greater than 1 decreases by at most 1 in each move. Since we started with $N=2026 > 1$, the process cannot end with zero integers greater than 1. Thus, exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains.

### Part 2: Invariance of the Value of $M$

For a fixed prime $p$, let the exponents of $p$ for the integers on the board be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N$. When Confucius chooses two integers $m, n$ at positions $i$ and $j$, the exponents $x_i, x_j$ are replaced by $x_i' = \min(x_i, x_j)$ and $x_j' = |x_i - x_j|$.

We claim that the greatest common divisor of the exponents of $p$ is invariant. For any two non-negative integers $x, y$, the following holds:
\[ \gcd(x, y) = \gcd(\min(x, y), |x - y|) \]
This is a fundamental property of the Euclidean algorithm:
- If $x > y > 0$, then $\gcd(x, y) = \gcd(y, x - y)$.
- If $x = y > 0$, then $\gcd(x, x) = x$ and $\gcd(x, 0) = x$.
- If $x > 0, y = 0$, then $\gcd(x, 0) = x$ and $\gcd(0, x) = x$.

Since the operation $(x_i, x_j) \to (\min(x_i, x_j), |x_i - x_j|)$ preserves the GCD of the two exponents, the GCD of the entire set $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N\}$ is invariant throughout the process. Let $g_p = \gcd(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_N))$.

At the end of the process, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1. Thus, for every prime $p$, the exponents are $v_p(M), 0, 0, \dots, 0$. The GCD of these exponents is $\gcd(v_p(M), 0, \dots, 0) = v_p(M)$. Since the GCD is invariant, we must have:
\[ v_p(M) = g_p = \gcd(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026})) \]
This holds for all primes $p$, so the final value $M$ is:
\[ M = \prod_p p^{\gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_{2026}))} \]
Since $M$ depends only on the initial set of integers, its value is independent of Confucius's choices. \(\square\)
