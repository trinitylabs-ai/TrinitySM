To prove the properties of the process described, let the integers on the blackboard be denoted by the set $S = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$ where $n = 2026$ and $a_i > 1$ for all $i$.

### Part 1: Termination and the existence of $M$

A move consists of choosing two integers $m, n > 1$ and replacing them with $x = \gcd(m, n)$ and $y = \frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)}$. We first analyze how the number of integers greater than 1 changes.

Let $k$ be the number of integers on the blackboard greater than 1. In each move, we replace $m, n > 1$ with $x$ and $y$.
1. If $m = n$, then $x = m > 1$ and $y = 1$. The count $k$ decreases by 1.
2. If $m \neq n$ and $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $x = 1$ and $y = mn > 1$. The count $k$ decreases by 1.
3. If $m \neq n$ and $\gcd(m, n) = g > 1$, then $x = g > 1$. Since $m \neq n$, it is impossible for both $m=g$ and $n=g$ to be true. If $m=g$, then $n=kg$ for some $k > 1$, so $y = \frac{kg \cdot g / g}{g} = k > 1$. If $m, n > g$, then $y = \frac{m}{g} \cdot \frac{n}{g} > 1$. In this case, the count $k$ remains the same.

In all cases, at least one of the resulting integers $x, y$ is greater than 1. Thus, $k$ is non-increasing and $k \ge 1$ always. The process terminates when $k < 2$, which means exactly one integer $M$ remains greater than 1.

To prove that this happens in finitely many moves, we define a monovariant. For any prime $p$, let $v_p(a)$ be the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $a$. Let $\Phi = \sum_{p} \sum_{i=1}^n v_p(a_i)^2$, where the sum over $p$ is over all primes dividing the initial product of the integers.
In a move on $m, n$, let $e_p = v_p(m)$ and $f_p = v_p(n)$. The new exponents are $e'_p = \min(e_p, f_p)$ and $f'_p = |e_p - f_p|$. Let $x = \min(e_p, f_p)$ and $y = \max(e_p, f_p)$. The change in $\Phi$ for prime $p$ is:
$$\Delta \Phi_p = (x^2 + y^2) - (x^2 + (y-x)^2) = x^2 + y^2 - (x^2 + y^2 - 2xy + x^2) = 2xy - x^2 = x(2y - x)$$
Since $y \ge x \ge 0$, we have $\Delta \Phi_p \ge 0$. Specifically, $\Delta \Phi_p = 0$ if and only if $x = 0$.
Thus, $\Phi$ is non-increasing. $\Phi$ remains constant if and only if for every prime $p$, $\min(e_p, f_p) = 0$. This condition is equivalent to $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.
As established, if $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, the count $k$ strictly decreases. If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then for at least one prime $p$, $x = \min(e_p, f_p) > 0$, so $\Delta \Phi_p > 0$ and $\Phi$ strictly decreases.
Since $\Phi \ge 0$ and $k \ge 1$ are integers (or sums of squares of integers), the process must terminate in finitely many moves.

### Part 2: Invariance of $M$

We now show that $M$ is independent of the choices made. For a fixed prime $p$, let $E_p = \{v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n)\}$ be the set of exponents. A move on $a_j, a_k$ replaces $e_j, e_k \in E_p$ with $\min(e_j, e_k)$ and $|e_j - e_k|$.
Assume without loss of generality that $e_j \le e_k$. Then the new exponents are $e_j$ and $e_k - e_j$. A fundamental property of the greatest common divisor is that $\gcd(e, f) = \gcd(e, f-e)$ for $f \ge e$. Consequently, the greatest common divisor of the set $E_p$ is invariant under every move:
$$\gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n)) = \text{constant}$$
At termination, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1, so for every prime $p$, only one exponent in $E_p$ can be non-zero. Let this exponent be $v_p(M)$. By the invariance of the $\gcd$:
$$v_p(M) = \gcd(v_p(a_{1, \text{initial}}), \dots, v_p(a_{n, \text{initial}}))$$
The value of $M$ is thus uniquely determined by the initial integers:
$$M = \prod_{p} p^{\gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n))}$$
where the product is over all primes $p$ that divide at least one $a_i$. This value is independent of Confucius's choices.

\(\square\)
