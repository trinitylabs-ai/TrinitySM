To prove the statements provided in the problem, we analyze the effect of the operation on the integers written on the blackboard. Let the 2026 integers be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{2026}$.

### Part (1): Termination and Final State

Let $N$ be the number of integers on the blackboard that are greater than 1. Let $S$ be the sum of the prime valuations of all integers on the blackboard:
\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^{2026} \sum_{p} v_p(x_i) \]
where $v_p(x)$ denotes the exponent of the prime $p$ in the prime factorization of $x$. Since each $x_i > 1$ initially, the initial sum $S$ is a positive integer.

In one move, Confucius chooses two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ and replaces them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $L = \frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)}$. We examine how $N$ and $S$ change during this operation:

1.  **Change in $S$**: For any prime $p$, let $a = v_p(m)$ and $b = v_p(n)$. The new valuations are $v_p(g) = \min(a, b)$ and $v_p(L) = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(g) = \max(a, b) - \min(a, b)$.
    The sum of valuations for $p$ changes from $a + b$ to $\min(a, b) + \max(a, b) - \min(a, b) = \max(a, b)$.
    The total change in $S$ is:
    \[ \Delta S = \sum_p (\max(v_p(m), v_p(n)) - (v_p(m) + v_p(n))) = -\sum_p \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) = -\sum_p v_p(\gcd(m, n)) \]
    If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then $S$ strictly decreases. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, $S$ remains unchanged.

2.  **Change in $N$**:
    - If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $g = 1$ and $L = \text{lcm}(m, n)$. Since $m, n > 1$, we have $L > 1$. Thus, $N$ decreases by exactly 1.
    - If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then $g > 1$. If $L = 1$, then $\text{lcm}(m, n) = \gcd(m, n)$, which implies $m = n$. In this case, $N$ decreases by 1. If $L > 1$, $N$ remains unchanged.

In every move, the pair $(N, S)$ decreases lexicographically: either $N$ decreases, or $N$ remains the same and $S$ decreases. Since $N$ is bounded below by 1 (because if $m, n > 1$, then at least one of $g = \gcd(m, n)$ or $L = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n)$ must be greater than 1) and $S$ is bounded below by 0, the process must terminate in finitely many moves. Termination occurs when no two integers greater than 1 can be chosen, which implies that exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains on the blackboard.

### Part (2): Invariance of $M$

For a fixed prime $p$, consider the set of valuations $\{v_p(x_1), v_p(x_2), \dots, v_p(x_{2026})\}$. In each move, two valuations $a$ and $b$ are replaced by $\min(a, b)$ and $|a - b|$. 
We observe that the greatest common divisor of these valuations is invariant:
\[ \gcd(a, b) = \gcd(\min(a, b), \max(a, b) - \min(a, b)) = \gcd(\min(a, b), |a - b|) \]
This is a fundamental property used in the subtraction-based Euclidean algorithm. Consequently, for every prime $p$, the value $G_p = \gcd(v_p(x_1), v_p(x_2), \dots, v_p(x_{2026}))$ is invariant throughout the process.

When the process terminates, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1. For all other integers $x_j = 1$, we have $v_p(x_j) = 0$. The invariant $G_p$ then becomes:
\[ G_p = \gcd(0, \dots, 0, v_p(M), 0, \dots, 0) = v_p(M) \]
Thus, the prime factorization of $M$ is uniquely determined by the initial integers:
\[ M = \prod_p p^{\gcd(v_p(x_1), \dots, v_p(x_{2026}))} \]
Since this expression depends only on the initial values $x_i$ and not on the sequence of moves, the value of $M$ is invariant.
