To prove the statements, we analyze the transformation of the integers on the blackboard through their prime factorizations and a lexicographical invariant.

### Part 1: Termination of the Process

Let the integers on the blackboard be $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ where $n=2026$. A move consists of choosing two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ from different positions and replacing them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $l = \frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)}$.

Let $\Omega(x)$ denote the number of prime factors of $x$ counted with multiplicity. For any two positive integers $m$ and $n$, we have:
$$\Omega(m) + \Omega(n) = \sum_{p} (v_p(m) + v_p(n))$$
where $v_p(x)$ is the exponent of prime $p$ in the factorization of $x$. After the move, the new sum of $\Omega$ values for these two positions is:
$$\Omega(g) + \Omega(l) = \Omega(\gcd(m, n)) + \Omega\left(\frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)}\right) = \Omega(\text{lcm}(m, n))$$
Using the identity $v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) = \max(v_p(m), v_p(n))$, we see that:
$$\Omega(m) + \Omega(n) - \Omega(\text{lcm}(m, n)) = \sum_{p} (v_p(m) + v_p(n) - \max(v_p(m), v_p(n))) = \sum_{p} \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) = \Omega(\gcd(m, n))$$
Let $S = \sum_{i=1}^n \Omega(a_i)$ be the total sum of $\Omega$ values, and let $k$ be the number of integers on the blackboard greater than 1. We examine the pair $(S, k)$ under a move:
1. If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then $\Omega(\gcd(m, n)) \ge 1$. The sum $S$ decreases by $\Omega(\gcd(m, n)) > 0$. Regardless of whether $k$ increases, decreases, or stays the same, the pair $(S, k)$ decreases lexicographically. (Note that $k$ cannot increase because we replace two integers $>1$ with at most two integers $>1$).
2. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $\Omega(\gcd(m, n)) = 0$, so $S$ remains constant. However, the new integers are $g = 1$ and $l = \text{lcm}(m, n)/1 = mn$. Since $m, n > 1$, we have $mn > 1$. Thus, we replace two integers $>1$ with exactly one integer $>1$, and $k$ decreases by 1. Again, $(S, k)$ decreases lexicographically.

Since $S \ge 0$ and $k \ge 0$ are integers, this lexicographical decrease cannot continue indefinitely. The process terminates when no two integers are greater than 1, meaning $k \le 1$. 
Since each move replaces $m, n > 1$ with $g$ and $l$ such that $g \cdot l = \text{lcm}(m, n) > 1$, at least one integer greater than 1 always remains on the board. Thus, the process terminates when exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains.

### Part 2: Invariance of $M$

For a fixed prime $p$, let $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ be the exponents $v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_n)$. A move on $m$ and $n$ replaces their exponents $(v_p(m), v_p(n))$ with:
$$v_p(g) = \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) \quad \text{and} \quad v_p(l) = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(\gcd(m, n)) = \max(v_p(m), v_p(n)) - \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) = |v_p(m) - v_p(n)|$$
It is a fundamental property of the greatest common divisor that for any non-negative integers $x$ and $y$:
$$\gcd(x, y) = \gcd(\min(x, y), |x - y|)$$
The $\gcd$ of a set of numbers is invariant if we replace two elements $x, y$ with $x', y'$ such that $\gcd(x, y) = \gcd(x', y')$. Specifically:
$$\gcd(x_1, \dots, x, y, \dots, x_n) = \gcd(\gcd(x, y), \{x_i\}_{i \neq \text{pos}(x,y)}) = \gcd(\gcd(\min(x, y), |x-y|), \{x_i\}_{i \neq \text{pos}(x,y)})$$
Consequently, for every prime $p$, the $\gcd$ of the set of exponents $\{v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n)\}$ is invariant under the move.

Let $g_p = \gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n))$ be the initial $\gcd$ for prime $p$. In the final state, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1, and all other $a_i = 1$. The exponents for prime $p$ are then $(v_p(M), 0, \dots, 0)$. The $\gcd$ of these exponents is:
$$\gcd(v_p(M), 0, \dots, 0) = v_p(M)$$
Thus, $v_p(M) = g_p$ for every prime $p$. This implies that $M$ is uniquely determined by the initial set of integers:
$$M = \prod_{p} p^{\gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n))}$$
Since $M$ depends only on the initial values, it is independent of the choices made by Confucius.
