Let the integers on the blackboard at any time $t$ be $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{2026}$. A move consists of choosing two integers $m, n > 1$ from different positions and replacing them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $k = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n)$.

### Part 1: Proof of Termination
We define $N(t)$ as the number of integers greater than 1 on the blackboard at time $t$. We define a potential function $V(t) = \sum_p \sum_{i=1}^{2026} (v_p(a_i))^2$, where $v_p(x)$ is the exponent of the prime $p$ in the prime factorization of $x$. The sum is taken over all primes $p$ that divide at least one of the initial integers on the blackboard.

Consider the effect of a move on $N(t)$ and $V(t)$:
1.  **Case 1: $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.**
    In this case, $g = 1$ and $k = \text{lcm}(m, n)/1 = mn$. The integers $m, n > 1$ are replaced by $1$ and $mn$. Thus, $N(t+1) = N(t) - 1$.
2.  **Case 2: $\gcd(m, n) > 1$.**
    - If $m = n$, then $g = m$ and $k = \text{lcm}(m, m)/m = 1$. The integers $m, n$ are replaced by $m$ and $1$. Thus, $N(t+1) = N(t) - 1$.
    - If $m \neq n$, then $g = \gcd(m, n) > 1$. Furthermore, since $m \neq n$, we have $\text{lcm}(m, n) > \gcd(m, n)$, so $k = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n) > 1$. Thus, $N(t+1) = N(t)$. We then examine the change in $V(t)$. For any prime $p$, let $x = v_p(m)$ and $y = v_p(n)$. The operation replaces $x, y$ with $x' = v_p(g) = \min(x, y)$ and $y' = v_p(k) = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(\gcd(m, n)) = \max(x, y) - \min(x, y) = |x - y|$. The change in the sum of squares for prime $p$ is:
    \[ \Delta V_p = \min(x, y)^2 + (x - y)^2 - (x^2 + y^2) = \min(x, y)^2 - 2xy. \]
    If $x, y > 0$, then $\Delta V_p = \min(x, y)(\min(x, y) - 2\max(x, y))$. Since $\max(x, y) \ge \min(x, y) > 0$, we have $\min(x, y) - 2\max(x, y) \le -\min(x, y) < 0$, so $\Delta V_p < 0$. If at least one of $x, y$ is 0, then $\Delta V_p = 0$. Since $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, there exists at least one prime $p$ such that $v_p(m) > 0$ and $v_p(n) > 0$. Thus, $V(t+1) < V(t)$.

The pair $(N(t), V(t))$ is a strictly decreasing sequence in lexicographical order. Since $N(t)$ and $V(t)$ are non-negative integers, the process must terminate in finitely many moves.

The process terminates when no two integers $m, n > 1$ exist, meaning $N(t) \le 1$. We observe that if $m, n > 1$, then $\text{lcm}(m, n) > 1$. Since the product of the two new integers is $g \cdot k = \text{lcm}(m, n)$, the property that at least one integer on the blackboard is greater than 1 is invariant. Given that we start with 2026 integers greater than 1, the process must end with exactly one integer $M > 1$.

### Part 2: Proof of Invariance
For a fixed prime $p$, let the exponents of $p$ on the board be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{2026}$. The operation replaces two exponents $x, y$ with $\min(x, y)$ and $|x - y|$.

This operation is a step in the subtraction-based Euclidean algorithm. A fundamental property of this algorithm is that it preserves the greatest common divisor:
\[ \gcd(x, y) = \gcd(\min(x, y), |x - y|). \]
Consequently, for every prime $p$, the value $g_p = \gcd(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{2026})$ is invariant throughout the process. At the end of the process, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1, meaning for each prime $p$, only one exponent $v_p(M)$ is non-zero and all other $v_p(a_i) = 0$. The invariant $g_p$ then satisfies:
\[ g_p = \gcd(0, 0, \dots, v_p(M), \dots, 0) = v_p(M). \]
Thus, the final value $M$ is uniquely determined by the initial integers:
\[ M = \prod_p p^{\gcd(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026}))}. \]
Since this expression depends only on the initial values, $M$ is independent of the choices made by Confucius.
