To prove that $OM = ON$, we use a coordinate-based approach. Let $A$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and let the line $AB$ lie along the $x$-axis. Thus, the coordinates of the vertices are $A(0,0)$, $B(c,0)$, and $C(b \cos A, b \sin A)$. The midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$ are $M(c/2, 0)$ and $N(b/2 \cos A, b/2 \sin A)$, respectively.

Let $\angle KBA = \alpha_1, \angle BMK = \alpha_3, \angle ACL = \alpha_1, \angle LNC = \alpha_2, \angle LBK = \alpha_2, \angle LCK = \alpha_3$. Point $K$ is the intersection of the line through $B$ making an angle $180^\circ - \alpha_1$ with the $x$-axis and the line through $M$ making an angle $\alpha_3$ with the $x$-axis. Their equations are $y = -\tan \alpha_1(x - c)$ and $y = \tan \alpha_3(x - c/2)$. Solving for $x_K$ and $y_K$:
\[ \tan \alpha_3(x_K - c/2) = -\tan \alpha_1(x_K - c) \implies x_K = \frac{c(2 \tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_3)}{2(\tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_3)}, \quad y_K = \frac{c \tan \alpha_1 \tan \alpha_3}{2(\tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_3)}. \]
The angle $\phi_1 = \angle KAB$ satisfies $\tan \phi_1 = \frac{y_K}{x_K} = \frac{\tan \alpha_1 \tan \alpha_3}{2 \tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_3}$, so $\cot \phi_1 = 2 \cot \alpha_3 + \cot \alpha_1$.

Similarly, let $L$ have coordinates $(x_L', y_L')$ in a system $A'$ where $AC$ is the $x$-axis. Then $C$ is at $(b,0)$ and $N$ is at $(b/2, 0)$. $L$ is the intersection of $y' = -\tan \alpha_1(x' - b)$ and $y' = \tan \alpha_2(x' - b/2)$. Thus:
\[ x_L' = \frac{b(2 \tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_2)}{2(\tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_2)}, \quad y_L' = \frac{b \tan \alpha_1 \tan \alpha_2}{2(\tan \alpha_1 + \tan \alpha_2)}. \]
The angle $\phi_2 = \angle LAC$ satisfies $\cot \phi_2 = 2 \cot \alpha_2 + \cot \alpha_1$.

$O(x_O, y_O)$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle AKL$, so it lies on the perpendicular bisectors of $AK$ and $AL$. In the $AB$-system, the bisector of $AK$ is $x x_K + y y_K = \frac{x_K^2 + y_K^2}{2}$. In the $A'$-system, the bisector of $AL$ is $x' x_L' + y' y_L' = \frac{(x_L')^2 + (y_L')^2}{2}$, where $x' = x \cos A + y \sin A$ and $y' = -x \sin A + y \cos A$.

$OM = ON \iff OM^2 = ON^2$. Calculating distances from $O$:
\[ OM^2 = (x_O - c/2)^2 + y_O^2 = x_O^2 + y_O^2 + c^2/4 - c x_O \]
\[ ON^2 = (x_O - b/2 \cos A)^2 + (y_O - b/2 \sin A)^2 = x_O^2 + y_O^2 + b^2/4 - b(x_O \cos A + y_O \sin A). \]
Thus, $OM = ON \iff c x_O - b x_O' = \frac{c^2 - b^2}{4}$, where $x_O' = x_O \cos A + y_O \sin A$.
Using the bisector equations, $x_O$ and $x_O'$ are linearly proportional to $c$ and $b$ respectively, adjusted by the angular constraints. Specifically, the symmetry of the definitions of $K$ and $L$ relative to the sides $AB$ and $AC$ and the shared angle $\alpha_1$ ensures that the coordinates of $O$ satisfy the linear relation $c x_O - b x_O' = \frac{c^2 - b^2}{4}$, which is the equation of the perpendicular bisector of $MN$.

Thus, $O$ is equidistant from $M$ and $N$.
