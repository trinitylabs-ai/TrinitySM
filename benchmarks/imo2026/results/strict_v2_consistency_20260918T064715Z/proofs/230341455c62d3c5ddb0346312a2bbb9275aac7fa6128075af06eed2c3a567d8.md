To prove the given statements, we analyze the operation on the integers written on the blackboard and track the properties that change or remain invariant during the process.

### Part (1): Termination of the Process

Let $N = 2026$. Let the set of integers on the blackboard be $S = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N\}$. A move consists of choosing two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ from different positions and replacing them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $l/g = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n)$. 

Let $C$ be the number of integers on the blackboard that are greater than 1, and let $P = \prod_{x \in S} x$ be the product of all integers on the blackboard. We consider the effect of a single move on $C$ and $P$:
1. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $g = 1$ and $l/g = \text{lcm}(m, n) = mn$. The number of integers greater than 1, $C$, decreases by 1 because $m, n > 1$ are replaced by $1$ and $mn > 1$. The product $P$ remains unchanged.
2. If $\gcd(m, n) = g > 1$, the product $P$ changes to $P' = P \cdot \frac{g \cdot (l/g)}{mn} = P \cdot \frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{mn} = P \cdot \frac{1}{\gcd(m, n)}$. Since $g > 1$, the product $P$ strictly decreases.

The process stops when no more moves are possible, which occurs if and only if $C \le 1$. Since $P$ is a positive integer and $C$ is a non-negative integer, and in every move either $C$ decreases or $P$ decreases, the process must terminate in finitely many moves.

We now show that $C$ cannot become 0. If $m, n > 1$, then $\text{lcm}(m, n) \ge m > 1$. If $g = 1$, then $l/g = \text{lcm}(m, n) > 1$. If $g > 1$, then $g$ is already greater than 1. Thus, at least one of the resulting integers is always greater than 1. Since we start with $C = 2026$, the process must terminate with $C = 1$. Thus, exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains on the blackboard.

### Part (2): Independence of $M$

For a fixed prime $p$, let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $x$. For each prime $p$, let the exponents of $p$ in the integers on the blackboard be $x_{1, p}, x_{2, p}, \dots, x_{N, p}$. 
The move $(m, n) \to (g, l/g)$ transforms the pair of exponents $(v_p(m), v_p(n))$ into:
$$v_p(g) = \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) \quad \text{and} \quad v_p(l/g) = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(\gcd(m, n)) = \max(v_p(m), v_p(n)) - \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) = |v_p(m) - v_p(n)|$$
Let $S_p = \{v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_N)\}$ be the multiset of exponents for prime $p$, and let $S_p^* = \{x \in S_p : x > 0\}$ be the set of its non-zero elements.
- If $v_p(m) = 0$ or $v_p(n) = 0$, the set $S_p^*$ remains unchanged.
- If $v_p(m) = x > 0$ and $v_p(n) = y > 0$, the elements $x, y$ are replaced by $\min(x, y)$ and $|x-y|$. 
  - If $x = y$, one non-zero element is removed from $S_p^*$.
  - If $x \neq y$, the elements change, but the greatest common divisor of the elements in $S_p^*$ is invariant because $\gcd(x, y) = \gcd(\min(x, y), |x-y|)$.

In all cases, the value $g_p = \gcd(S_p^*)$ (defined as 0 if $S_p^*$ is empty) is invariant throughout the process. When the process terminates, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1, meaning for every prime $p$, only one exponent $v_p(M)$ can be non-zero. Consequently, the final set $S_p^*$ contains only one element, $v_p(M)$. By the invariance of the $\gcd$ of non-zero elements:
$$v_p(M) = \gcd(\{v_p(a_i) : v_p(a_i) > 0\})$$
Since this value depends only on the initial integers $a_i$, the final value of $M = \prod_p p^{g_p}$ is independent of the choices made by Confucius. $\square$
