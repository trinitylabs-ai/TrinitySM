To determine the largest number $c$ such that Liu Bang can guarantee a total length of at least $c$ regardless of Xiang Yu's play, we analyze the game's mechanics based on the number of points $n$.

Let the final pieces be $l_1 \ge l_2 \ge \dots \ge l_N$, where $N$ is the total number of pieces. Liu and Xiang take turns claiming pieces greedily, so Liu receives $l_1, l_3, l_5, \dots$ and Xiang receives $l_2, l_4, l_6, \dots$. Liu's total length is $S = \sum_{i \text{ odd}} l_i$ and Xiang's is $T = \sum_{i \text{ even}} l_i$. Since $l_1 \ge l_2, l_3 \ge l_4$, and so on, we always have $S \ge T$. Since $S + T = 1$, it follows that $S \ge 1/2$ for any $n$.

**Case 1: $n = 1$**
Liu marks one point at $x = 1/3$. Xiang then marks one point at $y$. The resulting pieces have lengths $1/3, |y - 1/3|$, and $1 - y$. Let these be $a, b, c$. Liu's score is $S = l_1 + l_3 = 1 - l_2$, where $l_2$ is the median of $\{a, b, c\}$.
We examine $l_2 = \text{median}(1/3, |y - 1/3|, 1 - y)$. For $l_2$ to be different from $1/3$, either two values must be strictly greater than $1/3$ or two must be strictly less than $1/3$.
1. If $|y - 1/3| > 1/3$ and $1 - y > 1/3$, then $y > 2/3$ and $y < 2/3$, which is impossible.
2. If $|y - 1/3| < 1/3$ and $1 - y < 1/3$, then $0 < y < 2/3$ and $y > 2/3$, which is impossible.
Thus, $l_2$ is always $1/3$ for any $y \in (0, 1)$. Liu's total length is $S = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3$. Since Xiang can make $l_2$ as large as $1/3$ (e.g., by picking $y=2/3$), $c = 2/3$ for $n=1$.

**Case 2: $n \ge 2$**
Liu can always guarantee $S \ge 1/2$ by marking no points. In this case, Xiang marks $m \le n$ points, creating $N = m + 1 \le n + 1$ pieces. As established, $S \ge 1/2$ always.
We now check if Xiang can force $S$ to be arbitrarily close to $1/2$. If Liu marks $k$ points, Xiang can use his $n$ points to subdivide the largest resulting piece. If Liu marks no points, Xiang can mark points at $y_1 = 1/2$ and $y_2 = 1/2 + \epsilon$, creating pieces of length $1/2, \epsilon, 1/2 - \epsilon$. The sorted pieces are $l_1 = 1/2, l_2 = 1/2 - \epsilon, l_3 = \epsilon$. Liu's score is $S = l_1 + l_3 = 1/2 + \epsilon$. As $\epsilon \to 0$, $S \to 1/2$.
If Liu marks $k \ge 1$ points, Xiang can still use a similar strategy. By splitting the largest piece into two nearly equal halves and making all other pieces negligible, Xiang can ensure that $l_1 \approx l_2 \approx 1/2$ and all other $l_i \approx 0$. This results in $S \approx 1/2$. For example, if $n=2$ and Liu marks $x_1, x_2$, Xiang can mark $y_1, y_2$ such that the largest piece is split into two pieces of length $\approx 1/2$ each, forcing $l_1 \approx 1/2, l_2 \approx 1/2, l_3 \approx 0 \dots$, so $S \approx 1/2$.

Thus, for $n \ge 2$, the largest value Liu can guarantee is $1/2$.

The largest number $c$ is $2/3$ for $n=1$ and $1/2$ for $n \ge 2$.
