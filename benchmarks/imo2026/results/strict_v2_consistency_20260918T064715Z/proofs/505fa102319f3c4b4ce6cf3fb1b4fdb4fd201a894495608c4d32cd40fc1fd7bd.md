To solve the problem, we analyze the effect of the given operation on the integers on the blackboard.

### Part 1: Termination of the Process

Let the integers on the blackboard be $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ where $n = 2026$ and $a_i > 1$ for all $i$. Let $k$ be the number of integers on the blackboard that are greater than 1. A move is possible as long as $k \ge 2$. Let $S = \sum_{i=1}^n \Omega(a_i)$, where $\Omega(x)$ denotes the total number of prime factors of $x$ counted with multiplicity.

When Confucius chooses two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$, they are replaced by $x = \gcd(m, n)$ and $y = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n)$. Note that for any prime $p$, the exponents of $p$ in the prime factorization change as follows:
\[ v_p(x) = \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)), \quad v_p(y) = \max(v_p(m), v_p(n)) - \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) = |v_p(m) - v_p(n)| \]
The sum of the prime exponents for $m$ and $n$ was $\sum_p (v_p(m) + v_p(n))$, and the new sum is $\sum_p (\min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) + |v_p(m) - v_p(n)|)$. Using the identity $\min(a, b) + |a - b| = \max(a, b)$, the new sum is $\sum_p \max(v_p(m), v_p(n))$. The change in $S$ is:
\[ \Delta S = \sum_p \max(v_p(m), v_p(n)) - \sum_p (v_p(m) + v_p(n)) = - \sum_p \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)) = -\Omega(\gcd(m, n)) \]
We consider three cases for the move:
1. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $x = 1$ and $y = mn > 1$. Here, $k$ decreases by 1 and $S$ remains unchanged.
2. If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$ and $m \neq n$, then $x > 1$ and $y = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n) > 1$ (since $m \neq n \implies \text{lcm}(m, n) > \gcd(m, n)$). Here, $k$ remains the same and $S$ decreases by $\Omega(\gcd(m, n)) \ge 1$.
3. If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$ and $m = n$, then $x = m > 1$ and $y = 1$. Here, $k$ decreases by 1 and $S$ decreases by $\Omega(m) \ge 1$.

In every case, the pair $(k, S)$ decreases lexicographically. Since $k$ and $S$ are non-negative integers, the process must terminate. Because each move $(m, n) \to (x, y)$ preserves the property that at least one of the resulting integers is greater than 1 (if $x=1$ then $y=mn>1$, if $x>1$ then $x>1$), and we start with $k=2026$, the process must terminate at $k=1$. Thus, exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains.

### Part 2: Invariance of $M$

For any prime $p$, let $E_p = \{v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_n)\}$ be the set of exponents of $p$ in the prime factorizations of the integers on the blackboard. A move on $m, n$ transforms two exponents $a, b \in E_p$ into $\min(a, b)$ and $|a - b|$.
Define $g(E_p) = \gcd(e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n)$, where $\gcd(0, a) = a$ for $a \ge 0$ and $\gcd(0, 0) = 0$. We observe that:
- If $a=0$ or $b=0$, $\gcd(0, a) = a$ and $\gcd(\min(0, a), |0 - a|) = \gcd(0, a) = a$.
- If $a, b > 0$, then $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(\min(a, b), |a - b|)$ by the subtraction-based Euclidean algorithm.

Thus, for every prime $p$, the value $g(E_p)$ is invariant under the operation. At termination, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1, so the set of exponents for $p$ is $\{v_p(M), 0, 0, \dots, 0\}$. The invariant gives:
\[ v_p(M) = \gcd(v_p(M), 0, \dots, 0) = g(E_p) = \gcd(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_n)) \]
This means $M$ is given by the product:
\[ M = \prod_p p^{\gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_n))} \]
This value depends only on the initial integers $a_1, \dots, a_n$ and is independent of Confucius's choices. Since at least one $a_i > 1$, at least one $g(E_p) \ge 1$, so $M > 1$.

$\square$
