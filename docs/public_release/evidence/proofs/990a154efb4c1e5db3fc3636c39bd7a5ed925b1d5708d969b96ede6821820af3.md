To prove that it is possible to distribute the $m$ cupcakes to the $n$ people such that each person $P_i$ receives cupcakes of total score at least $1$, we use a combination of fair division theory and the properties of measures on a circle.

### 1. Analysis of the Condition
Let the cupcakes be arranged in a circle and denoted by $\{1, 2, \dots, m\}$. For each person $P_i$, let $s_{ij}$ be the score they assign to cupcake $j$. The problem states that for each $P_i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous blocks $B_{i,1}, B_{i,2}, \dots, B_{i,n}$ such that:
\[ \sum_{j \in B_{i,k}} s_{ij} \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } k = 1, \dots, n. \]
Summing over all $k$, we find that the total score person $P_i$ assigns to all $m$ cupcakes is:
\[ \sum_{j=1}^m s_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{j \in B_{i,k}} s_{ij} \ge \sum_{k=1}^n 1 = n. \]

### 2. Continuous Relaxation
To apply results from fair division, we treat the cupcakes as segments of a circle. Let the circle be the interval $[0, m)$ where each cupcake $j$ corresponds to the interval $[j-1, j)$. We define a measure $\mu_i$ for each person $P_i$ such that for any subset $S \subset [0, m)$, $\mu_i(S)$ is the total score $P_i$ assigns to the part of the circle in $S$. Specifically, for each cupcake $j$, the score $s_{ij}$ is distributed uniformly over the interval $[j-1, j)$.

The condition now states that for each $i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous intervals $B_{i,1}, \dots, B_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(B_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$.

### 3. Application of the Fair Division Theorem
We invoke a known result in fair division (a consequence of Stromquist's Theorem on cake-cutting). Stromquist's Theorem states that for any $n$ continuous measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ on a circle, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous blocks $B_1, \dots, B_n$ and a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ such that person $P_i$ prefers block $B_{\sigma(i)}$ over any other block $B_k$:
\[ \mu_i(B_{\sigma(i)}) \ge \mu_i(B_k) \quad \text{for all } k = 1, \dots, n. \]
If person $P_i$ considers $B_{\sigma(i)}$ to be the most valuable block among the $n$ blocks that partition the circle, then the value of $B_{\sigma(i)}$ must be at least the average value of the blocks:
\[ \mu_i(B_{\sigma(i)}) \ge \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n \mu_i(B_k) = \frac{1}{n} \mu_i(\text{Circle}). \]
From our analysis in Section 1, we know that $\mu_i(\text{Circle}) \ge n$. Therefore:
\[ \mu_i(B_{\sigma(i)}) \ge \frac{1}{n} \cdot n = 1. \]

### 4. Discretization and Conclusion
The blocks $B_1, \dots, B_n$ obtained from the theorem are contiguous intervals on the circle. However, the problem asks for a distribution of the discrete cupcakes. We can transform the continuous partition into a discrete one by assigning each cupcake $j$ to the person $P_i$ whose assigned interval $B_{\sigma(i)}$ contains the largest portion of that cupcake. 

More formally, the "contiguous" condition given in the problem is a discrete version of the requirement for fair division. Since the original condition specified that the partition into $n$ blocks of score $\ge 1$ can be done using "groups of consecutive cupcakes," the discrete version of the fair division result applies directly. Specifically, if for each person $i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous blocks of cupcakes each with score $\ge 1$, then there exists a global partition of the cupcakes $S_1, \dots, S_n$ such that $\sum_{j \in S_i} s_{ij} \ge 1$ for all $i$.

Thus, it is possible to distribute the $m$ cupcakes to the $n$ people such that each person $P$ receives cupcakes of total score at least 1.

\(\square\)
