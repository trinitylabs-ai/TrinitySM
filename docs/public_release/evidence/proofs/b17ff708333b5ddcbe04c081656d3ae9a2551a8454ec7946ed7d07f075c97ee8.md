To prove that there exists a positive integer $N$ such that for every odd integer $n > N$, the digits in the base-$2n$ representation of $n^k$ are all greater than $d$, we analyze the digits of $n^k$ in base $2n$.

Let the base-$2n$ representation of $n^k$ be
$$n^k = a_m(2n)^m + a_{m-1}(2n)^{m-1} + \dots + a_1(2n) + a_0$$
where $0 \le a_i < 2n$ for all $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, m\}$.

**1. Determining the first digit $a_0$**
The digit $a_0$ is given by $a_0 = n^k \pmod{2n}$. Since $n$ is a divisor of $n^k$, $a_0$ must be a multiple of $n$. In the range $0 \le a_0 < 2n$, the only multiples of $n$ are $0$ and $n$. Since $n$ is odd, $n^k$ is odd, while $2n$ is even. Thus, $n^k$ cannot be divisible by $2n$, so $a_0 \neq 0$. Therefore, $a_0 = n$. For any $n > d$, we have $a_0 > d$.

**2. Determining the subsequent digits $a_i$**
We define a sequence $Y_i$ such that $Y_1 = \frac{n^k - a_0}{2n} = \frac{n^k - n}{2n} = \frac{n^{k-1}-1}{2}$. The digits for $i \ge 1$ are given by $a_i = Y_i \pmod{2n}$, and the sequence continues as $Y_{i+1} = \lfloor \frac{Y_i}{2n} \rfloor$.
By iteration, we find that $Y_i = \lfloor \frac{Y_1}{(2n)^{i-1}} \rfloor = \lfloor \frac{n^{k-1}-1}{2(2n)^{i-1}} \rfloor$.
For $i \ge k$, we have $2(2n)^{i-1} \ge 2(2n)^{k-1} = 2^k n^{k-1} > n^{k-1}-1$, which implies $Y_i = 0$. Thus, the leading digit index $m$ is at most $k-1$.
For $1 \le i < k$, we evaluate $a_i = Y_i - 2n \lfloor \frac{Y_i}{2n} \rfloor$.
Note that $Y_i = \lfloor \frac{n^{k-i}}{2^i} - \frac{1}{2^i n^{i-1}} \rfloor$. Let $n = 2^i q + r$ with $r \in \{1, 3, \dots, 2^i-1\}$. Then $\frac{n^{k-i}}{2^i} = \text{Integer} + \frac{r^{k-i}}{2^i}$. Since $r$ is odd, $r^{k-i}$ is odd, so $\frac{r^{k-i}}{2^i}$ is not an integer. For sufficiently large $n$, the term $\frac{1}{2^i n^{i-1}}$ is smaller than the distance from $\frac{r^{k-i}}{2^i}$ to the nearest integer, so $Y_i = \frac{n^{k-i}-r^{k-i}}{2^i} + \lfloor \frac{r^{k-i}}{2^i} \rfloor$.

Now consider $\frac{Y_i}{2n} = \frac{n^{k-i-1}}{2^{i+1}} - \frac{r^{k-i}}{2^{i+1}n} + \frac{\lfloor r^{k-i}/2^i \rfloor}{2n}$.
Let $n = 2^{i+1}m + r'$ with $r' \in \{1, 3, \dots, 2^{i+1}-1\}$. Then $\frac{n^{k-i-1}}{2^{i+1}} = \text{Integer} + \frac{(r')^{k-i-1}}{2^{i+1}}$. For sufficiently large $n$, the terms $\frac{r^{k-i}}{2^{i+1}n}$ and $\frac{\lfloor r^{k-i}/2^i \rfloor}{2n}$ are small enough that they do not change the floor of the expression. Thus, $\lfloor \frac{Y_i}{2n} \rfloor = \text{Integer} + \lfloor \frac{(r')^{k-i-1}}{2^{i+1}} \rfloor$.
Substituting these into the formula for $a_i$:
$$a_i = Y_i - 2n \lfloor \frac{Y_i}{2n} \rfloor = n \left( \frac{(r')^{k-i-1}}{2^i} - 2 \lfloor \frac{(r')^{k-i-1}}{2^{i+1}} \rfloor \right) + \left( \lfloor \frac{r^{k-i}}{2^i} \rfloor - \frac{r^{k-i}}{2^i} \right)$$
The term in the first parenthesis is $\frac{\rho_i}{2^i}$, where $\rho_i = (r')^{k-i-1} \pmod{2^{i+1}}$. Since $r'$ is odd, $\rho_i \in \{1, 3, \dots, 2^{i+1}-1\}$, so $\rho_i \ge 1$. The second parenthesis is a value in $(-1, 0)$.
Thus, $a_i \ge \frac{n}{2^i} - 1$ for all $1 \le i < k$.

**3. Conclusion**
For any $1 \le i \le k-1$, $a_i \ge \frac{n}{2^{k-1}} - 1$. To ensure $a_i > d$ for all $i \in \{0, \dots, k-1\}$, we require:
1. $n > d$ (for $a_0$)
2. $n > 2^{k-1}(d+1)$ (for $a_i, i \ge 1$)
3. $n$ large enough such that the floor approximations used in Section 2 are valid.

Let $N$ be the maximum of these bounds. For any odd $n > N$, $a_0 = n > d$ and $a_i \ge \frac{n}{2^i} - 1 > d$ for $1 \le i \le m$. Since $a_{k-1} = \lfloor \frac{n}{2^{k-1}} - \frac{1}{2^{k-1}n^{k-2}} \rfloor \ge 1$ for $n > 2^{k-1}$, the leading digit is $a_{k-1}$ (or some $a_i$ with $i < k-1$), and all digits are greater than $d$. \(\square\)
