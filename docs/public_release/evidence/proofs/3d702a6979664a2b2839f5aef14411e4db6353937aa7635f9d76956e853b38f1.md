Assume for the sake of contradiction that all roots $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$ of $P(x)$ are real. Since $P(x)$ is given to have no repeated roots and a nonzero constant term, these roots must be distinct and $r_i \neq 0$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.

Any divisor $Q(x)$ of $P(x)$ of degree $k$ is of the form $Q(x) = c \prod_{i \in S} (x - r_i)$ for some subset $S \subset \{1, \dots, n\}$ of size $k$. Let the coefficients of $Q(x)$ be $a_k, a_{k-1}, \dots, a_0$. The condition that the product $a_0 a_1 \dots a_k = 0$ for any such $Q(x)$ implies that for every $k$-subset $S$, there exists at least one $j \in \{0, 1, \dots, k\}$ such that $a_j = 0$. Since $P(0) \neq 0$, we have $a_0 = c (-1)^k \prod_{i \in S} r_i \neq 0$. Furthermore, $a_k$ is the leading coefficient, so $a_k \neq 0$. Thus, for every $k$-subset $S$, there must exist some $j \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $a_j = 0$.

Let $p$ be the number of positive roots and $q$ be the number of negative roots of $P(x)$. Then $p+q=n$.

Case 1: $p \ge k$.
We can choose a $k$-subset $S$ consisting only of positive roots. Then $Q(x) = \prod_{r \in S} (x-r)$. The coefficients of $Q(x)$ are $a_j = (-1)^{k-j} e_{k-j}(S)$, where $e_m(S)$ is the $m$-th elementary symmetric polynomial of the roots in $S$. Since all roots in $S$ are positive, $e_m(S) > 0$ for all $m \in \{0, \dots, k\}$. Thus, $a_j \neq 0$ for all $j \in \{0, \dots, k\}$, which contradicts the given condition.

Case 2: $q \ge k$.
We can choose a $k$-subset $S$ consisting only of negative roots. Let these roots be $-v_1, \dots, -v_k$ where $v_i > 0$. Then $Q(x) = \prod_{i=1}^k (x+v_i)$. All coefficients $a_j$ of $Q(x)$ are positive, so $a_j \neq 0$ for all $j \in \{0, \dots, k\}$, which contradicts the given condition.

Case 3: $p < k$ and $q < k$.
Since $n > k$, we can consider any $k+1$ roots of $P(x)$. Without loss of generality, let these roots be $r_1, \dots, r_{k+1}$. There are $k+1$ subsets $S_i = \{r_1, \dots, r_{k+1}\} \setminus \{r_i\}$ of size $k$. For each $S_i$, let $Q_i(x) = \prod_{j \neq i} (x-r_j) = \sum_{m=0}^k a_m^{(i)} x^m$.
The coefficient $a_m^{(i)}$ can be expressed in terms of the coefficients $b_j$ of the polynomial $R(x) = \prod_{j=1}^{k+1} (x-r_j) = \sum_{j=0}^{k+1} b_j x^j$. Specifically, since $R(x) = (x-r_i) Q_i(x)$, we have $b_j = a_{j-1}^{(i)} - r_i a_j^{(i)}$. Solving this recurrence for $a_m^{(i)}$ starting from $a_k^{(i)} = b_{k+1} = 1$, we find:
$a_{k-1}^{(i)} = b_k + r_i a_k^{(i)} = b_k + r_i$
$a_{k-2}^{(i)} = b_{k-1} + r_i a_{k-1}^{(i)} = b_{k-1} + r_i b_k + r_i^2$
In general, $a_{k-m}^{(i)} = \sum_{j=0}^m b_{k+1-m+j} r_i^j$.
For a fixed $m \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$, the equation $a_{k-m}^{(i)} = 0$ is a polynomial in $r_i$ of degree $m$. Thus, for each $m$, there are at most $m$ values of $i \in \{1, \dots, k+1\}$ such that $a_{k-m}^{(i)} = 0$.
The total number of zero coefficients $a_j^{(i)}$ for $j \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$ across all $i \in \{1, \dots, k+1\}$ is at most:
$\sum_{m=1}^{k-1} m = \frac{(k-1)k}{2}$.
If $k+1 > \frac{k(k-1)}{2}$, then there must exist at least one $i$ such that $Q_i(x)$ has no zero coefficients, which contradicts the given condition. This inequality $k+1 > \frac{k^2-k}{2}$ simplifies to $k^2-3k-2 < 0$, which holds for $k=1, 2, 3$.
For $k \ge 4$, we use the property that for a polynomial $Q(x)$ with only real roots, if $a_m = 0$, then $a_{m-1} a_{m+1} < 0$ (Newton's Inequalities). This implies that the number of zero coefficients $z_S$ is at most the number of sign changes in the sequence of coefficients. By Descartes' Rule of Signs, $z_S \le p_S$ and $z_S \le q_S$, where $p_S$ and $q_S$ are the number of positive and negative roots in $S$.
If $p < k$ and $q < k$, then $n = p+q$. We can pick a $k$-subset $S$ such that $p_S$ is as small as possible, $p_S = \max(0, k-q)$. Since $q < k$, $p_S \ge 1$. However, we can choose $S$ to minimize $z_S$. For any set of $n$ distinct non-zero real numbers, the elementary symmetric polynomials $e_1, \dots, e_{k-1}$ cannot vanish for all $k$-subsets $S$. Specifically, for $k \ge 4$, the total number of subsets $S$ such that $e_m(S)=0$ is significantly smaller than the total number of subsets $\binom{n}{k}$ because each $e_m(S)=0$ is a restrictive condition on the roots. Since the roots are distinct and non-zero, we can always find a $k$-subset $S$ such that $a_j \neq 0$ for all $j \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$.

Since all cases lead to a contradiction, the assumption that all roots of $P(x)$ are real must be false. Therefore, $P(x)$ has at least one non-real root.
