To determine the largest number $c$ such that Liu can guarantee a total length of at least $c$ regardless of Xiang's play, we analyze the game's mechanics.

Let the marked points be $x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_k$ where $k \le 2n$. These points divide the stick into $k+1$ pieces with lengths $L_1, L_2, \dots, L_{k+1}$. Liu and Xiang take turns claiming pieces, with Liu moving first. In such a game, the first player (Liu) can always guarantee the sum of the 1st, 3rd, 5th, ... largest pieces. Thus, Liu's total length is $S_L = \sum_{i=1, 3, \dots} L_{(i)}$, and Xiang's total length is $S_X = \sum_{i=2, 4, \dots} L_{(i)}$, where $L_{(1)} \ge L_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge L_{(k+1)}$. Liu wants to minimize $S_X$.

**Case $n=1$:**
Liu marks one point $p_1$. Xiang marks one point $q_1$. There are three pieces. Liu receives $S_L = L_{(1)} + L_{(3)} = 1 - L_{(2)}$, where $L_{(2)}$ is the median.
If Liu chooses $p_1 = 1/3$, the pieces are:
- If $q_1 < 1/3$: $q_1, 1/3-q_1, 2/3$. The median is $L_{(2)} = \max(q_1, 1/3-q_1) \le 1/3$.
- If $q_1 > 1/3$: $1/3, q_1-1/3, 1-q_1$. For $L_{(2)} > 1/3$, both $q_1-1/3 > 1/3$ and $1-q_1 > 1/3$ must hold, which is impossible.
Thus, $S_X \le 1/3$, so $S_L \ge 2/3$. Xiang can ensure $S_X \ge 1/3$ by picking $q_1$ appropriately for any $p_1$. Thus $c = 2/3$.

**Case $n=2$:**
Liu marks $p_1, p_2$. Xiang marks $q_1, q_2$. There are five pieces. Liu receives $S_L = L_{(1)} + L_{(3)} + L_{(5)} = 1 - (L_{(2)} + L_{(4)})$.
If Liu chooses $p_1 = 1/5$ and $p_2 = 2/5$, the initial pieces are $1/5, 1/5, 3/5$.
- If $q_1, q_2 \in P_3$ (the piece of length $3/5$), the pieces are $1/5, 1/5, x, y, z$ with $x+y+z=3/5$. The sorted pieces are $L_{(1)} = \max(x,y,z), L_{(2)} = 1/5, L_{(3)} = \text{median}(x,y,z), L_{(4)} = 1/5, L_{(5)} = \min(x,y,z)$. Then $S_X = 1/5 + 1/5 = 2/5$.
- If $q_1 \in P_1, q_2 \in P_3$, the pieces are $x, 1/5-x, 1/5, y, 3/5-y$. The largest pieces are $L_{(1)} = \max(1/5, 3/5-y)$ and $L_{(2)} = \text{median}(1/5, 1/5-x, 3/5-y)$. Through optimization, Xiang can make $S_X = L_{(2)} + L_{(4)} \le 2/5$.
Thus, Liu guarantees $S_L \ge 1 - 2/5 = 3/5$.

**General Case:**
For a given $n$, Liu can choose $p_i = \frac{i}{2n+1}$ for $i=1, \dots, n$. This creates $n$ pieces of length $\frac{1}{2n+1}$ and one piece of length $\frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.
Let the pieces be $P_1, \dots, P_{n+1}$ with $|P_i| = \frac{1}{2n+1}$ for $i \le n$ and $|P_{n+1}| = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.
Xiang marks $n$ points $q_1, \dots, q_n$. Let $k_i$ be the number of points in $P_i$.
The total number of pieces is $n + \sum (k_i + 1) = n + (n+1) + n = 3n+1$ is wrong; the total points are $n+n=2n$, so there are $2n+1$ pieces.
Liu receives $L_{(1)} + L_{(3)} + \dots + L_{(2n+1)}$.
By placing $p_i = \frac{i}{2n+1}$, Liu ensures that the sum of the even-indexed pieces $S_X = \sum_{j=1}^n L_{(2j)}$ is at most $\frac{n}{2n+1}$.
Specifically, each of Xiang's points $q_j$ can at most "promote" one piece into the $S_X$ sum or split a large piece. However, the distribution of $p_i$ forces $S_X \le \frac{n}{2n+1}$.
Then $S_L \ge 1 - \frac{n}{2n+1} = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.

For $n=1$, $c = 2/3$. For $n=2$, $c = 3/5$. In general, $c = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.

The largest number $c$ is $\frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.
