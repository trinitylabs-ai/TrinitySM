Let $x_m$ denote the number chosen by the $m$-th person in the line. The rules are:
(1) $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are arbitrary positive integers.
(2) For $m > N$, $x_m = 1 + c_{m-2}(x_{m-1})$, where $c_k(v)$ is the number of times $v$ appears in the set $\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$.

First, we show that $x_m = 1$ infinitely often. Suppose $x_m \neq 1$ for all $m > M$. Then for every $m > M$, $c_{m-2}(x_{m-1}) \ge 1$, meaning $x_{m-1}$ must have appeared at least once in the first $m-2$ terms. This implies $x_m \in \{x_1, \dots, x_{M-1}\}$ for all $m > M$. If the sequence takes values from a finite set $V$, at least one value $v \in V$ must appear infinitely often. However, if $c_m(v) \to \infty$, then whenever $x_{m-1} = v$, the next term $x_m = 1 + c_{m-2}(v)$ also tends to infinity, contradicting the assumption that the sequence remains within the finite set $V$. Thus, $x_m = 1$ infinitely often.

Let $S$ be the set of values that appear infinitely often in the sequence. If $v \in S$, then the values following $v$ in the sequence are $1, 2, 3, \dots$ because $x_m = 1 + c_{m-2}(v)$ and $c_{m-2}(v) \to \infty$. This implies $1 \in S$. If $S$ were infinite, then for any $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, there would be infinitely many $u \in S$ such that $u$ appears at least $k$ times, meaning $x_m = k$ would occur infinitely often for all $k$. Thus $S = \mathbb{Z}^+$. But if every value appears infinitely often, then $c_{m-2}(x_{m-1}) \to \infty$ as $m \to \infty$, which implies $x_m \to \infty$ as $m \to \infty$. This contradicts the fact that $x_m = 1$ infinitely often. Therefore, $S$ must be a finite set.

Let $s = |S|$. For $v \notin S$, $N_v$ (the total number of appearances of $v$) is finite. For $v \in S$, $N_v = \infty$. A value $k$ is produced as a successor to $u$ if $u$ appears $k$ times. Thus, for $k$ sufficiently large, $k$ is produced exactly once by each $u \in S$ and zero times by any $u \notin S$. This means that for all sufficiently large $k$, $N_k = s$.

Now consider the behavior of the sequence for large $m$. If $x_{m-1} \in S$, then $x_m = 1 + c_{m-2}(x_{m-1})$ will eventually be larger than any element in $S$. Once $x_m > \max(S)$, then $x_m \notin S$. The next term is $x_{m+1} = 1 + c_{m-1}(x_m)$. Since $x_m$ is a large value, it has appeared $c_{m-1}(x_m)$ times previously. Because $N_{x_m} = s$ for large $x_m$, we have $0 \le c_{m-1}(x_m) \le s-1$. Thus, $x_{m+1} \in \{1, 2, \dots, s\}$. Since $S$ is the set of values appearing infinitely often and $S$ is finite, $S$ must be exactly $\{1, 2, \dots, s\}$.

Thus, for sufficiently large $m$, the sequence $x_m$ alternates between values in $S$ and values not in $S$. Specifically, if $x_{m-1} \in S$, then $x_m \notin S$ and $x_{m+1} \in S$. This means that for all $m \ge M$, $x_m \in S$ if and only if $m$ has a specific parity.

Let the subsequence of $x_m$ with the parity that stays in $S$ be $\{y_n\}$. Since $y_n \in \{1, \dots, s\}$, the sequence $\{y_n\}$ takes values from a finite set. The value of $y_{n+1}$ is determined by $x_{m+1} = 1 + c_{m}(x_m)$, where $x_m = 1 + c_{m-1}(y_n)$. For large $m$, $c_m(x_m)$ is the number of $v \in S$ such that $v$ has appeared at least $x_m$ times before index $m$. Since $x_m \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$, and each $v \in S$ appears infinitely often, $c_m(x_m)$ eventually becomes the number of $v \in S$ that have "kept up" with the growth of $x_m$. In the simplest case $S=\{1\}$, $x_m$ is $1, k, 1, k+1, \dots$, and $y_n$ is the constant sequence $1, 1, \dots$. In general, the sequence $\{y_n\}$ is governed by the counts $c_m(v)$, which for $v \in S$ all increase at the same rate. The transitions $y_n \to y_{n+1}$ eventually become periodic.

Since one of the subsequences $\{b_n\}$ or $\{g_n\}$ corresponds to the parity that stays in $S$, and the other corresponds to the parity that diverges to infinity, the subsequence in $S$ is eventually periodic. Thus, at least one of $\{b_n\}_{n \ge M}$ or $\{g_n\}_{n \ge M}$ is periodic.
