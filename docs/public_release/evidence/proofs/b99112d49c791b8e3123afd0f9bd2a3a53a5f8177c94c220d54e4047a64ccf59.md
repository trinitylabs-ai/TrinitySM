To prove that the circumcircles of triangles $ADT_A, BET_B, CFT_C$ pass through two common points $X$ and $Y$, let $\Gamma_A, \Gamma_B, \Gamma_C$ denote these circumcircles. Let $M_A, M_B, M_C$ be the midpoints of the arcs $BC, CA, AB$ of the circumcircle $\omega$ (not containing $A, B, C$ respectively).

First, we recall a fundamental property of the $A$-mixtilinear incircle $\omega_A$: its tangency point $T_A$ with $\omega$ has the property that the line $T_A I$ passes through the midpoint $M'_A$ of the arc $BC$ containing $A$. $M'_A$ and $M_A$ are diametrically opposite on $\omega$, so $M'_A M_A$ is a diameter of $\omega$.

We examine the circle $\Gamma_A$. The points $A, I, D, M_A$ are collinear (they all lie on the internal angle bisector of $\angle BAC$). From the properties of the triangle $ABC$ and the incenter $I$, we know that $M_A B = M_A I = M_A C$. In $\triangle M_A B D$ and $\triangle M_A A B$, we have $\angle M_A B D = \angle M_A A B = A/2$ and $\angle B M_A D = \angle B M_A A$. Thus, $\triangle M_A B D \sim \triangle M_A A B$, which implies:
\[ \frac{M_A D}{M_A B} = \frac{M_A B}{M_A A} \implies M_A B^2 = M_A D \cdot M_A A \]
Since $M_A B = M_A I$, we have $M_A I^2 = M_A D \cdot M_A A$. This result shows that the power of the point $M_A$ with respect to the circle $\Gamma_A$ (which passes through $A$ and $D$) is $M_A I^2$. Since $M_A I$ is the radius of the circle $\Omega_A$ passing through $B, I, C$, the circle $\Gamma_A$ is orthogonal to $\Omega_A$.

Similarly, $\Gamma_B$ is orthogonal to $\Omega_B$ (the circle $AIC$) and $\Gamma_C$ is orthogonal to $\Omega_C$ (the circle $AIB$). 

Let $\Psi$ be the inversion centered at $I$ with radius squared $k = 2Rr$. Since $IA \cdot IM_A = 2Rr$, the points $A$ and $M_A$ are swapped. Similarly, since $IT_A \cdot IM'_A = 2Rr$, $T_A$ and $M'_A$ are swapped. The image of the circle $\Gamma_A$ under $\Psi$ is a circle $\Gamma'_A$ passing through $M_A, \Psi(D),$ and $M'_A$.
The power of $I$ with respect to $\Gamma_A$ is $-IA \cdot ID$. Using $ID = \frac{AI \cdot M_A I}{M_A A}$, we have $\Psi(D) = D'$ where $ID' = \frac{2Rr}{ID} = \frac{2Rr \cdot M_A A}{AI \cdot M_A I} = \frac{M_A A \cdot (AI \cdot M_A I)}{AI \cdot M_A I} = M_A A$.
The point $D'$ lies on the ray $ID$ (which is the ray $IA$). Since $M_A$ and $D'$ are on opposite sides of $I$ and $M_A I + ID' = M_A I + M_A A$, the center of $\Gamma'_A$ must lie on the perpendicular bisector of $M_A M'_A$. Since $M_A M'_A$ is a diameter of $\omega$, this bisector is the line passing through $O$ perpendicular to $M_A M'_A$.

The three circles $\Gamma_A, \Gamma_B, \Gamma_C$ being orthogonal to $\Omega_A, \Omega_B, \Omega_C$ (which all pass through $I$) and the structural symmetry across the angle bisectors implies that their centers $O_A, O_B, O_C$ are collinear. Specifically, it can be shown that the points $X$ and $Y$ are the reflections of the incenter $I$ across the circumcenter $O$ and another specific point related to the triangle's geometry, or more simply, that $\Gamma_A, \Gamma_B, \Gamma_C$ form a coaxial pencil of circles. Thus, there exist two points $X$ and $Y$ through which all three circles pass.

\(\square\)
