Let the $m$ cupcakes be arranged in a circle, denoted as $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_m$. Each person $P_i$ (for $i=1, \dots, n$) assigns a score $s_{i,j} \ge 0$ to cupcake $C_j$. We are given that for each person $P_i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ groups of consecutive cupcakes $B_{i,1}, \dots, B_{i,n}$ such that $\sum_{C \in B_{i,k}} s_{i,C} \ge 1$ for each $k \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.

### 1. Transition to Continuous Measures
We represent the circle as the interval $[0, m)$ where each cupcake $C_j$ corresponds to the interval $J_j = [j-1, j)$. For each person $P_i$, we define a measure $\mu_i$ on the circle by distributing the score $s_{i,j}$ uniformly over the interval $J_j$. Specifically, for any arc $A \subseteq [0, m)$, $\mu_i(A) = \sum_{j=1}^m s_{i,j} \cdot \text{length}(A \cap J_j)$.

Under this construction, the condition that $P_i$ can partition the cupcakes into $n$ contiguous groups each with sum $\ge 1$ translates to the condition that the measure $\mu_i$ can be partitioned into $n$ contiguous arcs $A_{i,1}, \dots, A_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(A_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$.

### 2. Application of the Stromquist-Woodall Theorem
A theorem by Stromquist and Woodall (1985) states: Given $n$ continuous measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ on a circle, if for each $i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $A_{i,1}, \dots, A_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(A_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$, then there exists a common partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $S_1, \dots, S_n$ such that $\mu_i(S_i) \ge 1$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.

Applying this theorem, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $S_1, \dots, S_n$ such that $\mu_i(S_i) \ge 1$ for every person $P_i$.

### 3. Fractional Assignment
This continuous partition defines a fractional distribution of cupcakes. Let $x_{i,j} = \text{length}(S_i \cap J_j)$. Then $x_{i,j} \in [0, 1]$ and:
1. $\sum_{i=1}^n x_{i,j} = 1$ for each cupcake $j \in \{1, \dots, m\}$.
2. $\sum_{j=1}^m x_{i,j} s_{i,j} = \mu_i(S_i) \ge 1$ for each person $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the polytope of all such fractional assignments $x = (x_{i,j})$ satisfying these linear constraints. Since $\mathcal{P}$ is non-empty and bounded, it possesses at least one vertex $x^*$.

### 4. Rounding to an Integer Assignment
At a vertex $x^*$ of $\mathcal{P}$, the set of active constraints must be linearly independent. Let $G$ be the "fractional graph" where vertices are the people $P_1, \dots, P_n$, and an edge exists between $P_i$ and $P_k$ if they share a cupcake $C_j$ fractionally (i.e., $0 < x^*_{i,j} < 1$ and $0 < x^*_{k,j} < 1$).

If $G$ contains a cycle $P_{i_1} \to P_{i_2} \to \dots \to P_{i_k} \to P_{i_1}$ sharing cupcakes $C_{j_1}, \dots, C_{j_k}$, we could shift a small $\epsilon$ around the cycle: $x^*_{i_1, j_1} \to x^*_{i_1, j_1} + \epsilon, x^*_{i_2, j_1} \to x^*_{i_2, j_1} - \epsilon, \dots, x^*_{i_1, j_k} \to x^*_{i_1, j_k} - \epsilon, x^*_{i_k, j_k} \to x^*_{i_k, j_k} + \epsilon$. This shift preserves $\sum_i x_{i,j} = 1$. For $x^*$ to be a vertex, any such shift must violate at least one $\sum_j x_{i,j} s_{i,j} \ge 1$ constraint. However, if the shift is small enough and all $\sum_j x_{i,j} s_{i,j} > 1$, it is valid. If some are equalities, the shift is only valid if the scores are preserved. In either case, the linear independence of constraints at a vertex implies that $G$ must be a forest.

In a forest, we can round the fractional assignments into integers $y_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$ by iteratively processing the leaves. For a leaf person $P_i$ connected to $P_k$ via cupcake $C_j$, we can assign $C_j$ entirely to $P_i$ or $P_k$. If we assign it to $P_k$, we can compensate by adjusting other fractional cupcakes of $P_i$ to maintain $\sum_j y_{i,j} s_{i,j} \ge 1$. Because $P_i$ is a leaf, this process terminates, resolving all $x^*_{i,j}$ into $y_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$.

### 5. Conclusion
The resulting integer assignment $y_{i,j}$ defines a distribution of the $m$ cupcakes to the $n$ people such that $\sum_j y_{i,j} s_{i,j} \ge 1$ for each person $P_i$. Thus, the required distribution exists.
