To solve the problem, we analyze the operation performed by Confucius and its effect on the prime factorizations of the integers on the blackboard.

### 1. Termination of the Process
Let the integers on the blackboard be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{2026}$. A move consists of choosing two integers $m, n > 1$ and replacing them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $k = \text{lcm}(m, n)/g$.
Let $P = \prod_{i=1}^{2026} x_i$ be the product of all integers on the board and let $n_{>1}$ be the number of integers greater than 1.
In each move, the product of the two chosen numbers $m \cdot n$ is replaced by $g \cdot k = \gcd(m, n) \cdot \frac{\text{lcm}(m, n)}{\gcd(m, n)} = \text{lcm}(m, n)$.
Since $\text{lcm}(m, n) = \frac{mn}{\gcd(m, n)}$, the new product of all integers is $P_{new} = \frac{P}{\gcd(m, n)}$.
- If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then $P$ strictly decreases.
- If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $P$ remains constant, but the replacement is $(m, n) \to (1, mn)$. This strictly decreases $n_{>1}$ by 1.

Since $P$ is a positive integer and $n_{>1}$ is a non-negative integer, the pair $(P, n_{>1})$ decreases lexicographically in every move. Thus, the process must terminate in finitely many moves. The process stops when no two integers on the board are greater than 1, meaning exactly one or zero integers are greater than 1.

### 2. The Invariant and the Value of $M$
For any prime $p$, let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $x$. For a move $(m, n) \to (g, k)$, the exponents $a = v_p(m)$ and $b = v_p(n)$ are replaced by:
$$v_p(g) = \min(a, b), \quad v_p(k) = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(\gcd(m, n)) = \max(a, b) - \min(a, b) = |a - b|$$
We observe that the greatest common divisor of the exponents $\gcd(a, b)$ is invariant under this transformation:
- If $a=0, b=0$, then $\gcd(0, 0) = 0$ and $\gcd(0, 0) = 0$.
- If $a>0, b=0$, then $\gcd(a, 0) = a$ and $\gcd(0, a) = a$.
- If $a, b > 0$, then $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(\min(a, b), |a-b|)$ is a property of the subtraction-based Euclidean algorithm.

Thus, for any prime $p$, the value $g_p = \gcd(v_p(x_1), v_p(x_2), \dots, v_p(x_{2026}))$ is invariant throughout the process. At termination, only one integer $M$ can be greater than 1. For every prime $p$, only $v_p(M)$ can be non-zero, so we must have $v_p(M) = g_p$.
This implies $M = \prod_p p^{g_p}$, which depends only on the initial values.

### 3. Proof that $M > 1$
Since each $x_i > 1$, there exists at least one prime $q$ such that $v_q(x_j) > 0$ for some $j$. Then $g_q = \gcd(v_q(x_1), \dots, v_q(x_{2026}))$. Since $\gcd(a, 0) = a$ and $\gcd(a, b) > 0$ whenever $a$ or $b$ is positive, $g_q > 0$ as long as at least one $v_q(x_i) > 0$. Thus, $M$ must be greater than 1.

(1) We have proved that the process terminates and that exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains.
(2) We have proved that $M = \prod_p p^{\gcd(v_p(x_1), \dots, v_p(x_{2026}))}$, which is independent of the choices made.
