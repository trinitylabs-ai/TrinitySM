To find the smallest possible value of $c$ among all positive integers $n$ such that $n \equiv 6 \pmod 7$ and all divisors $d$ of $n$, we analyze the given expression.

Let $n$ be a positive integer such that $n \equiv 6 \pmod 7$, and let $d$ be a divisor of $n$. Let $k = n/d$. The expression is
\[ E = \left(d + \frac{n}{d}\right)^2 = (d+k)^2 = d^2 + 2dk + k^2 = d^2 + 2n + k^2. \]
The remainder when $E$ is divided by $n$ is the same as the remainder when $d^2 + k^2$ is divided by $n$. Let this remainder be $r$. By the definition of $c$, we have $r = n-c$. Thus,
\[ d^2 + k^2 \equiv n-c \equiv -c \pmod n. \]
This implies that there exists an integer $q$ such that $d^2 + k^2 = qn - c$. Substituting $n = dk$, we get the equation:
\[ d^2 + k^2 = qdk - c \implies k^2 - qdk + d^2 + c = 0. \]
For $k$ to be an integer, the discriminant of this quadratic in $k$ must be a perfect square:
\[ \Delta = (qd)^2 - 4(d^2 + c) = (q^2 - 4)d^2 - 4c = m^2 \]
for some non-negative integer $m$. We also have the constraint $n = dk \equiv 6 \pmod 7$. Modulo 7, the equation $d^2 + k^2 = qn - c$ becomes:
\[ d^2 + k^2 \equiv 6q - c \equiv -q - c \pmod 7. \]
Since $dk \equiv 6 \equiv -1 \pmod 7$, we have $k \equiv -d^{-1} \pmod 7$. Thus, $d^2 + k^2 \equiv d^2 + d^{-2} \pmod 7$. For $d \in \{1, \dots, 6\} \pmod 7$, the possible values of $d^2 + d^{-2} \pmod 7$ are:
- If $d \equiv 1, 6 \pmod 7$, then $d^2 \equiv 1$ and $d^2 + d^{-2} \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod 7$.
- If $d \equiv 2, 3, 4, 5 \pmod 7$, then $d^2 \equiv 4$ or $2$, and $d^2 + d^{-2} \equiv 4 + 2 = 6 \pmod 7$.
Thus, we must have:
1. $q + c \equiv -2 \equiv 5 \pmod 7$ if $d^2 + k^2 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$, or
2. $q + c \equiv -6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$ if $d^2 + k^2 \equiv 6 \pmod 7$.

We test small values of $c$:
- If $c=1$, then $q \equiv 4$ or $0 \pmod 7$. The condition $m^2 = (q^2-4)d^2 - 4$ implies $(m/2)^2 - (s^2-1)d^2 = -1$ if $q=2s$, which has no solutions because the period of the continued fraction of $\sqrt{s^2-1}$ is even. If $q$ is odd, $q$ must be a multiple of 3 to avoid $m^2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$. No small $n \equiv 6 \pmod 7$ are found.
- If $c=2$, then $q \equiv 3$ or $6 \pmod 7$. Testing $m^2 = (q^2-4)d^2 - 8$ yields no solutions for small $q$ and $d$ that satisfy $n \equiv 6 \pmod 7$.
- If $c=3$, then $q \equiv 2$ or $5 \pmod 7$. Let $q=5$. Then $m^2 = (25-4)d^2 - 12 = 21d^2 - 12$. For $d=4$, $m^2 = 21(16) - 12 = 336 - 12 = 324 = 18^2$.
The values for $k$ are $k = \frac{qd \pm m}{2} = \frac{5(4) \pm 18}{2}$, giving $k=19$ or $k=1$.
For $k=19$ and $d=4$, we have $n = dk = 76$.
Checking the condition: $76 = 10 \times 7 + 6 \equiv 6 \pmod 7$.
Calculating the remainder: $(4 + 19)^2 = 23^2 = 529$. Dividing 529 by 76:
$529 = 6 \times 76 + 73$.
The remainder is $73$, and $n-c = 76-3 = 73$. Thus $c=3$ is possible.

The smallest possible value of $c$ is 3.
