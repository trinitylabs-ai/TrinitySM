Let the positions of the cities be represented by complex numbers. Alice chooses points $P$ and $Q$ and a subset $S$ of the plane. This defines a set of complex numbers $W = \{ \frac{r-p}{q-p} : R \in S \}$, where $p, q, r$ are the complex numbers associated with $P, Q, R$. Let $X = W \cup \{1-w : w \in W\}$.

A road exists between cities $A$ and $B$ (represented by complex numbers $a$ and $b$) if and only if for every city $C$ (represented by $c$) distinct from $A$ and $B$, $\triangle PQR$ is directly similar to either $\triangle ABC$ or $\triangle BAC$ for some $R \in S$. This is equivalent to $\frac{c-a}{b-a} \in W$ or $\frac{c-b}{a-b} \in W$. Since $\frac{c-b}{a-b} = 1 - \frac{c-a}{b-a}$, the condition is $\frac{c-a}{b-a} \in X$. Thus, an edge $AB$ exists if and only if $V \setminus \{A, B\} \subseteq a + X(b-a)$, where $V$ is the set of all cities. Alice wins if the resulting graph $G = (V, E)$ is both connected and planar. Otherwise, Bob wins.

Let $Y = \mathbb{C} \setminus X$. We consider two cases based on the topological properties of $Y \setminus \mathbb{R}$.

Case 1: $Y \setminus \mathbb{R}$ is nowhere dense in $\mathbb{C}$.
Bob can ensure that $G$ is the complete graph $K_\infty$ by picking $V = \{v_1, v_2, \dots\}$ greedily. For $n=1, 2$, pick $v_1, v_2$ such that $|v_1 - v_2| > 1$. For $n \ge 3$, Bob picks $v_n$ such that:
(i) $|v_n - v_k| > 1$ for all $k < n$.
(ii) $v_n$ is not collinear with any two points in $\{v_1, \dots, v_{n-1}\}$.
(iii) For all $i, j < n$ with $i \neq j$, the ratio $\frac{v_n - v_i}{v_j - v_i} \in X$.

Condition (i) excludes a finite union of closed disks. Condition (ii) excludes a finite union of lines. For Condition (iii), $v_n$ must satisfy $v_n \in \bigcap_{i \neq j < n} (v_i + X(v_j-v_i))$, which is equivalent to $v_n \notin \bigcup_{i \neq j < n} (v_i + Y(v_j-v_i))$. Since $v_n$ is not collinear with $v_i$ and $v_j$, the ratio $\frac{v_n - v_i}{v_j - v_i}$ is non-real. Since $Y \setminus \mathbb{R}$ is nowhere dense, each set $(v_i + Y(v_j-v_i)) \setminus \text{Line}(v_i, v_j)$ is nowhere dense. The union of finitely many nowhere dense sets and lines cannot cover the plane, so a valid $v_n$ always exists. In this case, $G = K_\infty$, which is non-planar, so Bob wins.

Case 2: $Y \setminus \mathbb{R}$ is not nowhere dense.
If $Y \setminus \mathbb{R}$ is not nowhere dense, its closure contains an open set, so there exists an open disk $U \subseteq Y \setminus \mathbb{R}$ with radius $\epsilon > 0$. Bob can ensure that $G$ is an empty graph. He constructs $V$ in stages $V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \dots$.
Let $M = \max(1, 1/\sqrt{\epsilon})$. Pick $V_1 = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$ to be non-collinear points such that $|v_i - v_j| > M$ for all $i, j \in \{1, 2, 3\}$.
Given $V_r$, Bob constructs $V_{r+1}$ by adding a witness city $w_{ij}$ for every distinct pair $v_i, v_j \in V_r$. For each pair $(v_i, v_j)$, he picks $w_{ij} = v_i + y_{ij}(v_j - v_i)$ for some $y_{ij} \in U$. To ensure $w_{ij}$ is a valid city, he requires:
(i) $|w_{ij} - v_k| > M$ for all $v_k \in V_r$ and $|w_{ij} - w_{lm}| > M$ for all $(l, m) \neq (i, j)$.
(ii) $w_{ij}$ is not collinear with any two points already placed.

For a fixed $(i, j)$, the set of $y \in U$ violating (i) is a union of disks $D_k$ with radii $r_k = M/|v_j - v_i|$. The total area of these excluded disks is at most $N \pi (M/|v_j - v_i|)^2$, where $N$ is the number of points currently in $V$. Since $U$ is an open disk of radius $\epsilon$, its area is $\pi \epsilon^2$. Bob can ensure the excluded area is smaller than the area of $U$ by ensuring $|v_j - v_i|$ is sufficiently large. By picking the initial $V_1$ and subsequent $w_{ij}$ sufficiently far apart (which is possible since $U$ is open and the target region $v_i + U(v_j-v_i)$ has radius $\epsilon |v_j-v_i|$, which grows as $|v_j-v_i|$ increases), he can always find valid $y_{ij} \in U$.

By this construction, every pair of cities $(A, B)$ eventually belongs to some $V_r \times V_r$ and is assigned a witness $C \in V_{r+1}$ such that $\frac{c-a}{b-a} \in U \subseteq Y$. Thus, no roads exist in $G$. $G$ is an empty graph on infinitely many vertices, which is disconnected, so Bob wins.

In all cases, Bob has a winning strategy.
