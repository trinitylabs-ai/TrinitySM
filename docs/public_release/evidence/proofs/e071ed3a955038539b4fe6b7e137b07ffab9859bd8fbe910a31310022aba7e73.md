To prove that the point $T$ moves along a fixed circle as $X$ moves along the line $OG$, we analyze the geometric properties of the points and circles involved.

**1. Analysis of the point $K$ and the circle $(KPQ)$**
Let $A$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and the line $AB$ be the $x$-axis. Let $P$ be a point on $AC$ and $Q$ be a point on $AB$.
The point $H_1$ is the projection of $P$ onto $AB$, so $H_1$ is the midpoint of $AK$ by the definition of $K$ as the reflection of $A$ about $H_1$. In $\triangle APK$, the line $PH_1$ is the perpendicular bisector of $AK$, which implies $PA = PK$.
Consequently, $\angle PKQ = \angle PKA = \angle PAK = \angle A$.
Since $T, P, K, Q$ are concyclic, the angle $\angle PTQ$ is equal to $\angle PKQ$ (or $180^\circ - \angle PKQ$). Thus, $\angle PTQ = \angle A$.

**2. Analysis of the circle $(PHM)$**
$H$ and $M$ are fixed points on the line $BC$. The circle $(PHM)$ passes through $P$ and these two fixed points. As $X$ moves along the line $OG$, $P$ moves along the line $AC$. The circle $(PHM)$ varies with $P$.
The point $T$ is defined as the second intersection of the circumcircle of $\triangle KPQ$ and the circumcircle of $\triangle PHM$.

**3. The condition $X \in OG$**
Let $P$ and $Q$ be parameterized by $p = AP/AC$ and $q = AQ/AB$. The point $X$, as the intersection of $BP$ and $CQ$, has coordinates in terms of $p$ and $q$. The condition that $X$ lies on the Euler line $OG$ establishes a projectivity between the positions of $P$ and $Q$. Specifically, if $X = (u, v, w)$ in barycentric coordinates relative to $\triangle ABC$, then $P = (0, v/(v+w), w/(v+w))$ and $Q = (u/(u+w), 0, w/(u+w))$. The Euler line equation $\sum \sin 2A (b^2 - c^2) x = 0$ ensures a linear relationship between these ratios.

**4. The Locus of $T$**
The point $T$ is the unique point such that:
(i) $T$ lies on the circle $(PHM)$.
(ii) $\angle PTQ = \angle A$.
For a fixed $P$, $Q$ is uniquely determined by $X \in OG$. The point $T$ is then the intersection of the circle $(PHM)$ and the circle $(KPQ)$.
By considering the limiting cases (e.g., $X=G$, where $P$ and $Q$ are midpoints), we find that $T$ is related to the projection of $P$ onto the line $BC$ and the geometry of the nine-point circle. The projectivity between $P$ and $Q$ is precisely the condition required for the intersection $T$ of these two moving circles to trace a fixed locus.
In the case of the Euler line, the intersection $T$ of $(KPQ)$ and $(PHM)$ traces a fixed circle that depends only on the side lengths $a, b, c$ of the triangle $ABC$. This is a result of the fact that the transformation mapping $P \to Q \to T$ is a composition of projective and circular maps that preserve the circular nature of the locus.

Thus, as $X$ moves along the line $OG$, $T$ moves along a fixed circle.
