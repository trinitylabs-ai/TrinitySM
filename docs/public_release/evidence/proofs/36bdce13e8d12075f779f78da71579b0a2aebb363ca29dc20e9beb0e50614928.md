To find the largest number $c$ that Liu can guarantee, let us analyze the process for small values of $n$.

**Case $n=1$:**
Liu marks $k_L \le 1$ point, then Xiang marks $k_X \le 1$ point. If Liu marks 1 point at $x=1/3$, the stick is divided into $[0, 1/3]$ and $[1/3, 1]$. Xiang then marks a point $y$.
- If $y \in [0, 1/3]$, the pieces have lengths $y, 1/3-y, 2/3$. Sorted: $2/3, \max(y, 1/3-y), \min(y, 1/3-y)$. Liu takes the largest and smallest: $2/3 + \min(y, 1/3-y) \ge 2/3$.
- If $y \in [1/3, 1]$, the pieces have lengths $1/3, y-1/3, 1-y$. Sorted: $l_1, l_2, l_3$. Liu's total is $l_1+l_3 = 1-l_2$. To minimize this, Xiang maximizes $l_2$. Since $l_2 \le 1/3$ (because $l_1 \ge l_2 \ge l_3$), $l_2$ is maximized when $l_1=l_2=1/3$, giving $L = 2/3$.
Thus, for $n=1$, $c=2/3$.

**Case $n=2$:**
Liu marks 2 points, creating lengths $a, b, c$. Xiang marks 2 points. Total pieces: 5. Liu takes $l_1, l_3, l_5$, and Xiang takes $l_2, l_4$.
Liu wants to minimize $S = l_2+l_4$. If Liu chooses $a=b=1/4, c=1/2$, we analyze Xiang's strategies:
- Split $c=1/2$ into 3 pieces of $1/6$: Pieces are $1/4, 1/4, 1/6, 1/6, 1/6$. Sorted: $1/4, 1/4, 1/6, 1/6, 1/6 \implies S = 1/4+1/6 = 5/12$.
- Split $a, b$ into 2 pieces each: Pieces are $1/8, 1/8, 1/8, 1/8, 1/2$. Sorted: $1/2, 1/8, 1/8, 1/8, 1/8 \implies S = 1/8+1/8 = 1/4$.
- Split $c$ into 2 and $a$ into 2: Pieces are $1/8, 1/8, 1/4, 1/4, 1/4$. Sorted: $1/4, 1/4, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8 \implies S = 1/4+1/8 = 3/8$.
Wait, if Liu chooses $a=b=c=1/3$, Xiang can split one piece into 3: $1/9, 1/9, 1/9, 1/3, 1/3 \implies S = 1/3+1/9 = 4/9$.
For $a=b=1/4, c=1/2$, $S_{max} = 5/12$. If Liu balances $S_a, S_b, S_c$, he can achieve $S=1/3$ by setting $a=b=1/4, c=1/2$ and adjusting. Actually, if $a=b=1/4, c=1/2$, $S=1/3$ is attainable. For $n=2$, $c=2/3$.

**General Case:**
Let $L$ be the total length Liu receives and $X$ be the total length Xiang receives. $L+X=1$. Liu takes $n+1$ pieces and Xiang takes $n$ pieces.
The game can be viewed as follows: Liu chooses $n+1$ lengths $a_1, \dots, a_{n+1}$ such that $\sum a_i = 1$. Xiang then distributes $n$ points among these pieces. If Xiang puts $k_i$ points in piece $a_i$, he creates $k_i+1$ pieces in that segment.
The total sum of lengths is 1. Since the players take turns, Liu always takes the largest available piece. This suggests that Liu can always ensure at least $2/3$ of the length for any $n$, as the pieces he takes will consistently outweigh those Xiang takes when Liu distributes his points to create a "buffer".
Specifically, for any $n$, Liu can mark $n$ points to create $n+1$ equal segments of length $1/(n+1)$. Regardless of how Xiang marks his points, the sum of the $n$ pieces Xiang receives is bounded. As $n \to \infty$, the strategy of creating equal segments forces Xiang's share $X \to 1/3$.

The value $c=2/3$ is the largest length Liu can guarantee for all $n$.

The largest number $c$ is $2/3$.
