To determine the largest number $c$ that Liu can guarantee, let the stick be represented by the interval $[0, 1]$. Liu marks $k_L \le n$ points, and Xiang subsequently marks $k_X \le n$ points. The resulting $M = k_L + k_X + 1$ pieces are claimed alternately by the players, starting with Liu. Each player always claims the largest available piece. Thus, if the sorted lengths are $L_{(1)} \ge L_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge L_{(M)}$, Liu's total length is $S_L = \sum_{i=1}^{\lceil M/2 \rceil} L_{(2i-1)}$.

Liu's objective is to choose $k_L$ and the positions of the points to maximize $S_L$, while Xiang chooses $k_X$ and the positions of the points to minimize it.

Consider the case where Liu creates $n+1$ pieces of lengths $L_1, L_2, \dots, L_{n+1}$. Xiang can then distribute his $n$ points among these pieces. Let $k_i$ be the number of points Xiang marks in piece $i$, where $\sum k_i = n$. Piece $i$ is then divided into $k_i + 1$ smaller pieces. The total number of pieces is $M = \sum (k_i + 1) = 2n + 1$.

Xiang's optimal strategy to minimize $S_L$ is to make the pieces as equal as possible. If Xiang splits a piece $L_j$ into $n+1$ equal pieces, each has length $L_j/(n+1)$. If Liu chooses $L_1 = L_2 = \dots = L_n = \frac{1}{2n+1}$ and $L_{n+1} = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$, and Xiang splits $L_{n+1}$ into $n+1$ equal pieces, all $2n+1$ pieces have length $\frac{1}{2n+1}$, and $S_L = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$. If Xiang splits any other piece $L_j$ ($j \le n$), $S_L$ will be at least $\frac{n+1}{2n+1}$. Thus, Liu can guarantee $S_L \ge \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$.

Alternatively, Liu can make $L_1, \dots, L_n$ arbitrarily small ($\epsilon$) and $L_{n+1} \approx 1$. Xiang's best response is to split $L_{n+1}$ into $n+1$ equal pieces of length $\approx \frac{1}{n+1}$. The pieces are then $n$ pieces of length $\epsilon$ and $n+1$ pieces of length $\frac{1}{n+1}$. Liu's sum is $S_L \approx \lceil \frac{n+1}{2} \rceil \frac{1}{n+1}$.

Comparing these two strategies:
1. If $n$ is odd, let $n = 2k-1$. The first strategy gives $c = \frac{2k}{4k-1}$, and the second gives $c = \frac{k}{2k} = \frac{1}{2}$. Since $\frac{2k}{4k-1} > \frac{1}{2}$, the first strategy is better.
2. If $n$ is even, let $n = 2k$. The first strategy gives $c = \frac{2k+1}{4k+1}$, and the second gives $c = \frac{k+1}{2k+1} = \frac{2k+2}{4k+2}$. Since $\frac{2k+2}{4k+2} > \frac{2k+1}{4k+1}$, the second strategy is better.

For odd $n$, $c = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$. For even $n$, $c = \frac{n+2}{2n+2} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2n+2}$.
These can be unified as $c = \frac{n+1}{2n+1}$ for odd $n$ and $c = \frac{n+2}{2n+2}$ for even $n$. However, we can observe that $\frac{n+1}{2n+1}$ is the result when Liu makes pieces equal and $\frac{\lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil}{n+1}$ is the result when Liu makes one piece dominant. 

For $n=1$, $c = 2/3$. For $n=2$, $c = 2/3$. For $n=3$, $c = 4/7$. For $n=4$, $c = 3/5$.

The largest number $c$ is $\frac{n+1}{2n+1}$ if $n$ is odd, and $\frac{n+2}{2n+2}$ if $n$ is even.
