To find the maximum possible value of $t$ for the given function $f$, we first analyze the structure of $f$. The set $S$ consists of $n=120$ students. The function $f: \mathcal{P}(S) \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(S)$ is defined by $f(X) = \{y \in S \mid \exists x \in X, x \text{ loves } y\}$. Let $R$ be the "love" relation on $S$, where $xRy$ if $x$ loves $y$. Then $f(X) = \{y \in S \mid \exists x \in X, xRy\}$. This is the image of the set $X$ under the relation $R$.

A key property of $f$ is that it distributes over unions: $f(X \cup Y) = f(X) \cup f(Y)$. This means $f$ is a join-homomorphism on the lattice $(\mathcal{P}(S), \subseteq)$. Furthermore, $f(X) = \bigcup_{x \in X} f(\{x\})$. Let $S_k(x) = f^k(\{x\})$ be the set of students reachable from student $x$ by a path of length exactly $k$ in the directed graph $G=(S, R)$. Then $f^k(X) = \bigcup_{x \in X} S_k(x)$.

We are looking for the maximum size $t$ of a sequence $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_t$ of distinct subsets of $S$ such that for any $1 \leq i < j \leq t$, there exists $k_{ij} \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $f^{k_{ij}}(A_i) = A_j$. This condition implies that $A_2 = f^{k_1}(A_1)$, $A_3 = f^{k_2}(A_2)$, and in general $A_{j} = f^{k_{j-1}}(A_{j-1})$. Thus, the sequence $A_1, \dots, A_t$ is a subsequence of the orbit of $A_1$ under $f$: $\mathcal{O}(A_1) = \{A_1, f(A_1), f^2(A_1), \dots\}$. The maximum value of $t$ is therefore the maximum number of distinct elements in any orbit of $f$.

For any $X \in \mathcal{P}(S)$, the sequence $f^k(X)$ eventually becomes periodic. Let $m(X)$ be the pre-period and $p(X)$ be the period. The number of distinct elements in the orbit is $m(X) + p(X)$.
The period $p(X)$ is the least common multiple of the periods of the sequences $S_k(x)$ for $x \in X$. The period of $S_k(x)$ is the LCM of the periods of the strongly connected components (SCCs) reachable from $x$. The period of an SCC is the greatest common divisor of all its cycle lengths. The maximum possible LCM of integers whose sum is at most $n$ is given by Landau's function $g(n)$. For $n=120$, $g(120)$ is approximately $2 \cdot 10^{10}$, which is significantly smaller than $2^{70}$.

The pre-period $m(X)$ is the time it takes for the sequence $f^k(X)$ to enter its periodic phase. For a single vertex $x$, the sequence $S_k(x)$ reaches its periodic phase in at most $n^2$ steps (specifically, the exponent of the adjacency matrix of the graph). Thus, $m(X) \le n^2$.

Combining these, the maximum size of an orbit is $M(f) \le n^2 + g(n)$. For $n=120$:
\[ M(f) \le 120^2 + g(120) \approx 14400 + 2 \cdot 10^{10} \ll 2^{70} \approx 1.18 \cdot 10^{21} \]
The bound $2^{70}$ is thus a very loose upper bound on the maximum possible value of $t$.

The proof is complete by observing that the size of any orbit of the join-homomorphism $f$ is bounded by the sum of the maximum pre-period and the maximum period of the Boolean matrix associated with the relation $R$, both of which are far smaller than $2^{70}$.
