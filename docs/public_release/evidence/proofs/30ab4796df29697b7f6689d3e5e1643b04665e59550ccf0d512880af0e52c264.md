To solve the problem, let the 2026 integers on the blackboard be $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{2026}$.

### Part (1): Termination of the Process

We define a move as choosing two integers $m, n > 1$ and replacing them with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $l' = \text{lcm}(m, n)/\gcd(m, n)$. Note that the product of the two numbers is $g \cdot l' = \text{lcm}(m, n)$, which is less than or equal to the original product $m \cdot n$. Specifically, $g \cdot l' = m \cdot n$ if and only if $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

Let $P = \prod_{i=1}^{2026} a_i$ be the product of all integers on the blackboard. Let $N_{>1}$ be the number of integers on the blackboard greater than 1. We consider the state of the blackboard as a pair $(P, N_{>1})$.
- If $\gcd(m, n) > 1$, then $\text{lcm}(m, n) < m \cdot n$, so $P$ strictly decreases.
- If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $g = 1$ and $l' = m \cdot n$. Since $m, n > 1$, we have $l' > 1$. The number of elements greater than 1 decreases by 1 ($N_{>1} \to N_{>1} - 1$), and $P$ remains constant.

Because $P$ is a positive integer and $N_{>1}$ is a non-negative integer, this process must terminate. The process stops when no two integers are greater than 1, meaning at most one integer $M$ is greater than 1. Since the operation $(m, n) \to (g, l')$ preserves at least one factor greater than 1 (because $\text{lcm}(m, n) > 1$ for $m, n > 1$), and we started with integers greater than 1, we must end with exactly one integer $M > 1$.

### Part (2): Invariance of $M$

To show that $M$ is invariant, we analyze the exponents of the prime factorization of the numbers. For any prime $p$, let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $x$. Let the exponents of the numbers on the blackboard for a fixed prime $p$ be the set $S_p = \{v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026})\}$.

When Confucius replaces $m$ and $n$ with $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and $l' = \text{lcm}(m, n)/g$, the exponents $x = v_p(m)$ and $y = v_p(n)$ are replaced by:
$$v_p(g) = \min(x, y) \quad \text{and} \quad v_p(l') = v_p(\text{lcm}(m, n)) - v_p(g) = \max(x, y) - \min(x, y) = |x - y|$$
We observe that the greatest common divisor of the set $S_p$ is invariant under this operation. Specifically, for any $x, y \in \mathbb{N}_0$:
- If $x, y > 0$, then $\gcd(\min(x, y), |x - y|) = \gcd(x, y)$.
- If $x > 0$ and $y = 0$, the pair $(x, 0)$ is replaced by $(\min(x, 0), |x - 0|) = (0, x)$, and the $\gcd$ of the set $S_p$ is unchanged.
- If $x = 0$ and $y = 0$, the pair $(0, 0)$ is replaced by $(0, 0)$, and the $\gcd$ is unchanged.

Let $g_p = \gcd(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026}))$ be the initial $\gcd$ of the exponents for prime $p$. Throughout the process, this value $g_p$ remains invariant. At the end of the process, only one integer $M$ is greater than 1, so for all other integers $a_k$, $v_p(a_k) = 0$. Thus:
$$g_p = \gcd(0, 0, \dots, v_p(M), \dots, 0) = v_p(M)$$
This proves that for every prime $p$, the exponent of $p$ in $M$ is $v_p(M) = \gcd(v_p(a_1), \dots, v_p(a_{2026}))$. Since the prime factorization of $M$ is uniquely determined by the initial values $a_i$, the value of $M$ is independent of Confucius's choices.
