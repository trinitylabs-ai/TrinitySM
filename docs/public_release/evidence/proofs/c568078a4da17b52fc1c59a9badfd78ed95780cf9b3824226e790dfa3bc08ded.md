Let $M = 3002$ be the number of rows and $N = 3001$ be the number of columns. Peter selects $M-2 = 3000$ cells, one from each row $r \in \{2, \dots, M-1\}$, such that no two selected cells are in the same column. This selection is described by an injective function $f: \{2, \dots, M-1\} \to \{1, \dots, N\}$, where the cell $(r, f(r))$ is a trap. James starts at $(1, 1)$ and aims to reach row $M$. If James enters a trap, he receives a penalty of 1 point and returns to $(1, 1)$.

We wish to find the smallest positive integer $n$ such that James can reach row $M$ before receiving $n$ penalties. This means James must be able to reach row $M$ with at most $n-1$ penalties.

First, we show that $n \le 4$ by providing a strategy for James to reach row $M$ with at most 3 penalties.
James employs the following strategy:
1. Attempt to reach row $M$ by moving vertically down column 1. If he hits a trap at $(r_1, 1)$, he learns $f(r_1) = 1$.
2. If a penalty occurred, attempt to reach row $M$ by moving from $(1, 1)$ to $(1, 2)$ and then vertically down column 2. If he hits a trap at $(r_2, 2)$, he learns $f(r_2) = 2$.
3. If a second penalty occurred, attempt to reach row $M$ by moving from $(1, 1)$ to $(1, 3)$ and then vertically down column 3. If he hits a trap at $(r_3, 3)$, he learns $f(r_3) = 3$.
4. If three penalties have occurred, James knows $f(r_1) = 1, f(r_2) = 2$, and $f(r_3) = 3$. He can now construct a guaranteed safe path using only columns 1, 2, and 3.

For any row $r \in \{2, \dots, M-1\}$, let $S_r = \{1, 2, 3\} \setminus \{f(r)\}$ be the set of columns in $\{1, 2, 3\}$ that are not traps in row $r$. Since $f$ is a function, $|S_r| \ge 2$. For any two adjacent rows $r$ and $r+1$, the intersection $S_r \cap S_{r+1}$ contains at least $3 - 1 - 1 = 1$ column. Let $c_r \in S_r \cap S_{r+1}$. James can move from row $r$ to row $r+1$ using column $c_r$. To transition from $c_{r-1}$ to $c_r$ in row $r$, he moves $(r, c_{r-1}) \to (r, c_r)$. This is safe because both $c_{r-1}$ and $c_r$ are in $S_r$. Since row 1 and row $M$ have no traps, James can start at $(1, 1)$, move to $(1, c_1)$, and proceed through the rows using these safe columns. Thus, James reaches row $M$ with at most 3 penalties, so $n \le 4$.

Next, we show that $n \ge 4$ by demonstrating that Peter can force at least 3 penalties.
Suppose James uses a strategy consisting of a sequence of paths $P_1, P_2, \dots$. Peter can ensure that $P_1, P_2$, and $P_3$ all hit traps. For $P_1$, Peter picks any cell $(r_1, c_1) \in P_1$ (with $2 \le r_1 \le M-1$) and sets $f(r_1) = c_1$. For $P_2$, Peter picks $(r_2, c_2) \in P_2$ such that $r_2 \neq r_1$ and $c_2 \neq c_1$, and sets $f(r_2) = c_2$. For $P_3$, Peter picks $(r_3, c_3) \in P_3$ such that $r_3 \notin \{r_1, r_2\}$ and $c_3 \notin \{c_1, c_2\}$, and sets $f(r_3) = c_3$. 

To see that 2 penalties are insufficient, consider the case where Peter places traps at $(r, 1)$ and $(r+1, 2)$ for some $r$. To pass row $r$, James must be in a column $c \neq 1$. To pass row $r+1$, he must be in a column $c' \neq 2$. If he uses only columns 1 and 2, he must switch from column 2 to column 1. A switch $(k, 2) \to (k, 1)$ is safe only if $f(k) \notin \{1, 2\}$, which means $k \neq r$ and $k \neq r+1$. If he switches at $k < r$, he will hit the trap at $(r, 1)$. If he switches at $k > r+1$, he will hit the trap at $(r+1, 2)$. Thus, he must use a third column $c=3$. However, Peter can place a trap at $(r_3, 3)$ for some $r_3$, forcing a third penalty.

Since Peter can force 3 penalties and James can always succeed after 3 penalties, the smallest such positive integer $n$ is 4.
