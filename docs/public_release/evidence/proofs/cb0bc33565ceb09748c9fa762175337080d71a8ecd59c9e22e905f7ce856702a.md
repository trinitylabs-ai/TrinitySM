To determine the largest number $c$ that Liu Bang can guarantee, we analyze the game's structure. Let $n$ be the number of points each player can mark. The total number of pieces produced is $m \le 2n+1$. Liu and Xiang take turns picking the largest available piece. Thus, Liu receives the $1^{\text{st}}, 3^{\text{rd}}, 5^{\text{th}}, \dots$ largest pieces, and Xiang receives the $2^{\text{nd}}, 4^{\text{th}}, 6^{\text{th}}, \dots$ largest.

Let the pieces be $l_{(1)} \ge l_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge l_{(m)}$. Liu's total length is $L = \sum_{i=1}^{\lceil m/2 \rceil} l_{(2i-1)}$. Xiang's goal is to minimize $L$, which is equivalent to maximizing $S = \sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor} l_{(2i)}$.

**Case $n=1$:**
Liu marks one point $p_1$. This divides the stick into $L_1 = p_1$ and $L_2 = 1-p_1$. Xiang then marks one point $q_1$.
- If $q_1 < p_1$, the pieces are $q_1, p_1-q_1, 1-p_1$.
- If $q_1 > p_1$, the pieces are $p_1, q_1-p_1, 1-q_1$.
In either case, the pieces are $x, y, z$ where $x+y=p_1$ and $z=1-p_1$ (or vice versa). Xiang wants to maximize the median $l_{(2)}$.
If $p_1 = 1/2$, the pieces are $q_1, 1/2-q_1, 1/2$. The median is $\max(q_1, 1/2-q_1)$. To maximize this, Xiang can pick $q_1 \to 1/2$ or $q_1 \to 0$, making $l_{(2)} \to 1/2$. Then $L = 1 - l_{(2)} \to 1/2$.
If $p_1 = 1/3$, the pieces are $q_1, 1/3-q_1, 2/3$ (if $q_1 < 1/3$) or $1/3, q_1-1/3, 1-q_1$ (if $q_1 > 1/3$). In the first case, $l_{(2)} = \max(q_1, 1/3-q_1) \ge 1/6$. In the second case, Xiang can pick $q_1=2/3$, making pieces $1/3, 1/3, 1/3$, so $l_{(2)}=1/3$. Thus $L = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3$.
However, Xiang can always pick $q_1 = p_1 + \epsilon$, making pieces $p_1, \epsilon, 1-p_1-\epsilon$. The sorted pieces are $\max(p_1, 1-p_1), \min(p_1, 1-p_1), \epsilon$. The median is $\min(p_1, 1-p_1)$, which is at most $1/2$. Liu can choose $p_1 \to 0$ to make $l_{(2)} \to 0$, but if Liu marks no points, Xiang marks $q_1=1/2$, and $L=1/2$. Thus $c=1/2$ for $n=1$.

**General Case $n$:**
Liu can choose to mark no points. Then Xiang marks $n$ points, creating $n+1$ pieces. To minimize Liu's share, Xiang makes all pieces equal to $1/(n+1)$. Liu then takes $\lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil$ pieces.
For $n=1$, $L = 1/2$. For $n=2$, $L = 2/3$. For $n=3$, $L = 2/4 = 1/2$.
However, Liu can mark points to force a better outcome. If Liu marks $n$ points such that he creates $n+1$ pieces of length $1/(n+1)$, any point Xiang marks in a piece $L_i$ splits it into smaller pieces.
Consider the strategy where Liu marks $n$ points to create $n+1$ equal pieces of length $1/(n+1)$. Any point Xiang marks in $L_i$ creates pieces $x, \frac{1}{n+1}-x$.
If $n$ is even, let $n=2k$. Liu creates $2k+1$ pieces of length $1/(2k+1)$. Xiang can split these. If Xiang splits one piece into $n+1$ pieces of size $\frac{1}{(2k+1)(n+1)}$, the sorted list consists of $2k$ pieces of size $1/(2k+1)$ and $n+1$ smaller pieces. Liu takes $k$ pieces of $1/(2k+1)$ and some smaller ones.
The optimal strategy for Liu is to ensure that no matter how Xiang splits the stick, Liu can always claim a set of pieces that sum to at least $1/2$. Since Liu moves first in a game of picking the largest values, he can always guarantee at least half of the total length if the number of pieces is even, or slightly more if odd, provided the distribution is balanced. Specifically, for any set of pieces, $l_{(1)} + l_{(3)} + \dots \ge l_{(2)} + l_{(4)} + \dots$. Thus $L \ge 1/2$ is always guaranteed regardless of $n$.

For $n=1$, we found $c=1/2$. For $n \ge 2$, Liu can mark points to ensure $L \ge 1/2$. For any $n$, Liu can mark no points, and if Xiang marks $n$ points, Liu takes $\lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil$ of the $n+1$ pieces. If Xiang makes them equal, $L = \frac{\lceil (n+1)/2 \rceil}{n+1} \ge 1/2$.

The largest $c$ that Liu can guarantee for all $n$ is $1/2$.
