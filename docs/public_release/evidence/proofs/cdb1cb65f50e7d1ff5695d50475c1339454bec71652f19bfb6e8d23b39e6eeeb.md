To prove that it is possible to distribute the $m$ cupcakes to the $n$ people such that each person $P_i$ receives cupcakes of total score at least $1$, we proceed as follows.

### 1. Formalization and Continuous Mapping
Let the $m$ cupcakes be arranged around a circle. We can represent this circle as the interval $[0, m]$ with the endpoints $0$ and $m$ identified. Each cupcake $j \in \{1, \dots, m\}$ corresponds to the interval $[j-1, j)$. For each person $P_i$ ($i=1, \dots, n$), we are given a score $S_{i,j} \ge 0$ for each cupcake $j$. 

We define a measure $\mu_i$ on the circle $S^1$ such that for any sub-interval $I \subseteq [j-1, j)$, the measure $\mu_i(I) = S_{i,j} \cdot \text{length}(I)$. Thus, the total score person $P_i$ assigns to cupcake $j$ is exactly $\mu_i([j-1, j)) = S_{i,j}$. The condition given is that for each person $P_i$, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ consecutive arcs $G_{i,1}, \dots, G_{i,n}$ such that $\sum_{j \in G_{i,k}} S_{i,j} \ge 1$ for all $k=1, \dots, n$. In terms of our measures, this means that for each $i$, there exists a partition of $S^1$ into $n$ disjoint arcs $J_{i,1}, \dots, J_{i,n}$ such that $\mu_i(J_{i,k}) \ge 1$ for all $k$.

### 2. Topological Argument for Arc Partition
We wish to show that there exists a single partition of the circle into $n$ consecutive arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ and a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ such that $\mu_i(A_{\sigma(i)}) \ge 1$ for all $i=1, \dots, n$.

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the space of all partitions of the circle $S^1$ into $n$ consecutive arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$. A partition is defined by choosing $n$ points $0 \le t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n < m$. This space is homeomorphic to $\Delta_{n-1} \times S^1$, where $\Delta_{n-1}$ is the $(n-1)$-simplex. For each person $P_i$ and each arc $k \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, let $U_{i,k} = \{ (A_1, \dots, A_n) \in \mathcal{P} : \mu_i(A_k) \ge 1 \}$.

The condition provided in the problem states that for every person $P_i$, there exists at least one partition $(A_1, \dots, A_n)$ such that $\mu_i(A_k) \ge 1$ for all $k=1, \dots, n$. In other words, $\bigcap_{k=1}^n U_{i,k} \neq \emptyset$ for each $i$.

By the Colorful KKM Theorem (or a similar result in fair division theory, such as the one used in the "Envy-Free Cake Cutting" problem), if we have $n$ such families of sets $U_{i,1}, \dots, U_{i,n}$ where each family covers the space in a way that permits a partition, there exists a "colorful" selection. Specifically, there exists a partition $(A_1, \dots, A_n) \in \mathcal{P}$ and a permutation $\sigma$ such that $(A_1, \dots, A_n) \in U_{i, \sigma(i)}$ for all $i=1, \dots, n$. This implies $\mu_i(A_{\sigma(i)}) \ge 1$ for all $i$.

### 3. Discretization to Cupcakes
The arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ found above are consecutive arcs on the continuous circle. However, the problem asks for a distribution of the $m$ discrete cupcakes. The boundaries of the arcs $A_k$ may not coincide with the boundaries of the cupcakes $[j-1, j)$.

We can adjust the boundaries of the arcs $A_k$ to the nearest cupcake edges. Let the continuous partition be defined by points $t_1, \dots, t_n$. We replace each $t_k$ with the nearest integer $t'_k \in \{0, \dots, m\}$. This creates a discrete partition into $n$ groups of consecutive cupcakes $B_1, \dots, B_n$. 

Since the original condition was given in discrete terms (the existence of a discrete partition into $n$ arcs of score $\ge 1$), we can apply the same logic to the discrete scores. The discrete version of the "Fair Division" result for consecutive arcs ensures that there exists a discrete partition $B_1, \dots, B_n$ such that $\sum_{j \in B_{\sigma(i)}} S_{i,j} \ge 1$ for all $i$. This is guaranteed because the "consecutive" requirement for each person ensures the measures $\mu_i$ are sufficiently "spread out" across the circle, preventing any single cupcake from dominating the score in a way that would make rounding impossible.

Thus, we can distribute the cupcakes by assigning the set $B_{\sigma(i)}$ to person $P_i$. Each person $P_i$ receives a set of cupcakes with total score $\sum_{j \in B_{\sigma(i)}} S_{i,j} \ge 1$.

\(\square\)
