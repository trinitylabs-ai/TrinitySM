To prove that the polynomial $P(x)$ has a non-real root, we assume the contrary: that all $n$ roots $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$ of $P(x)$ are real. Since $P(x)$ has no repeated roots and a nonzero constant term, the roots must be distinct and non-zero.

Any divisor $Q(x)$ of $P(x)$ of degree $k$ must be of the form $Q(x) = C \prod_{r \in S} (x-r)$ for some subset $S \subset \{r_1, \dots, r_n\}$ of size $k$. The coefficients $a_j$ of $Q(x) = \sum_{j=0}^k a_j x^j$ are given by the elementary symmetric polynomials $e_{k-j}$ of the elements of $S$:
\[ a_j = C(-1)^{k-j} e_{k-j}(S), \quad \text{where } e_m(S) = \sum_{\substack{T \subset S \\ |T|=m}} \prod_{r \in T} r. \]
The problem states that for any such divisor, the product $a_0 a_1 \dots a_k = 0$. Since $a_k = C \neq 0$ and $a_0 = C(-1)^k \prod_{r \in S} r \neq 0$ (as $r_i \neq 0$), there must exist some $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $a_j = 0$. This implies that for every subset $S$ of size $k$, there is at least one $m \in \{1, 2, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $e_m(S) = 0$. If $k=1$, the condition $a_0 a_1 = 0$ implies $r=0$ for any root $r$ of $P(x)$, which contradicts $P(0) \neq 0$. For $k > 1$, let $\mathcal{S}_m = \{ S \subset \{r_1, \dots, r_n\} : |S|=k, e_m(S) = 0 \}$. Then we must have:
\[ \bigcup_{m=1}^{k-1} \mathcal{S}_m = \{ S \subset \{r_1, \dots, r_n\} : |S|=k \}, \quad \text{which implies } \sum_{m=1}^{k-1} |\mathcal{S}_m| \ge \binom{n}{k}. \]
We now bound the size of $\mathcal{S}_m$. Let $S_1, S_2$ be two distinct subsets in $\mathcal{S}_m$ that share $k-1$ elements. Let $T = S_1 \cap S_2$ and $S_1 = T \cup \{r_p\}, S_2 = T \cup \{r_q\}$. Then:
\[ e_m(S_1) = e_m(T) + r_p e_{m-1}(T) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad e_m(S_2) = e_m(T) + r_q e_{m-1}(T) = 0. \]
Subtracting these equations yields $(r_p - r_q) e_{m-1}(T) = 0$. Since $r_p \neq r_q$, we must have $e_{m-1}(T) = 0$, which subsequently implies $e_m(T) = 0$. Thus, for $S_1, S_2 \in \mathcal{S}_m$ to share $k-1$ elements, their intersection $T$ (a set of size $k-1$) must satisfy $e_m(T) = 0$ and $e_{m-1}(T) = 0$.

However, a known property of polynomials with only real roots (Newton's Inequalities) states that if a polynomial $\sum c_i x^i$ has only real roots, it cannot have two consecutive coefficients equal to zero unless all coefficients from that point to the end (or beginning) are zero. For the polynomial $f_T(x) = \prod_{r \in T} (x-r)$, the coefficients are proportional to $e_j(T)$. Since $e_0(T)=1$ and $e_{k-1}(T) = \prod_{r \in T} r \neq 0$, no two consecutive $e_j(T)$ can be zero. Thus, no two subsets in $\mathcal{S}_m$ can share $k-1$ elements.

This implies that $\mathcal{S}_m$ is a collection of $k$-subsets such that no two share $k-1$ elements. The maximum size of such a collection is $A(n, 4, k)$. Using the identity $A(n, 4, k) = A(n, 4, n-k)$ and the Johnson bound $A(n, 4, w) \le \frac{1}{w} \binom{n}{w-1}$, let $w = n-k$. Then:
\[ |\mathcal{S}_m| \le A(n, 4, n-k) \le \frac{1}{n-k} \binom{n}{n-k-1} = \frac{1}{n-k} \binom{n}{k+1} = \frac{1}{n-k} \frac{n-k}{k+1} \binom{n}{k} = \frac{1}{k+1} \binom{n}{k}. \]
Summing over $m=1, \dots, k-1$:
\[ \sum_{m=1}^{k-1} |\mathcal{S}_m| \le \frac{k-1}{k+1} \binom{n}{k} < \binom{n}{k}. \]
This contradicts our earlier finding that $\sum |\mathcal{S}_m| \ge \binom{n}{k}$. Therefore, the assumption that all roots are real must be false, and $P(x)$ has at least one non-real root.
