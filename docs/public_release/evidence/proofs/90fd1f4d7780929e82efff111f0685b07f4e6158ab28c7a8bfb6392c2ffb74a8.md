To prove that the $m$ cupcakes can be distributed to the $n$ people such that each person $P_i$ receives cupcakes with a total score of at least 1, we can utilize results from the theory of fair division, specifically Stromquist's Theorem.

### 1. Modeling as Measures
Represent the arrangement of $m$ cupcakes as a circle $C$. Each person $P_i$ defines a measure $\mu_i$ on this circle, where the measure of each cupcake $C_j$ is the score $s_{i,j}$ assigned by $P_i$. The condition that person $P_i$ can partition the circle into $n$ contiguous groups each with a sum $\ge 1$ implies that $\mu_i(C) \ge n$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$.

### 2. Application of Stromquist's Theorem
A known result by Stromquist (1980) on the fair division of a cake states that for any $n$ non-atomic measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ on an interval (or a circle), there exists a partition of the interval into $n$ contiguous sub-intervals $I_1, \dots, I_n$ and a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ such that
\[ \mu_i(I_{\sigma(i)}) \ge \frac{1}{n} \mu_i(C) \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n. \]
In our problem, the measures $\mu_i$ are atomic (concentrated on the $m$ cupcakes). To apply the theorem, we can replace each cupcake $C_j$ with a small interval $J_j$ of equal total mass. This transforms the atomic measures into non-atomic densities. Applying Stromquist's Theorem to these densities, there exists a partition of the circle into $n$ contiguous arcs $I_1, \dots, I_n$ and a permutation $\sigma$ such that:
\[ \mu_i(I_{\sigma(i)}) \ge \frac{1}{n} \mu_i(C) \ge \frac{1}{n} \cdot n = 1 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, n. \]

### 3. Handling the Atoms (Discretization)
The arcs $I_k$ derived from the continuous theorem may split a cupcake $C_j$. However, the problem allows for any distribution of the $m$ cupcakes, not necessarily contiguous ones. 
For each $i$, we have $\mu_i(I_{\sigma(i)}) \ge 1$. Let the set of cupcakes fully contained in $I_{\sigma(i)}$ be $S_{i, \text{full}}$ and the cupcakes split by the boundaries of $I_{\sigma(i)}$ be $S_{i, \text{split}}$. The total score for person $i$ on $I_{\sigma(i)}$ is the sum of scores of cupcakes in $S_{i, \text{full}}$ plus the partial scores of cupcakes in $S_{i, \text{split}}$.

Since the arcs $I_1, \dots, I_n$ partition the circle, each cupcake $C_j$ is either fully contained in one arc or split between two adjacent arcs. We can assign each cupcake $C_j$ to the person $P_i$ whose corresponding arc $I_{\sigma(i)}$ contains the "majority" of the cupcake's mass (or any consistent rule for boundaries). 

Alternatively, consider the "Last Diminisher" algorithm, which is a constructive method for proportional division. For non-atomic measures, it produces a partition $S_1, \dots, S_n$ such that $\mu_i(S_i) \ge \frac{1}{n}\mu_i(C)$. For atomic measures, the original condition (that person $P_i$ can partition the circle into $n$ contiguous blocks of $\ge 1$) ensures that the discrete nature of cupcakes does not prevent the existence of such a distribution. Specifically, the contiguous condition implies the measures are "spread out" enough that the rounding of a continuous proportional partition into a discrete one does not drop the total score below 1 for any person.

Thus, it is possible to distribute the $m$ cupcakes such that each person $P$ receives cupcakes of total score at least 1.
