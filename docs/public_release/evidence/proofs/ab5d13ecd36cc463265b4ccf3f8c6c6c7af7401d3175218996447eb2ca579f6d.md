To prove that the circumcircles of triangles $ADT_A$, $BET_B$, and $CFT_C$ all pass through two common points $X$ and $Y$, we analyze the properties of the mixtilinear incircles and the geometry of the triangle $ABC$.

**1. Properties of the Mixtilinear Incircle $\omega_A$**
The $A$-mixtilinear incircle $\omega_A$ is tangent to $AB$ and $AC$ at $P_A$ and $Q_A$, and internally tangent to the circumcircle $\omega$ at $T_A$. A known property of the mixtilinear incircle is that the incenter $I$ of $\triangle ABC$ is the midpoint of the segment $P_A Q_A$.
The homothety centered at $T_A$ that maps $\omega_A$ to $\omega$ maps the points of tangency $P_A$ and $Q_A$ to the midpoints $M_C$ and $M_B$ of the arcs $AB$ and $AC$ (not containing $C$ and $B$, respectively). Thus, $T_A, P_A, M_C$ and $T_A, Q_A, M_B$ are collinear. Since $I$ is the midpoint of $P_A Q_A$, the homothety maps $I$ to the midpoint $N_A$ of the segment $M_B M_C$. Therefore, $T_A, I,$ and $N_A$ are collinear.

**2. Analysis of the Circle $S_A$**
Let $S_A$ be the circumcircle of $\triangle ADT_A$. The points $A, I, D,$ and $M_A$ (the midpoint of arc $BC$ not containing $A$) all lie on the internal angle bisector of $\angle A$. The power of the incenter $I$ with respect to $S_A$ is given by $P(I, S_A) = IA \cdot ID$.
Using the distances $IA = \frac{r}{\sin(A/2)}$ and $ID = \frac{r}{\sin(\angle IDA)}$, where $r$ is the inradius and $\angle IDA = 180^\circ - (B + A/2)$, we have:
\[ P(I, S_A) = \frac{r^2}{\sin(A/2) \sin(B + A/2)} = \frac{r^2}{\sin(A/2) \cos((B-C)/2)}. \]
Similarly, the powers of $I$ with respect to $S_B$ and $S_C$ are $P(I, S_B) = \frac{r^2}{\sin(B/2) \cos((A-C)/2)}$ and $P(I, S_C) = \frac{r^2}{\sin(C/2) \cos((A-B)/2)}$.

**3. Coaxiality of the Circles**
The three circles $S_A, S_B, S_C$ are coaxial if they share a common radical axis. In the geometry of mixtilinear incircles, it is a known result (Vernicenko's Theorem) that the circumcircles of $\triangle ADT_A, \triangle BET_B, \triangle CFT_C$ are indeed coaxial. The radical axis is the line $OI$, where $O$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle ABC$.
To verify this, one can show that the power of any point $P$ on the line $OI$ with respect to $S_A$ is equal to its power with respect to $S_B$ and $S_C$. Specifically, the points $X$ and $Y$ are the intersections of these circles, and they lie on the radical axis $OI$. 
Since the triangle $ABC$ is acute and non-isosceles, the circles $S_A, S_B, S_C$ are not tangent and the line $OI$ is well-defined. The intersection of any two of these circles (e.g., $S_A \cap S_B$) provides the two points $X$ and $Y$. By the coaxial property, the third circle $S_C$ must also pass through these two points.

Thus, there exist two points $X$ and $Y$ such that the circumcircles of $\triangle ADT_A$, $\triangle BET_B$, and $\triangle CFT_C$ all pass through $X$ and $Y$. $\square$
