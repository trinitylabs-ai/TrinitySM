# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The functional equation $f(f(y)) = 2f(y) - y$ and the substitution $f(x) = x + c(x)$ are correctly derived. The bounds $-g(x, z) \le c(x) - c(z) \le h(x, z)$ are correctly established for $z \in \text{Im}(f)$. The continuity of $c$ on $\text{Im}(f)$ is correctly justified.
Claim gap: The proof contains a load-bearing defect in the density argument (Lines 26–28) used to prove $c(x)$ is constant. The claim that points $z_1 + nc$ and $z_2 + mc'$ can be made arbitrarily close is false when the ratio $c(z_1)/c(z_2)$ is rational and the offset $z_1 - z_2$ is not compatible with the lattice generated by the constants. While the bounds $g$ and $h$ vanish as arguments tend to infinity even with bounded distance, the text explicitly relies on the convergence of the points ($z_1 + nc \to z_2 + mc'$), which is mathematically incorrect for rational ratios. Additionally, the argument for the $c=0$ case (Lines 30–31) assumes that points in the image with $c(z) > 0$ must accumulate near points with $c(z) = 0$, which is not justified.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The density argument requires a repair noting that $g(x, z)$ and $h(x, z)$ vanish as $x, z \to \infty$ provided $x/z \to 1$, which holds for rational ratios by choosing $n, m$ proportional to the ratio of constants. The $c=0$ case requires a topological argument showing that the sets $\{x : c(x) > 0\}$ and $\{x : c(x) = 0\}$ cannot be separated in the domain without violating the original inequalities.
Decisive checks: Line 28 claims "As $z_1 + nc \to z_2 + mc'$", which is a demonstrated defect for rational ratios (distance is bounded below). The conclusion that $c$ is constant is correct but the justification is flawed.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The functional equation $f(f(y)) = 2f(y) - y$ and $c(y) \ge 0$ are correctly derived. The asymptotic analysis (Lines 12–26) is rigorous and correctly establishes that if $c(y_0) > 0$, then $\lim_{x \to \infty} (f(x) - x) = c(y_0)$. This implies $c(y)$ is constant for all $y$ where $c(y) > 0$.
Claim gap: The proof contains a logical defect in the handling of the $c=0$ case (Lines 28–30). It substitutes $f(y)=y$ into the inequalities to obtain bounds depending on a specific $y$, but then incorrectly optimizes these bounds over all $y$ (treating $y$ as a free variable) to conclude $f(x)=x$. The inequalities only hold for the specific $y$ where $f(y)=y$, so the maximization/minimization step is invalid.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The asymptotic derivation is sound. The $c=0$ case requires a repair showing that if $f(y)=y$ for some $y$, the inequalities force $f(x)=x$ for all $x$ (e.g., by showing that a jump in $c(x)$ would violate the inequalities near the boundary).
Decisive checks: Line 30's optimization over $y$ is a demonstrated defect. However, the asymptotic argument (Lines 12–26) is verified and provides a strong justification for the constant nature of $c(y)$ in the non-trivial case.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a stronger mathematical solution. Its asymptotic analysis (Lines 12–26) is rigorous, correctly handles the general case where $c(y) > 0$, and establishes the limit behavior of $f(x)$ without relying on number-theoretic density arguments. Proof A's density argument contains a specific mathematical error regarding rational ratios (claiming convergence of points that remain separated), and its topological argument for the $c=0$ case is also weak. While Proof B has a logical flaw in the final step of the $c=0$ case (optimizing over a fixed parameter), this is a localized deduction error, whereas Proof A's defect lies in the core mechanism used to prove the function is constant. Proof B's verified progress on the main case is more substantial and robust.
