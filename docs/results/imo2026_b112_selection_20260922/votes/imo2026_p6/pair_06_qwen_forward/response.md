# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The set $S$ of sequence terms is exactly the set of integers sharing a factor with every element of $S$. $S$ is a finite union of arithmetic progressions if the collection of minimal hitting sets of the prime factor family is finite.
Claim gap: The induction proof for the finiteness of minimal hitting sets contains a logical gap. The inductive step claims that a specific subset $\mathcal{M}'_p$ constitutes the *entire* collection of minimal hitting sets for a subfamily $\mathcal{F}_p$. This is false if $\mathcal{F}_p$ possesses minimal hitting sets of size $k$ that do not contain $p$ (which are accounted for in other branches of the decomposition). Consequently, the application of the inductive hypothesis (which assumes a bound on *all* minimal hitting sets) is unjustified.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The structural characterization of $S$ and the reduction to hitting sets are correct. The gap is in the combinatorial lemma proving finiteness. A repair would require strengthening the induction hypothesis to "the set of minimal hitting sets of size $\le k$ is finite" and verifying that the decomposition covers all such sets, which the proof attempts but fails to rigorously justify regarding the "minimality" preservation in the subfamily context without extra assumptions.
Decisive checks: Line 13 claims $\mathcal{M}'_p$ is the collection of minimal hitting sets for $\mathcal{F}_p$. Counter-example logic: If $H$ is a minimal hitting set for $\mathcal{F}_p$ of size $k$ and $p \notin H$, then $H$ is a minimal hitting set for $\mathcal{F}$ (since $H$ hits $\mathcal{F}_p$ and sets containing $p$ are hit by... wait, if $p \notin H$, $H$ must hit sets containing $p$ via other elements. If it does, $H \in \mathcal{M}$ but $H \notin \mathcal{M}_p$, so $H \notin \mathcal{M}'_p$. Thus $\mathcal{M}'_p$ is incomplete. The induction fails.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The gaps $a_{n+1} - a_n$ are bounded by $M = \prod_{p|a_1} p$. The sequence eventually becomes periodic if the terms rely only on prime factors $\le M$.
Claim gap: The proof asserts that $a_{n+1}$ must share factors with previous terms using only primes $\le M$ (i.e., primes dividing $a_1$). This is justified by a heuristic argument that "large primes are expensive," but it is not rigorously proven. It is possible (though unlikely in greedy constructions) that a large prime factor provides a "closer" valid candidate than any combination of small primes, or that the structure of previous terms forces the use of a large prime to satisfy the GCD condition for some $a_i$ while small primes are insufficient or misaligned. The reduction to a finite state machine depends entirely on this unproven restriction.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The bound on gaps is correct. The stabilization of the set of prime factor intersections is correct. The periodicity conclusion follows *if* the restriction to small primes holds. The gap is the lack of proof that the greedy choice never introduces or relies on prime factors $> M$ in a way that breaks the periodicity modulo $L_0$.
Decisive checks: Line 5 claims $a_{n+1}$ always shares a prime factor with $M$ (true) and implies it is determined by hitting sets within $\mathcal{P}_M$. The argument that "a multiple of a hitting set $H \subseteq \mathcal{P}_M$ would provide a smaller or equal candidate" is insufficient. It ignores the possibility that the *smallest* integer $> a_n$ satisfying the condition might be a multiple of a large prime $q$ that happens to be very close to $a_n$ (e.g., $a_n + 1$), whereas the next multiple of the small hitting set might be further away. While structural constraints of the sequence likely prevent this, the proof does not demonstrate it.

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Proof A provides a complete structural characterization of the sequence set $S$ and correctly reduces the problem to the finiteness of minimal hitting sets. Although the induction argument in Proof A has a gap regarding the completeness of the subset $\mathcal{M}'_p$, the core mathematical insight (that $S$ is a finite union of APs) is robust and the gap is a technical flaw in a combinatorial lemma that is likely true. Proof B relies on a heuristic "cost" argument to restrict the prime factors of the sequence to a finite set, which is not rigorously justified and leaves open the possibility that large primes could disrupt the periodicity. Proof A's approach is more fundamental and closer to a complete proof, whereas Proof B's central mechanism (the restriction to small primes) is asserted without sufficient proof.
