To find the maximum possible value of $t$, denoted by $M(f)$, we analyze the structure of the function $f: F \to F$ and the "lovely relationship" defined on the set $F$ of all $2^{120}$ subsets of students.

**1. Analysis of the function $f$**
Let $S$ be the set of $N=120$ students. $F = \mathcal{P}(S)$ is the power set of $S$. For each student $x \in S$, let $L(x) \subseteq S$ be the set of students loved by $x$. The function $f: F \to F$ is defined as:
$$f(X) = \{y \in S \mid \exists x \in X, y \in L(x)\} = \bigcup_{x \in X} L(x)$$
This function $f$ is a join-homomorphism of the lattice $(F, \cup)$, because for any $X, Y \in F$:
$$f(X \cup Y) = \bigcup_{x \in X \cup Y} L(x) = \left( \bigcup_{x \in X} L(x) \right) \cup \left( \bigcup_{x \in Y} L(x) \right) = f(X) \cup f(Y)$$
Such a function can be represented by a Boolean adjacency matrix $M$ of size $N \times N$, where $M_{ij} = 1$ if student $i$ loves student $j$, and $0$ otherwise. The action of $f$ on a set $X$ corresponds to the Boolean matrix-vector multiplication $v_{f(X)} = M v_X$, where $v_X$ is the characteristic vector of $X$ and the multiplication is performed in the Boolean semiring $(\{0, 1\}, \lor, \land)$.

**2. The Lovely Relationship and Chains**
Two sets $A, B \in F$ have a lovely relationship if there exists a natural number $k$ such that $f^{k}(A)=B$. This defines a reachability preorder $\le$ on $F$, where $A \le B$ if $f^k(A) = B$ for some $k \ge 0$.
The problem asks for the maximum $t$ such that there exist distinct $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_t$ where for any $1 \le i < j \le t$, $A_i$ and $A_j$ have a lovely relationship. This means $A_1 \le A_2 \le \dots \le A_t$. Since the $A_i$ are distinct, this sequence forms a chain in the preorder.
Any such chain must be contained within the orbit of $A_1$ under $f$:
$$O(A_1) = \{A_1, f(A_1), f^2(A_1), \dots\}$$
The number of distinct elements in this orbit is the pre-period $m$ plus the period $p$ of the sequence $f^k(A_1)$.

**3. Bounding the Orbit Size**
The sequence of vectors $v_k = M^k v_{A_1}$ is determined by the powers of the Boolean matrix $M$. Since there are only $2^N$ possible vectors in $\{0, 1\}^N$, the sequence $v_k$ must eventually repeat by the pigeonhole principle. Thus, the sequence $M^k$ is eventually periodic.

The period $p$ of the sequence $M^k$ is the least common multiple (LCM) of the periods of the strongly connected components (SCCs) of the directed graph $G$ associated with $M$. The period of an SCC is the greatest common divisor (GCD) of the lengths of all its cycles. Since the GCD of cycle lengths in an SCC of size $n$ is at most $n$, the period $p$ is bounded by:
$$p \le \text{lcm}(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_c) \le \text{lcm}(n_1, n_2, \dots, n_c) \le g(N)$$
where $g(N)$ is the Landau function, the maximum LCM of a partition of $N$.

The pre-period $m$ is the number of steps until the sequence $M^k$ enters its cycle. For a primitive Boolean matrix of size $n$, the exponent (pre-period) is at most $(n-1)^2 + 1$ by Wielandt's Theorem. For a general Boolean matrix of size $N$, the pre-period $m$ is bounded by a polynomial in $N$, specifically $m \le N^2$.

For $N=120$, the maximum period $g(120)$ is the maximum LCM of integers whose sum is 120. Using a prime power partition such as $16+9+5+7+11+13+17+19+23 = 120$, we find $g(120) \ge 16 \cdot 9 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 17 \cdot 19 \cdot 23 \approx 2.6 \times 10^{10}$. The actual value of $g(120)$ is approximately $2.6 \times 10^{10}$.
The total number of distinct elements in any orbit is thus:
$$t \le m + p \le N^2 + g(N) \le 120^2 + 2.6 \times 10^{10} \approx 14400 + 2.6 \times 10^{10}$$
Comparing this to $2^{70}$:
$$2^{70} = (2^{10})^7 \approx (10^3)^7 = 10^{21}$$
Since $14400 + 2.6 \times 10^{10} \ll 10^{21}$, the maximum possible value $M(f)$ is significantly smaller than $2^{70}$.

Thus, we have shown that for any such function $f$, the maximum number of distinct elements $A_1, \dots, A_t$ forming a lovely relationship chain is bounded by $N^2 + g(N)$, which is strictly less than $2^{70}$.

\(\square\)
