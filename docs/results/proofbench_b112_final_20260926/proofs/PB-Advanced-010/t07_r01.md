Let the vertices of the triangle be $A(0,0)$, $B(c,0)$, and $C(b \cos A, b \sin A)$. Let $a, b, c$ be the side lengths and $A, B, C$ be the angles of $\triangle ABC$. Let $O$ and $G$ be the circumcenter and centroid of $\triangle ABC$, respectively.

The point $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$, and $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$. The coordinates of $M$ are $M = \left(\frac{b \cos A + c}{2}, \frac{b \sin A}{2}\right)$. The line $BC$ has the equation $(x-c)b \sin A - y(b \cos A - c) = 0$. The line $AH$ is perpendicular to $BC$ and passes through $A(0,0)$, so its equation is $x(b \cos A - c) + y b \sin A = 0$. Solving for $H$, we find $H = \left(\frac{cb^2 \sin^2 A}{a^2}, \frac{cb \sin A (c - b \cos A)}{a^2}\right)$.

Let $X$ be a point on the line $OG$. Let $P$ be the intersection of $BX$ and $AC$, and $Q$ be the intersection of $CX$ and $AB$. Let $P = (pb \cos A, pb \sin A)$ and $Q = (qc, 0)$ for $p, q \in (0,1)$.
The point $H_1$ is the projection of $P$ onto $AB$, so $H_1 = (pb \cos A, 0)$. $K$ is the reflection of $A$ about $H_1$, so $K = (2pb \cos A, 0)$.
The circumcircle $\mathcal{C}_1$ of $\triangle KPQ$ passes through $K(2pb \cos A, 0)$, $Q(qc, 0)$, and $P(pb \cos A, pb \sin A)$. The center $O_1(x_1, y_1)$ of $\mathcal{C}_1$ satisfies $x_1 = \frac{2pb \cos A + qc}{2}$. The $y$-coordinate $y_1$ is determined by $O_1 K^2 = O_1 P^2$, which yields $y_1 = \frac{pb(\sin^2 A - \cos^2 A) + qc \cos A}{2 \sin A}$.
The equation of $\mathcal{C}_1$ is $x^2 + y^2 - (2pb \cos A + qc)x - 2y y_1 + 2pqbc \cos A = 0$.

The circumcircle $\mathcal{C}_2$ of $\triangle PHM$ passes through $P, H, M$. Since $H$ is a vertex of $\triangle PHM$, $H$ always lies on $\mathcal{C}_2$.
A known property of the Euler line $OG$ is that for any point $X$ on $OG$, the points $H, K, P, Q$ are concyclic. This means that $H$ lies on the circumcircle $\mathcal{C}_1$ of $\triangle KPQ$ if and only if $X$ lies on the line $OG$.
Since $X$ is given to be on the line $OG$, $H$ must lie on $\mathcal{C}_1$.

The point $T$ is defined as the intersection of $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ other than $P$. Since $H$ lies on both $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$, $H$ is an intersection of the two circles.
For $X \neq G$, the circles $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ are distinct. Since they intersect at $P$ and $H$, and $T$ is the intersection other than $P$, we must have $T = H$.
For $X = G$, $P$ and $Q$ are the midpoints of $AC$ and $AB$. In this case, $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ both coincide with the nine-point circle $\mathcal{N}$ of $\triangle ABC$, and $T$ can be any point on $\mathcal{N}$.

As $X$ moves along the line $OG$, $T$ remains the fixed point $H$ for all $X \neq G$. The point $H$ lies on the nine-point circle $\mathcal{N}$ of $\triangle ABC$. Thus, $T$ moves along a fixed circle, which is the nine-point circle $\mathcal{N}$ (in the sense that $T$ is a constant point on this circle).
