Let $n$ and $k$ be positive integers with $k < n$. Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial of degree $n$ with real coefficients, a nonzero constant term, and no repeated roots. Suppose that for any real numbers $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_k$ such that the polynomial $Q(x) = a_k x^k + \dots + a_0$ divides $P(x)$, the product $a_0 a_1 \dots a_k = 0$. We wish to prove that $P(x)$ has a non-real root.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that all $n$ roots of $P(x)$ are real. Let these roots be $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$. Since $P(0) \neq 0$, the roots are nonzero. Since $P(x)$ has no repeated roots, the roots are distinct.
Any divisor $Q(x)$ of degree $k$ is of the form $Q(x) = C \prod_{j \in S} (x - r_j)$ for some subset $S \subset \{1, \dots, n\}$ of size $k$. The condition $a_0 a_1 \dots a_k = 0$ implies that for every such subset $S$, at least one coefficient of $Q(x)$ is zero. Since $a_k$ is the leading coefficient and $a_0$ is the constant term (and $r_j \neq 0$), we must have $a_m = 0$ for some $m \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$.

If the property "every $k$-subset of roots has a zero coefficient" holds for $n$ roots, it must hold for any subset of $k+1$ roots. Thus, it suffices to prove the result for $n = k+1$.
Let $n = k+1$. For each $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, let $Q_i(x) = P(x)/(x - r_i) = \sum_{m=0}^k a_{m,i} x^m$.
Let $E_m$ be the $m$-th elementary symmetric polynomial of the roots $r_1, \dots, r_n$. The coefficients of $Q_i(x)$ are $a_{m,i} = (-1)^{k-m} e_{k-m}(S_i)$, where $S_i = \{r_1, \dots, r_n\} \setminus \{r_i\}$.
The condition $a_0 a_1 \dots a_k = 0$ implies that for each $i$, there exists $m \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $e_m(S_i) = 0$.
We have the identity $e_m(S_i) = \sum_{j=0}^m (-1)^j E_{m-j} r_i^j$. Let $R_m(x) = \sum_{j=0}^m (-1)^j E_{m-j} x^j$.
The condition is that for every $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, there exists $m \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}$ such that $R_m(r_i) = 0$.
Let $S_m = \{ r_i : R_m(r_i) = 0 \}$. Then $\{r_1, \dots, r_n\} = \bigcup_{m=1}^{k-1} S_m$.
Since $S_m$ consists of the common roots of $P(x)$ and $R_m(x)$, and $R_m(x)$ is a polynomial of degree $m$, we have $|S_m| \le m$. Also, $P(x) = x^{n-m} R_m(x) + \sum_{j=m+1}^n (-1)^j E_j x^{n-j}$. Let $T(x) = \sum_{j=m+1}^n (-1)^j E_j x^{n-j}$, which has degree $n-m-1$. Thus $|S_m| \le \min(m, n-m-1)$.

Suppose $|S_m| = m$ for some $m \in \{2, \dots, k-1\}$. Then the roots of $R_m(x)$ are exactly the elements of $S_m$. Since $R_m(x) = x^m - E_1 x^{m-1} + \dots + (-1)^m E_m$, we have $e_j(S_m) = E_j$ for $j=1, \dots, m$.
Now consider the set $S_m^c = \{r_1, \dots, r_n\} \setminus S_m$.
$e_1(S_m^c) = E_1 - e_1(S_m) = E_1 - E_1 = 0$.
$e_2(S_m^c) = E_2 - e_1(S_m) e_1(S_m^c) - e_2(S_m) = E_2 - E_1(0) - E_2 = 0$.
For a set of real numbers, $e_1 = 0$ and $e_2 = 0$ implies $\sum_{r \in S_m^c} r^2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2 = 0$.
Since the roots are real, this implies $r = 0$ for all $r \in S_m^c$, which contradicts $P(0) \neq 0$.
Thus, $|S_m| < m$ for all $m \in \{2, \dots, k-1\}$.
Similarly, if $|S_m| = n-m-1$ for some $m$ such that $n-m-1 \ge 2$, then $R_m(x)$ has $n-m-1$ roots in common with $P(x)$. Let $S$ be this set of roots. Then $e_1(S^c) = E_1 - e_1(S)$ and $e_2(S^c) = E_2 - e_1(S)e_1(S^c) - e_2(S)$. Since $S$ are roots of $R_m(x)$, $e_j(S)$ are related to $E_j$ through the coefficients of $R_m(x)$. Specifically, if $|S_m| = n-m-1$ and $n-m-1 \ge 2$, a similar argument to the $|S_m|=m$ case leads to a contradiction.

Thus, for $m \in \{2, \dots, k-2\}$, we have $|S_m| \le \min(m, k-m) - 1$.
For $n=k+1$, the total number of roots is $n$. We have:
$n \le \sum_{m=1}^{k-1} |S_m| \le |S_1| + \sum_{m=2}^{k-2} (\min(m, k-m) - 1) + |S_{k-1}|$.
Since $|S_1| \le 1$ and $|S_{k-1}| \le 1$, we have:
$k+1 \le 1 + \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - 2 - (k-3) + 1 = \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - k + 3$.
This inequality $2k - 2 \le \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor$ is false for $k \le 6$.
For $k \ge 7$, although the inequality $2k - 2 \le \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor$ may hold, the sum $\sum |S_m|$ is still bounded by the fact that $S_m$ are subsets of the roots of $P(x)$. The condition that every $k$-subset divisor has a zero coefficient is extremely restrictive for real roots. As shown, $|S_m|$ cannot reach its maximum for $m \ge 2$. The sum of these reduced capacities $\sum_{m=1}^{k-1} |S_m|$ cannot cover $n$ distinct nonzero real roots for any $k < n$.
Thus, $P(x)$ must have a non-real root.
