To prove that for any positive integers $a, c$ and integer $b$, there exists a positive integer $n$ such that the expression $\frac{a^{F_n} + n - b}{c}$ is an integer, we must show that the congruence
\[ n + a^{F_n} \equiv b \pmod{c} \]
has a solution in $\mathbb{Z}^+$.

**1. Periodicity of the sequence $F_n \pmod{m}$**
Let $m_0 = c$ and define the $\phi$-chain $m_{i+1} = \phi(m_i)$ for $i \ge 0$. Since $\phi(m) < m$ for $m > 1$, there exists $k$ such that $m_k = 1$.
We claim that for each $i \in \{0, \dots, k\}$, the sequence $F_n \pmod{m_i}$ is periodic for $n$ sufficiently large.
- For $m_k = 1$, $F_n \equiv 0 \pmod{1}$ for all $n$, so it is periodic with period $T_k = 1$.
- For $m_i$ with $i < k$, we have $F_n \equiv n^{F_{n-1}} \pmod{m_i}$. If $T_i$ is a multiple of $m_i$ and $T_{i+1}$ (the period of $F_n \pmod{m_{i+1}}$), then for $n$ sufficiently large:
  \[ F_{n+T_i} \equiv (n+T_i)^{F_{n+T_i-1}} \equiv n^{F_{n+T_i-1}} \pmod{m_i}. \]
  Since $T_i$ is a multiple of $T_{i+1}$, $F_{n+T_i-1} \equiv F_{n-1} \pmod{m_{i+1}}$. Because $m_{i+1} = \phi(m_i)$, the generalized Euler's Totient Theorem states that $x^u \equiv x^v \pmod{m}$ if $u \equiv v \pmod{\phi(m)}$ and $u, v \ge \phi(m)$. Since $F_n$ grows rapidly ($F_1=1, F_2=2, F_3=9, F_4=4^9, \dots$), $F_{n-1} \ge \phi(m_i)$ for $n \ge 5$ (or larger if $\phi(m_i)$ is very large). Thus,
  \[ n^{F_{n+T_i-1}} \equiv n^{F_{n-1}} \equiv F_n \pmod{m_i}. \]
  Hence, $T_i = \text{lcm}(m_i, T_{i+1})$ is a period for $n$ sufficiently large. By induction, $T_1 = \text{lcm}(m_1, m_2, \dots, m_k)$.

**2. Analysis of $d = \gcd(T_1, c)$**
We show that $d = \gcd(T_1, c) < c$ for $c > 1$. Let $p$ be the largest prime factor of $c$, and let $v = v_p(c)$.
The value of $m_1 = \phi(c)$ is $m_1 = c \prod_{q|c} \frac{q-1}{q}$. Since $p$ is the largest prime factor of $c$, for any prime $q|c$, $q \le p$, so $q-1 < p$, which implies $v_p(q-1) = 0$. Thus, $v_p(m_1) = v_p(c) - 1 = v-1$.
For $i \ge 1$, the prime factors of $m_i$ are the prime factors of $\phi(m_{i-1})$. Since the largest prime factor of $m_1$ is at most $p$, any prime factor $r$ of $m_i$ satisfies $r \le p$. If $v_p(m_i) > 0$, then $v_p(m_{i+1}) = v_p(\phi(m_i)) = v_p(m_i) - 1$ because $p$ is the only prime factor of $m_i$ that is not strictly less than $p$.
Thus, $v_p(T_1) = \max(v_p(m_1), \dots, v_p(m_k)) = v_p(m_1) = v-1$.
Since $v_p(T_1) = v-1 < v = v_p(c)$, $c$ does not divide $T_1$, so $d = \gcd(T_1, c) < c$.

**3. Inductive Proof**
We proceed by induction on $c$. Let $P(c)$ be the statement: "For any $b \in \mathbb{Z}$ and any $N \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, there exists $n > N$ such that $n + a^{F_n} \equiv b \pmod{c}$."
- Base case: For $c=1$, $n + a^{F_n} \equiv b \pmod{1}$ is always true for any $n > N$.
- Inductive step: Assume $P(c')$ is true for all $c' < c$.
Let $b \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $N \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. Let $T_1$ be the period of $F_n \pmod{\phi(c)}$ for $n \ge N_{\phi(c)}$, and let $d = \gcd(T_1, c)$. Since $d < c$, by the inductive hypothesis $P(d)$, there exists $n_0 > \max(N, N_{\phi(c)}, 5, \text{threshold for } F_n \ge \phi(c))$ such that $n_0 + a^{F_{n_0}} \equiv b \pmod{d}$.
Define $g(n) = n + a^{F_n} \pmod{c}$. For $n \ge N_{\phi(c)}$, we have $F_{n+T_1} \equiv F_n \pmod{\phi(c)}$. Since $F_n \ge \phi(c)$ for the chosen $n_0$, we have $a^{F_{n+T_1}} \equiv a^{F_n} \pmod{c}$ by the generalized Euler's Totient Theorem. Thus,
\[ g(n + T_1) = (n + T_1) + a^{F_{n+T_1}} \equiv n + T_1 + a^{F_n} \equiv g(n) + T_1 \pmod{c}. \]
By iteration, $g(n_0 + kT_1) \equiv g(n_0) + kT_1 \pmod{c}$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.
We seek $k$ such that $kT_1 \equiv b - g(n_0) \pmod{c}$. This linear congruence has a solution if and only if $\gcd(T_1, c)$ divides $b - g(n_0)$.
Since $\gcd(T_1, c) = d$ and $g(n_0) = n_0 + a^{F_{n_0}} \equiv b \pmod{d}$, the condition $d \mid (b - g(n_0))$ is satisfied.
Choosing a sufficiently large $k$ ensures $n = n_0 + kT_1 > N$ and $n + a^{F_n} \equiv b \pmod{c}$.
Thus $P(c)$ is true. By induction, the statement holds for all $c \in \mathbb{Z}^+$.
