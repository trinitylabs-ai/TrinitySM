We proceed by contradiction. Assume that for every integer $k \geq 1$, the polynomial $P_k(x) = \sum_{i=0}^k c_i x^i$ has at least $k$ distinct real roots.

Since the degree of $P_k(x)$ is at most $k$, the assumption implies that $P_k(x)$ must have exactly $k$ distinct real roots. This further implies that $c_k \neq 0$ for all $k \geq 1$, because if $c_k = 0$, then $\deg(P_k) \leq k-1$, and a polynomial of degree at most $k-1$ cannot have $k$ distinct roots.

Consider the power series $f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n z^n$. A theorem by George Pólya states that if all the partial sums $P_k(z)$ of a power series have only real roots, then the power series converges for all $z \in \mathbb{C}$. In other words, $f(z)$ is an entire function.

For any entire function $f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n z^n$, the radius of convergence $R$ is infinite. According to the Cauchy-Hadamard theorem, the radius of convergence is given by
\[ \frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |c_n|^{1/n}. \]
Since $R = \infty$, it must be that
\[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} |c_n|^{1/n} = 0. \]
This implies that $\lim_{n \to \infty} |c_n|^{1/n} = 0$.

However, we have established that $c_n$ are integers and $c_n \neq 0$ for all $n \geq 1$. For any non-zero integer $c_n$, we have $|c_n| \geq 1$. Consequently,
\[ |c_n|^{1/n} \geq 1^{1/n} = 1 \]
for all $n \geq 1$. This implies that
\[ \limsup_{n \to \infty} |c_n|^{1/n} \geq 1, \]
which contradicts the requirement that the limit be 0.

Therefore, the assumption that $P_k(x)$ has at least $k$ distinct real roots for all $k \geq 1$ must be false. Thus, there exists some integer $k \geq 1$ such that $P_k(x)$ has fewer than $k$ distinct real roots. Since $c_0 \neq 0$, $P_0(x) = c_0$ has 0 distinct real roots, and the condition "fewer than $k$ distinct real roots" for $k=0$ would require $0 < 0$, which is impossible; hence the required $k$ must be at least 1.
