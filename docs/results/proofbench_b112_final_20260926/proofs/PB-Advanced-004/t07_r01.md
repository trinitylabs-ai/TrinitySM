To prove that we can choose two diagonals of a convex $(18n+2)$-gon $P$ such that the three resulting parts each contain at least $3n$ and at most $9n$ triangles, we utilize the properties of the dual graph of the polygon's triangulation.

### 1. The Dual Graph Construction
Let the convex $(18n+2)$-gon $P$ be divided into $18n$ triangles by $18n-1$ diagonals. We construct the dual graph $T$ of this triangulation as follows:
- Each triangle in $P$ is represented by a node in $T$.
- Two nodes are connected by an edge if the corresponding triangles share a common diagonal.

Since $P$ is a convex polygon, the dual graph $T$ is a tree. Because each triangle has exactly three sides, each node in $T$ has a degree of at most 3. The tree $T$ contains $N = 18n$ nodes and $N-1 = 18n-1$ edges. Each edge in $T$ corresponds to a unique diagonal of the polygon $P$. Removing two edges from $T$ splits the tree into three connected components; this corresponds to choosing two diagonals in $P$ that divide the polygon into three parts. The number of triangles in each part is equal to the number of nodes in each corresponding component.

### 2. Finding the First Diagonal
A centroid of a tree is a node $v$ such that every connected component of $T \setminus \{v\}$ has at most $N/2$ nodes. For $N=18n$, let $v$ be a centroid of $T$. The removal of $v$ splits $T$ into at most three components $T_1, T_2, T_3$ with sizes $s_1, s_2, s_3$. We have:
$$s_1 + s_2 + s_3 = N-1 = 18n-1, \quad \text{and} \quad 0 \le s_i \le 9n \text{ for } i=1, 2, 3.$$
We claim that at least one $s_i$ must satisfy $6n \le s_i \le 9n$. If $s_i < 6n$ for all $i$, then $s_1 + s_2 + s_3 \le (6n-1) + (6n-1) + (6n-1) = 18n-3$, which contradicts $s_1 + s_2 + s_3 = 18n-1$. Thus, there exists an index $i$ such that $6n \le s_i \le 9n$.
Let $e_1$ be the edge in $T$ connecting $v$ to the subtree $T_i$. Removing $e_1$ splits $T$ into two components: $T_A$ of size $k_1 = s_i \in [6n, 9n]$ and $T_B$ of size $M = 18n - s_i \in [9n, 12n]$.

### 3. Finding the Second Diagonal
We now seek an edge $e_2$ within the subtree $T_B$ to split it into two components $T_j$ and $T_k$ of sizes $k_2$ and $k_3$. We require $k_2, k_3 \in [3n, 9n]$. Since $k_2 + k_3 = M \le 12n$, if both $k_2, k_3 \ge 3n$, then $k_2 = M - k_3 \le 12n - 3n = 9n$ and $k_3 = M - k_2 \le 12n - 3n = 9n$. Thus, it suffices to find $e_2$ such that $k_2, k_3 \ge 3n$.

Let $v_M$ be the centroid of $T_B$. The components of $T_B \setminus \{v_M\}$ have sizes $s'_1, s'_2, s'_3$ such that $s'_1 + s'_2 + s'_3 = M-1$ and $s'_j \le M/2$. By the same logic used for $T$, at least one $s'_j$ must satisfy:
$$s'_j \ge \left\lceil \frac{M-1}{3} \right\rceil$$
Given $M \ge 9n$, we have $s'_j \ge \lceil (9n-1)/3 \rceil = 3n$. Let $e_2$ be the edge connecting $v_M$ to the subtree of size $s'_j$. The two components of $T_B \setminus \{e_2\}$ have sizes:
- $k_2 = s'_j \ge 3n$. Since $s'_j \le M/2 \le 12n/2$, we have $k_2 \le 6n$.
- $k_3 = M - s'_j \ge M - M/2 = M/2 \ge 9n/2 = 4.5n$. Since $s'_j \ge 3n$, we have $k_3 \le 12n - 3n = 9n$.

### 4. Final Verification
The two edges $e_1$ and $e_2$ divide the tree $T$ into three components with sizes:
1. $k_1 \in [6n, 9n]$
2. $k_2 \in [3n, 6n]$
3. $k_3 \in [4.5n, 9n]$
All three sizes are within the range $[3n, 9n]$. Since each edge in $T$ corresponds to a diagonal in $P$, choosing the diagonals corresponding to $e_1$ and $e_2$ divides $P$ into three parts each containing between $3n$ and $9n$ triangles.

\(\square\)
