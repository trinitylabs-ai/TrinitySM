To prove that $XO$ is perpendicular to $DE$ in an acute triangle $ABC$, we employ coordinate geometry. Let $C$ be the origin $(0,0)$ and let the line $AC$ be the $x$-axis. Let the angles of $\triangle ABC$ be $A, B, C$ and its side lengths be $a, b, c$.

### 1. Coordinates of the points
*   $C = (0, 0)$.
*   Since $AC$ is the $x$-axis, $A = (b, 0)$.
*   $B = (a \cos C, a \sin C)$.
*   $D$ is the foot of the altitude from $B$ to $AC$, so $D = (a \cos C, 0)$.
*   $E$ is the foot of the altitude from $C$ to $AB$. In the right triangle $\triangle AEC$, $\angle AEC = 90^\circ$, thus $\angle ECA = 90^\circ - A$. Let $r = CE$. From the law of sines in $\triangle ABC$, $r = a \sin B = b \sin A$. The coordinates of $E$ are:
    $E = (r \cos(90^\circ - A), r \sin(90^\circ - A)) = (r \sin A, r \cos A)$.

### 2. Reflections $E_1$ and $E_2$
*   $E_1$ is the reflection of $E$ across $AC$ (the $x$-axis), so $E_1 = (r \sin A, -r \cos A)$.
*   $E_2$ is the reflection of $E$ across $BC$. The line $BC$ makes an angle $C$ with the $x$-axis. The angle of the segment $CE$ is $90^\circ - A$. The angle of the segment $CE_2$ is therefore $2C - (90^\circ - A) = 2C + A - 90^\circ$.
    $E_2 = (r \cos(2C + A - 90^\circ), r \sin(2C + A - 90^\circ)) = (r \sin(2C + A), -r \cos(2C + A))$.

### 3. The circumcircle $\Gamma$ and its center $O$
The circle $\Gamma$ passes through $C(0,0)$, $E_1$, and $E_2$. Its equation is of the form $x^2 + y^2 - 2gx - 2fy = 0$, where the center is $O = (g, f)$. Since $E_1$ and $E_2$ lie on $\Gamma$:
1.  $r^2 - 2gr \sin A + 2fr \cos A = 0 \implies 2g \sin A - 2f \cos A = r$
2.  $r^2 - 2gr \sin(2C + A) + 2fr \cos(2C + A) = 0 \implies 2g \sin(2C + A) - 2f \cos(2C + A) = r$

Solving this system for $g$ and $f$:
Subtracting (1) from (2) gives $2g(\sin(2C+A) - \sin A) - 2f(\cos(2C+A) - \cos A) = 0$. Using sum-to-product identities:
$2g(2 \cos(C+A) \sin C) - 2f(-2 \sin(C+A) \sin C) = 0 \implies g \cos(C+A) + f \sin(C+A) = 0$.
Thus $f = -g \cot(C+A)$. Substituting this into (1):
$2g \sin A + 2g \cot(C+A) \cos A = r \implies 2g \frac{\sin A \sin(C+A) + \cos A \cos(C+A)}{\sin(C+A)} = r$.
$2g \frac{\cos(C+A-A)}{\sin(C+A)} = r \implies g = \frac{r \sin(C+A)}{2 \cos C}$.
Then $f = -\frac{r \sin(C+A)}{2 \cos C} \cdot \frac{\cos(C+A)}{\sin(C+A)} = -\frac{r \cos(C+A)}{2 \cos C}$.

### 4. Point $X$ and vector $\vec{XO}$
$X$ is the intersection of $\Gamma$ and $AC$ ($y=0$) other than $C$. Substituting $y=0$ into the circle equation $x^2 + y^2 - 2gx - 2fy = 0$ yields $x^2 - 2gx = 0$, so $x = 2g$.
$X = (2g, 0)$.
The vector $\vec{XO} = O - X = (g - 2g, f - 0) = (-g, f) = \left( -\frac{r \sin(C+A)}{2 \cos C}, -\frac{r \cos(C+A)}{2 \cos C} \right)$.

### 5. Vector $\vec{DE}$ and perpendicularity
$\vec{DE} = E - D = (r \sin A - a \cos C, r \cos A)$.
Since $r = a \sin B$ and $\cos C = -\cos(A+B) = \sin A \sin B - \cos A \cos B$:
$r \sin A - a \cos C = a \sin B \sin A - a(\sin A \sin B - \cos A \cos B) = a \cos A \cos B$.
Thus, $\vec{DE} = (a \cos A \cos B, a \sin B \cos A)$.
The dot product $\vec{XO} \cdot \vec{DE}$ is:
$\vec{XO} \cdot \vec{DE} = (-g)(a \cos A \cos B) + (f)(a \sin B \cos A)$
$= -\frac{r \sin(C+A)}{2 \cos C} a \cos A \cos B - \frac{r \cos(C+A)}{2 \cos C} a \sin B \cos A$
$= -\frac{ra \cos A}{2 \cos C} [ \sin(C+A) \cos B + \cos(C+A) \sin B ]$
$= -\frac{ra \cos A}{2 \cos C} \sin(C+A+B)$.
Since $A+B+C = 180^\circ$, $\sin(A+B+C) = 0$. Therefore, $\vec{XO} \cdot \vec{DE} = 0$, which proves that $XO \perp DE$.

\(\square\)
