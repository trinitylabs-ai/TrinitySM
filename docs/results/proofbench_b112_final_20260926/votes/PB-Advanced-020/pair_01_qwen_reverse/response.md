# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The sequence simplifies to $a_n = \gcd(x^n+y, y^n+x)$ for $x \neq y$. If the limit exists, $a_n$ must be eventually constant $L$. The proof rigorously establishes that any prime divisor $p$ of $L$ must either divide $\gcd(x,y)$ or equal $2$. It correctly proves that if $x=1, y>1$ or if $x'+y'$ (where $x=gx', y=gy'$) has a prime factor $p>2$, then $a_n$ cannot be eventually constant.
Claim gap: The proof fails to rigorously handle the case where all prime factors of $x'+y'$ are $2$ (i.e., $x'+y'$ is a power of $2$). The argument relies on a specific example ($x'=1, y'=3$) and an unsupported assertion that $b_n$ "cannot remain constant," leaving a logical gap for general odd $x' \neq y'$.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The simplification, case $x=y$, and prime factor analysis are verified as correct. The gap in the $p=2$ subcase requires an independent periodicity or Zsigmondy-type argument to close, which is absent.
Decisive checks: Verified that $x^n \equiv -y \pmod L$ and $x^{n+1} \equiv -y \pmod L$ correctly imply $y(x-1) \equiv 0 \pmod L$. Verified the prime factor deduction: if $p \nmid x$, then $x \equiv 1 \pmod p \implies y \equiv -1 \pmod p$; symmetrically $y \equiv 1 \pmod p \implies x \equiv -1 \pmod p$, forcing $p=2$. This chain is sound. The contradiction for $p>2$ via order arguments is verified. The $p=2$ case remains unresolved.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The sequence simplifies to $a_n = \gcd(x^n+y, y^n+x)$. Assuming the limit exists, the proof rigorously derives that the eventual constant $L$ must satisfy $L \in \{g, 2g\}$ where $g=\gcd(x,y)$. It proves that if $\gcd(g, X+Y)=1$ (with $x=gX, y=gY$), then $a_n$ is a multiple of $g(X+Y) \ge 3g$ for infinitely many $n$, contradicting the bound $L \le 2g$.
Claim gap: The proof does not fully resolve the subcase where all prime factors of $X+Y$ divide $g$. It asserts that $a_n$ will oscillate or diverge but lacks a rigorous derivation for this specific configuration.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The derivation of $\gcd(L', g)=1$ and $\gcd(L', Y)=1$ is verified. The step $Y(gX-1) \equiv 0 \pmod{L'} \implies gX \equiv 1 \pmod{L'}$ is correct. The deduction $L' \mid 2$ follows rigorously from $X \equiv Y$ and $X \equiv -Y \pmod{L'}$ combined with $\gcd(X, L')=1$. The contradiction for $\gcd(g, X+Y)=1$ is complete. The remaining gap is identical in scope to Proof A's but is preceded by a significantly tighter bound.
Decisive checks: Verified that $g^{n-1}X^n \equiv -Y \pmod{L'}$ and $g^n X^{n+1} \equiv -Y \pmod{L'}$ for $n \ge N$ correctly yield $gX \equiv 1 \pmod{L'}$. Verified that $(gX)^{n-1}X \equiv X \pmod{L'}$ combined with the congruence gives $X \equiv -Y \pmod{L'}$. The combination $X \equiv Y$ and $X \equiv -Y$ forcing $L' \mid 2$ is mathematically airtight. The infinite occurrence of $a_n \ge 3g$ when $\gcd(g, X+Y)=1$ is verified via Euler's theorem.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B establishes a strictly stronger necessary condition for the limit ($L \in \{g, 2g\}$) compared to Proof A's looser constraint ($L$ divides a power of 2 times $g$). B's derivation of this bound is rigorous, leveraging precise gcd properties and modular arithmetic to force $L' \mid 2$. While both proofs share a gap in the specific case where prime factors of $X+Y$ divide $g$, Proof B's framework is mathematically superior: it covers a wider range of cases completely, imposes a tighter bound that makes the remaining gap smaller in scope, and avoids the hand-wavy assertions present in A's treatment of the $p=2$ case. B's deductive structure is more robust and closer to a complete proof.
