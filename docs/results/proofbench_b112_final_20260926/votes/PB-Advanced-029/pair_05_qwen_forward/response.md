# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The necessary condition that $k$ must be even is correctly derived from the case $n=2$. The sufficiency for $k=2$ is correctly established using Catalan numbers.
Claim gap: The sufficiency proof for even $k > 2$ contains a fundamental logical flaw. The argument reduces the problem to prime powers $m=p^a$ and uses the congruence $\binom{m-1}{i} \equiv (-1)^i \pmod p$. While this congruence holds for $m=p^a$, the proof fails to justify the divisibility of the higher-order terms in the binomial expansion modulo $p^a$. Specifically, it asserts without proof that $\sum \binom{m-1}{i}^k \equiv \sum \binom{m-1}{i}^2 \pmod{p^a}$, which is a strong and unsupported claim. Furthermore, if the reduction to prime powers is not explicitly handled as a separate case (i.e., if $m$ is treated as a general integer divisible by $p$), the congruence $\binom{m-1}{i} \equiv (-1)^i \pmod p$ is false for $i \ge p$, invalidating the expansion for composite $m$.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The reduction to prime powers is a standard technique, but the proof text conflates the general $m$ with $p^a$ in the application of Lucas's Theorem. The gap in the higher-order term analysis is substantive; verifying the divisibility modulo $p^a$ for $k>2$ requires deeper $p$-adic analysis (e.g., Wolstenholme-type congruences) not present in the submission.
Decisive checks: The derivation of the necessary condition is correct. The sufficiency for $k=2$ is correct. The step claiming $\sum \binom{m-1}{i}^k \equiv \sum \binom{m-1}{i}^2 \pmod{p^a}$ is unverified and likely false or at least non-trivial. The application of $\binom{m-1}{i} \equiv (-1)^i \pmod p$ is only valid if $m=p^a$ or $i < p$, which is not guaranteed for general $m$ in the sum.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The necessary condition that $k$ must be even is correctly derived. The sufficiency for $k=2$ is correctly established. The algebraic decomposition $\binom{m-1}{i} = (-1)^i + E_i$ is valid. The linear term ($r=1$) in the expansion is shown to be divisible by $m$ (despite a sign error in the intermediate calculation, the final divisibility conclusion holds). The quadratic term ($r=2$) is correctly shown to be divisible by $m$ using the Catalan number property.
Claim gap: The proof asserts that higher-order terms ($r > 2$) "similarly vanish" modulo $m$ without justification. This is a gap, as it requires proving that sums of powers of $E_i$ are divisible by $m$. However, the structure of $E_i$ (being a sum of terms involving $\binom{m}{j}$) provides a stronger basis for this claim than Proof A's approach.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: There is a sign error in the calculation of $\sum (-1)^i E_i$ (Line 27), where the result is stated as $m(-1)^m$ instead of $-m(-1)^m$. However, since this term is subtracted and both results are divisible by $m$, the conclusion that the linear term vanishes modulo $m$ remains correct. The gap for $r>2$ is an assertion, but the preceding steps are rigorous and correctly handle the most difficult parts of the expansion (linear and quadratic).
Decisive checks: The identity $\binom{m-1}{i} = \sum_{j=0}^i (-1)^{i-j} \binom{m}{j}$ is correct. The calculation for $r=2$ yielding $\binom{2m-2}{m-1} + m$ is correct and divisible by $m$. The sign error in the linear term does not affect the validity of the divisibility claim.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a structurally sounder argument. It correctly decomposes the binomial coefficient and rigorously proves the divisibility of the linear and quadratic terms in the expansion. Although it contains a minor sign error in the linear term calculation (which does not affect the final divisibility result) and asserts the vanishing of higher-order terms, its method is valid for all $m$. Proof A, on the other hand, relies on a congruence $\binom{m-1}{i} \equiv (-1)^i \pmod p$ that is false for composite $m$ when $i \ge p$, or fails to justify the reduction to prime powers properly. Additionally, Proof A's claim that the sum of $k$-th powers is congruent to the sum of squares modulo $p^a$ is an unsupported leap. Proof B's explicit handling of the $r=2$ case via Catalan numbers is a significant mathematical advantage over Proof A's heuristic assertion.
