# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: Assuming all $n$ roots of $P(x)$ are real, distinct, and non-zero, the proof correctly reduces the problem to $n=k+1$. It establishes that each root $r_i$ must be a root of some polynomial $R_m(x)$ of degree $m \in \{1,\dots,k-1\}$. Crucially, it rigorously proves that for $m \in \{2,\dots,k-2\}$, the overlap $|S_m|$ cannot reach the trivial bound $\min(m,k-m)$; specifically, $|S_m| \le \min(m,k-m)-1$. This algebraic refinement yields the inequality $k+1 \le \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - k + 3$, which is verified to fail for $k \le 6$, establishing the result for those cases.
Claim gap: The improved bound $\lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - k + 3$ still exceeds $k+1$ for $k \ge 7$. The proof acknowledges this (line 30) but does not provide a rigorous argument to close the gap for large $k$, relying instead on a qualitative assertion that the constraints are "extremely restrictive."
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The reduction to $n=k+1$ is standard and valid. The symmetric polynomial identities in lines 15-21 are verified as correct and require no repair. The gap for $k \ge 7$ is intrinsic to the counting method used and is explicitly noted by the author.
Decisive checks: 
- Line 10-11: Correctly identifies $R_m(x)$ as monic of degree $m$ with coefficients given by elementary symmetric polynomials $E_j$. The index shift from $k-m$ to $m$ is valid since the range $\{1,\dots,k-1\}$ is symmetric.
- Lines 15-21: Verified. If $|S_m|=m$, then $S_m$ are all roots of $R_m$. Using $e_1(A\cup B)=e_1(A)+e_1(B)$ and $e_2(A\cup B)=e_2(A)+e_2(B)+e_1(A)e_1(B)$, the proof correctly deduces $e_1(S_m^c)=0$ and $e_2(S_m^c)=0$. For real numbers, this forces $\sum_{r\in S_m^c} r^2 = 0$, contradicting $P(0)\neq 0$. This step is mathematically sound and non-trivial.
- Line 28 arithmetic: Verified. Removing $m=1,k-1$ from the sum gives $\lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - 2$. Subtracting $1$ for each of the $k-3$ middle terms and adding back the endpoint bounds $|S_1|,|S_{k-1}| \le 1$ yields exactly $\lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - k + 3$. The inequality $2k-2 \le \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor$ correctly fails for $k\le 6$.

## Proof B
Established theorem: Assuming all roots are real, distinct, and non-zero, the proof correctly reduces to $n=k+1$ and derives the same covering condition: each root is a zero of some $f_d(x)$ for $d\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}$. It establishes the trivial counting bound $n \le \sum_{d=1}^{k-1} \min(d,k-d) = \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor$, which yields contradictions for $k\le 4$. It also correctly proves $n < 2k$ via a sign argument (line 5).
Claim gap: Like Proof A, the trivial bound $\lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor \ge k+1$ for $k \ge 5$ leaves the proof incomplete. The attempt to resolve $k \ge 5$ (line 22) provides a single example for $k=5$ and asserts that the resulting system of equations "cannot be satisfied by distinct non-zero real numbers" without proof. This is a heuristic hand-wave rather than a justified mathematical step.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The sign argument in line 5 is valid but does not strengthen the counting bound for large $k$. The reduction and polynomial definitions are correct. The gap for $k \ge 5$ is unaddressed rigorously.
Decisive checks:
- Line 5: Verified. If $\ge k$ roots share a sign, all elementary symmetric polynomials of that subset are non-zero, violating the hypothesis. Thus $p<k, q<k \implies n<2k$. Correct but insufficient for the main counting argument.
- Lines 14-19: Correctly relates coefficients of $Q_j(x)$ to $f_d(r)$ and bounds common roots by $\min(d, k-d)$.
- Line 22: Unverified. The claim that the constraints for $k=5$ cannot be satisfied is stated without derivation. No general argument is provided for $k>5$. This leaves the core obligation for large $k$ completely unsupported.

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Both proofs correctly reduce the problem to $n=k+1$ and derive the same fundamental covering bound $\sum \min(m,k-m) = \lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor$, which fails to yield a contradiction for $k \ge 7$ (A) or $k \ge 5$ (B). The decisive difference lies in how each handles this gap. Proof A provides a rigorous algebraic refinement (lines 15-21) proving that the overlap $|S_m|$ cannot reach its trivial maximum for $2 \le m \le k-2$, strictly improving the bound to $\lfloor k^2/4 \rfloor - k + 3$ and closing the case for $k \le 6$. This derivation is verified and mathematically substantive. Proof B offers only a sign-based necessary condition ($n<2k$) and a heuristic example for $k=5$, leaving the large-$k$ case entirely unjustified. While neither proof is complete for all $k$, Proof A demonstrates stronger justified progress by concretely tightening the counting argument through verified symmetric polynomial identities, whereas Proof B's attempt to address the same gap lacks mathematical rigor.
