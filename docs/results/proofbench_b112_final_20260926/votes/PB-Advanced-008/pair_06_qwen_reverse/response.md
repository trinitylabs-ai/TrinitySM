# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The proof correctly establishes that for $n \equiv r \pmod L$ (where $L = \text{lcm}(m_1, \dots, m_k)$ and $m_i$ is the $\phi$-chain of $c$), the value of $F_n \pmod{m_1}$ stabilizes to a constant $v_1(r)$ for sufficiently large $n$. It correctly reduces the original congruence to finding $r \in \{1, \dots, L\}$ such that the linear congruence $qL \equiv b - r - W(r) \pmod c$ is solvable, where $W(r) = a^{m_1 + v_1(r)} \pmod c$. This solvability condition is rigorously shown to be equivalent to finding $r$ such that $h(r) = r + W(r) \equiv b \pmod d$, where $d = \gcd(L, c)$.
Claim gap: The proof asserts that the map $h(r) = r + W(r) \pmod d$ is surjective onto $\mathbb{Z}_d$ (Step 25) but provides no rigorous justification. It demonstrates that $h(1) \not\equiv h(L) \pmod d$ for $d \ge 2$, proving the image contains at least two distinct values, but this does not establish that the image covers all residues modulo $d$. Without surjectivity, the existence of a solution for an arbitrary target $b$ is not guaranteed.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The argument relies on the heuristic phrase "flexibility of $v_1(r)$" to claim surjectivity. No mathematical repair is present in the submission to bridge this gap.
Decisive checks: The reduction to the linear congruence (Steps 17-19) and the periodicity of $F_n \pmod{m_1}$ (Steps 7-12) are verified. The claim that $h(r)$ is surjective (Step 25) is a demonstrated defect; distinctness of two values does not imply surjectivity for $d > 2$, and the dependence of $W(r)$ on $r$ is not analyzed sufficiently to guarantee coverage of $\mathbb{Z}_d$.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The proof establishes that $F_n \pmod{\phi(c)}$ is periodic for sufficiently large $n$ with period $T_1 = \text{lcm}(m_1, \dots, m_k)$. It rigorously proves that $d = \gcd(T_1, c) < c$ for $c > 1$ using $p$-adic valuations of the $\phi$-chain. It then uses strong induction on $c$ to show that for any $b$, there exists $n$ satisfying the congruence. The inductive step correctly finds a "seed" $n_0$ satisfying the condition modulo $d$ (using hypothesis $P(d)$) and lifts it to a solution modulo $c$ by stepping by multiples of $T_1$.
Claim gap: NONE supported by checks. The proof explicitly handles the "sufficiently large $n$" requirement by choosing the seed $n_0$ larger than the periodicity and exponent thresholds required by the generalized Euler theorem.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The argument is self-contained and rigorous.
Decisive checks: The valuation argument for $d < c$ (Steps 16-20) is verified: for the largest prime factor $p$ of $c$, $v_p(T_1) = v_p(c) - 1$, ensuring $c \nmid T_1$ and thus $d < c$. The inductive lifting step (Steps 26-32) is verified: $g(n_0 + kT_1) \equiv g(n_0) + kT_1 \pmod c$, and the linear congruence for $k$ is solvable because $\gcd(T_1, c) = d$ divides $b - g(n_0)$ by the choice of $n_0$. The quantifier handling for $N$ in the induction hypothesis correctly allows pushing $n_0$ beyond all necessary thresholds.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a complete and rigorous solution using induction on $c$. It correctly identifies that $d = \gcd(T_1, c) < c$ via a verified $p$-adic valuation argument and uses the inductive hypothesis to find a solution modulo $d$, which is then lifted to modulo $c$ using the periodicity of $F_n$. Proof A reduces the problem to a surjectivity claim about a function $h(r)$ modulo $d$ but fails to prove this surjectivity, offering only a heuristic argument and a check for two specific values. Proof B's inductive structure elegantly avoids the need for such a difficult surjectivity analysis, making it mathematically superior and complete.
