# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: For all positive integers $a, c$ and integer $b$, there exists a positive integer $n$ such that $a^{F_n} + n \equiv b \pmod{c}$. The proof establishes this via strong induction on $c$, leveraging a shift property of the sequence modulo $c$ and a strict descent argument on the modulus via $\gcd(L, c) < c$.
Claim gap: NONE. All logical steps are verified and correctly quantified.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The handling of "sufficiently large $n$" for the generalized Euler theorem conditions and the superexponential growth of $F_n$ is standard and correctly bounded within the submission. No external lemmas or silent repairs were required.
Decisive checks: 
- **Periodicity & Shift Property (Lines 3-12):** Verified. For $L = T(\phi(c))$, the sequence $F_n \pmod{\phi(c)}$ is eventually periodic with period dividing $L$. By the generalized Euler theorem, $a^{F_{n+L}} \equiv a^{F_n} \pmod{c}$ once $F_n \ge \phi(c)$. Thus $g_c(n+L) \equiv g_c(n) + L \pmod{c}$ for $n \ge K_{shift}$. The quantifier "for $n$ sufficiently large" is correctly managed by choosing $n_0 \ge K_{shift}$ in the inductive step.
- **Descent Argument (Lines 17-20):** Verified. Let $p$ be the largest prime factor of $c$ with $v_p(c)=k$. Since $p \nmid (q-1)$ for any prime $q|c$, $v_p(\phi(c)) = k-1$. Iterating $\phi$ reduces the $p$-adic valuation by exactly 1 until it reaches 0. Thus $v_p(L) = v_p(\text{lcm}(\phi(c), \phi^2(c), \dots)) = k-1$. Hence $v_p(\gcd(L,c)) = k-1 < k$, proving $\gcd(L,c) < c$. This guarantees the induction strictly descends.
- **Inductive Step & Linear Congruence (Lines 22-27):** Verified. The hypothesis $P(c')$ provides $n_0 \ge \max(K, K_{shift})$ such that $g_{c'}(n_0) \equiv b \pmod{c'}$. Setting $n = n_0 + mL$ and applying the shift property yields $g_c(n) \equiv g_c(n_0) + mL \pmod{c}$. Solving for $m$ requires $\gcd(L,c) \mid (b - g_c(n_0))$, which is exactly $g_c(n_0) \equiv b \pmod{c'}$. Since $c' | c$, $g_c(n_0) \pmod{c'} = g_{c'}(n_0)$, matching the hypothesis. Choosing $m \ge 0$ preserves $n \ge K$. All quantifiers and domains align perfectly.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The problem is correctly reduced to showing that the map $f(r) = a^{V(r) + \phi(c)} + r \pmod{h}$ is surjective onto $\mathbb{Z}_h$, where $h = \gcd(c, \phi(c))$ and $V(r)$ is the stable residue of $F_n \pmod{\phi(c)}$ for $n \equiv r \pmod M$. The algebraic reduction to this surjectivity condition is sound.
Claim gap: The surjectivity of $f(r)$ modulo $h$ for $h > 2$ is asserted without proof. The argument relies on heuristic claims that $V(r)$ can be freely varied modulo $\phi(h)$ and that the linear term $r$ combines with the exponential term to cover all residues. No rigorous mechanism or lemma is provided to guarantee this coverage, leaving the core existence obligation unmet.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The gap is load-bearing; repairing it requires a separate, non-trivial analysis of the distribution of power tower residues modulo $\phi(h)$, which is absent from the submission.
Decisive checks:
- **Stabilization of $F_n \pmod{m_0}$ (Lines 5-9):** Verified. The induction on $i$ correctly shows $F_n \pmod{m_i}$ stabilizes to a constant $V_i(R)$ depending only on $R \pmod M$, using the generalized Euler theorem for large exponents. The domain restriction to $n \equiv R \pmod M$ is correctly applied.
- **Surjectivity Claim (Lines 17-22):** Demonstrated defect. For $h > 2$, the proof states: "we can pick $r$ such that $r \equiv z \pmod h$ and $r \equiv 0 \pmod{\text{rad}(m_0)}$, we can make $V(r) \equiv 0 \pmod{m_0}$ for any $z$ that is a multiple of $\text{rad}(h)$." This is mathematically unjustified. $V(r)$ is determined by the recursive power tower structure modulo $\phi(c)$, not by independent choices of $r$ modulo $\text{rad}(m_0)$. The claim that varying $r$ yields arbitrary values of $V(r) \pmod{\phi(h)}$ is a heuristic assertion, not a proof. Without establishing surjectivity, the existence of $R$ (and thus $n$) is not guaranteed. The quantifier shift from "there exists $R$" to "the map is surjective" is correct, but the surjectivity itself remains unproven.

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Proof A provides a complete, rigorous solution. Its inductive descent on $c$ via $\gcd(L, c) < c$ is correctly justified by a precise $p$-adic valuation argument, and the shift property cleanly reduces the problem to a solvable linear congruence with properly managed quantifiers and thresholds. Proof B correctly reduces the problem to a surjectivity condition but fails to prove it, relying on unsupported heuristic claims about the distribution of power tower residues. Since A's central chain of implications is fully verified and B's core obligation remains unproven, A is decisively stronger.
