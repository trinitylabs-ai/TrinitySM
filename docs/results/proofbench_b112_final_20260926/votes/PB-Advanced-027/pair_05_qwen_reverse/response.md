# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: Bob has a winning strategy. The proof correctly translates the direct similarity condition into a complex ratio condition $\frac{c-a}{b-a} \in T$ and defines $U = \mathbb{C} \setminus T$. It argues that Bob can either construct a $K_5$ subgraph (Case 1) or kill all edges (Case 2) depending on the geometric nature of $U$.
Claim gap: Case 1 contains a load-bearing defect in the geometric construction of $K_5$. The argument claims that by picking $C_1, C_2$ far apart, the "complements of disks" in the forbidden sets $V_{C_i C_j}(U)$ become large disks, allowing $C_3, C_4, C_5$ to be chosen in their intersection. This is imprecise and potentially false: $V_{C_i C_j}(U)$ may contain multiple disjoint complements of disks, and their interiors need not intersect. Furthermore, the classification of $U$ as "contained in a finite union of lines and disks" is arbitrary and lacks a rigorous topological justification for why the forbidden sets can always be avoided. The argument hand-waves over the intersection of allowed regions required to place the five points.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The gap in Case 1 prevents full verification. The translation of the similarity condition and the setup of $U$ are correct. Case 2 is plausible but relies on the same vague "not contained in finite union" criterion without addressing measure/topology.
Decisive checks: Lines 4-8 correctly derive the ratio condition. Lines 15-22 attempt a greedy $K_5$ construction but fail to rigorously justify that the intersection of the allowed regions is non-empty when $U$ contains $0$ or has complex geometry. The claim that "intersection of finitely many open disks is a non-empty open set" ignores that the forbidden sets are complements of disks, so Bob must pick points *inside* disks, but multiple such disks may be disjoint or arranged such that no common point exists.

## Proof B
Established theorem: Bob has a winning strategy. The proof correctly identifies $F = \mathbb{C} \setminus (L \cup (1-L))$ and partitions the analysis into three exhaustive cases based on the topological properties of $F$: non-empty interior, empty interior but dense, and empty interior and not dense. In each case, Bob has a constructive strategy to either isolate a vertex (disconnecting the graph) or build a $K_\infty$ subgraph (ensuring non-planarity).
Claim gap: NONE supported by checks. The topological arguments are precise. The use of affine and Möbius transformations to preserve the "empty interior" property is correct. The inductive avoidance of finite unions of sets with empty interior (plus distance/collinearity constraints) is rigorously justified by the fact that $\mathbb{C}$ cannot be covered by a finite union of sets with empty interior and bounded disks.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The proof stands as written.
Decisive checks: Lines 1-2 correctly derive the ratio condition and define $F$. Lines 5-11 (Case 1) correctly isolate $v_1$ by placing $v_{n+1}$ in an open disk $v_1 + (v_n - v_1)U$, which is always possible since an open disk minus a finite union of lines/circles is non-empty. Lines 14-15 (Subcase 2a) correctly use density to kill all edges. Lines 17-30 (Subcase 2b) correctly show that if $F$ has empty interior, the forbidden sets $S_{i,j}$ and $T_{i,k}$ also have empty interior. A finite union of such sets cannot cover $\mathbb{C}$, so $v_n$ always exists. The inductive construction of $K_\infty$ is valid and correctly handles both existing and new edges.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a complete, rigorous, and topologically precise argument covering all cases exhaustively. Its case division (interior vs. no interior, dense vs. not dense) is standard and cleanly handles the geometry of the forbidden sets using correct properties of affine and Möbius maps. Proof A's Case 1 relies on a flawed geometric intuition about intersecting "large disks" to avoid complements of disks, which fails to guarantee a valid placement for the $K_5$ vertices when multiple disjoint forbidden regions exist. Proof B's avoidance argument is mathematically sound and leaves no gaps. Both correctly conclude Bob wins, but B's justification is substantially stronger and fully verified.
