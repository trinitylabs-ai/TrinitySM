# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The set $V$ of values appearing infinitely often is non-empty. The sequence eventually alternates between bounded values and terms that grow without bound.
Claim gap: The proof fails to establish that $V$ is finite, and the subsequent state-space/parity argument does not rigorously connect the eventual behavior of $x_m$ to the periodicity of $\{b_n\}$ or $\{g_n\}$. The claim that an odd period $L$ leads to a contradiction is hand-wavy and lacks a verified mechanism.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: None supplied; the gaps are load-bearing. The argument that $V$ infinite implies every integer appears infinitely often is false (it only implies each appears at least once). The finite state space claim relies on the "relative order of counts" stabilizing, which is asserted without proof.
Decisive checks: Line 5 claims "This implies every positive integer $i$ appears infinitely often (since $V$ is infinite)". This is a demonstrated defect: if $V$ is infinite, for each $v \in V$ the term $x_{m_i+1}=i$ shows $i$ appears at least once, not infinitely often. The proof cannot deduce $V=\mathbb{Z}^+$ from this. Lines 9-13 assert periodicity via a finite state space of "relative orders" and parity arguments, but do not prove the state transitions are deterministic or that the parity of the period forces one subsequence to be bounded. The link between $x_m$'s structure and $\{b_n\}, \{g_n\}$ periodicity remains unresolved.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The value 1 appears infinitely often. For sufficiently large $m$, if $a_m=1$ then $a_{m+2}=1$. Consequently, one of the subsequences $\{b_n\}$ or $\{g_n\}$ is eventually constant 1, hence periodic.
Claim gap: NONE supported by checks. The argument that $C(1, m-1)$ eventually dominates all other counts is justified by the recurrence dynamics: values generated from 1 are strictly increasing and thus appear at most once, ensuring 1s accumulate faster than any other value.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The proof's own observation (Line 10) that large values $1+C(1, j-2)$ are strictly increasing and appear at most once rigorously supports the claim in Line 12 that $C(1, m-1)$ exceeds other frequencies for large $m$.
Decisive checks: Line 4 correctly proves 1 must appear for $m>N$ by contradiction (finite range implies some value repeats infinitely, triggering unbounded growth). Lines 7-14 correctly establish $a_m=1 \implies a_{m+2}=1$ for large $m$: $a_{m+1}=K=1+C(1, m-1)$, and $C(K, m)=0$ because $K$ would require some earlier count to equal $C(1, m-1)$, which is impossible since $C(1, \cdot)$ strictly increases at $m$ and dominates other counts. Line 17-19 correctly deduces that $a_{m+2k}=1$ for all $k$, making one parity subsequence eventually constant. This directly satisfies the problem statement.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a complete, rigorous derivation that directly establishes the required periodicity by proving the sequence eventually satisfies $a_m=1 \implies a_{m+2}=1$, making one gender's subsequence eventually constant. Its key step ($C(K,m)=0$) is correctly justified by the strictly increasing nature of values generated from 1. Proof A contains a demonstrated logical defect in claiming $V$ infinite implies every integer appears infinitely often, and its subsequent state-space/parity argument relies on unverified assertions about finite states and period parity that do not rigorously connect to the problem's conclusion. B's argument is self-contained and mathematically sound, while A's central chain of implications breaks down at the finiteness of $V$ and the periodicity deduction.
