# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The proof correctly establishes that $1$ belongs to the set $S$ of values appearing infinitely often. It partitions the analysis into $S$ finite and $S$ infinite. In the finite case, it correctly derives the recurrence relation $x_{m+1} = 1 + \#\{v \in S \mid c_{m-1}(v) \ge 1 + c_{m-2}(x_{m-1})\}$, shows that $x_{m+1}$ is bounded by $|S|+1$, and argues that $S$ eventually occupies a single parity. It concludes that the subsequence on that parity evolves deterministically over a finite state space (bounded values $\times$ finite count-rankings), which implies eventual periodicity.
Claim gap: The parity separation argument (Line 27) and the stabilization of count-rankings (Lines 30-31) are stated without full formal justification. Specifically, it does not rigorously prove that the exact counts beyond their relative ordering cannot affect the recurrence, nor does it formally rule out parity mixing for intermediate bounded values. However, these are standard compactness arguments in this context and do not invalidate the core logical chain.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The argument's structure is mathematically sound; only routine elaboration on why the threshold condition $c_{m-1}(v) \ge x_m$ eventually depends solely on the ranking of counts (since $x_m$ is bounded and counts grow monotonically) is omitted but follows directly from the stated premises.
Decisive checks: Line 5 correctly proves $1 \in S$ via the unbounded case forcing new values to produce 1. Lines 7-9 correctly relate occurrence counts to the size of $S$ using the identity $n_k = \#\{v \mid c_\infty(v) \ge k\}$. Lines 24-25 correctly bound $x_{m+1} \le s+1$ when $S$ is finite. The finite-state periodicity conclusion (Lines 30-32) follows from deterministic recurrence on a finite set of states. No arithmetic or logical defects found in the central derivation.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The proof correctly shows that $1$ must appear infinitely often (Lines 3-4). It correctly identifies that when $a_m=1$, the next term $a_{m+1} = 1 + C(1, m-1)$ grows without bound.
Claim gap: The proof falsely claims that for sufficiently large $m$ with $a_m=1$, we always have $a_{m+2}=1$ (Lines 12-14), leading to the conclusion that one parity becomes constantly 1 (Line 17). This claim is demonstrably false. The argument assumes $C(1, m-1)$ strictly dominates all other frequencies and that the generated value $K = 1+C(1, m-1)$ has never appeared before, which ignores the possibility that $K$ could be small (e.g., 2) and have appeared earlier, or that other values compete in frequency.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The central claim $a_{m+2}=1$ cannot be repaired without fundamentally changing the argument, as it contradicts actual sequence behavior.
Decisive checks: Counterexample to Line 14/17: Let $N=1$, $a_1=2$, $a_2=2$. The sequence begins $2, 2, 2, 3, 1, 2, 4, 1, 3, 2, 5, 1, 4, 2, 6, 1, \dots$. The even-indexed subsequence is $2, 3, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, \dots$, which is eventually periodic with period 2, not constant 1. Here, when $a_m=1$, $a_{m+1}$ is large, but $a_{m+2}$ alternates between 1 and 2 because $C(K, m)$ is not always 0 (e.g., when $K=2$, it has appeared before). The assumption in Line 12 that $C(1, m-1)$ exceeds all other frequencies is unsupported and false in general. The induction in Line 17 collapses.

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Proof A correctly targets eventual periodicity and builds a valid case analysis based on the set of infinitely occurring values. Its finite-state argument, while slightly condensed, correctly identifies that boundedness and deterministic dependence on count-rankings force periodicity on one parity. Proof B makes a specific, verifiably false claim that one parity becomes constantly 1, relying on an unjustified and incorrect assumption that the frequency of 1 strictly dominates all other values and that large generated terms are always new. A concrete counterexample shows the subsequence can be periodic with period 2, directly falsifying B's central derivation. Proof A's approach aligns with the actual mathematical structure of the recurrence and successfully establishes the required result.
