# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: James can guarantee reaching the last row with at most $3000$ penalties, proving $n \le 3001$ is sufficient. The sequential column-testing strategy (Lines 3-9) is correctly described: after $k$ penalties in distinct columns, the $(k+1)$-th column is provably free, allowing safe traversal.
Claim gap: The lower bound proof (Lines 11-22) fails to rigorously establish that $n \ge 3001$. It asserts Peter can dynamically maintain a set of consistent configurations to block all safe paths for $m < 3000$ penalties, but does not construct a fixed adversarial configuration $P$ or prove that the dynamic choices can be realized by a single valid $P$. The argument relies on vague assertions about "guaranteed safe" cells and adjacency constraints without formal verification.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The lower bound argument is left as an unresolved heuristic. No substantive repair was supplied; the gap remains in the necessity proof.
Decisive checks: Lines 3-9 correctly verify sufficiency. Lines 11-22 attempt a lower bound via an adaptive Peter strategy. Line 14 correctly notes that in unhit rows, James can only safely visit columns in $C_{hit}$. However, Lines 18-21 claim Peter can force $C_{hit}$ to lack adjacent columns or block column switches without demonstrating how a single fixed $P$ enforces this against an arbitrary James strategy. The transition from "Peter can declare..." to "no path exists" lacks a constructive or counting argument, leaving the lower bound unverified.

## Proof B
Established theorem: James can guarantee success with at most $3000$ penalties ($n \le 3001$), and no strategy can guarantee success with fewer than $3000$ penalties ($n \ge 3001$). The proof establishes $n=3001$ exactly.
Claim gap: NONE. Both sufficiency and necessity are fully justified.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The argument is self-contained and relies only on the problem's constraints and standard information-theoretic reasoning about unknown penalty locations.
Decisive checks: Lines 1-3 correctly verify sufficiency. Lines 5-18 establish necessity by fixing a specific adversarial configuration $P = \{(r, r-1) : r=2,\dots,N+1\}$. Line 7 correctly deduces that in any unknown row $u$, James must restrict movement to columns in $C_P$ to guarantee safety, since any column outside $C_P$ could be the hidden blocker. Lines 9-17 use a clean inductive argument on the column sequence $c^{(i)}$ used to cross known rows $r_i$. The constraint that horizontal traversal in row $r_i$ must avoid $x_i = r_i-1$ forces $c^{(i-1)}$ and $c^{(i)}$ to lie on the same side of $x_i$. Since $x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_k$, induction shows $c^{(i)} \in \{x_{i+1}, \dots, x_k\}$. At $i=k$, this forces $c^{(k-1)} = x_k$, but row $r_k$ requires avoiding $x_k$, yielding a contradiction. The arithmetic and logical flow are verified and airtight.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Both proofs correctly establish the upper bound $n \le 3001$ via the sequential column strategy. The decisive difference lies in the lower bound. Proof A's necessity argument relies on an ill-defined dynamic adversarial strategy and hand-wavy claims about blocking column switches, failing to construct a fixed configuration or rigorously prove the impossibility of a safe path for $m < 3000$ penalties. Proof B fixes a concrete diagonal configuration $P = \{(r, r-1)\}$ and uses a precise information-theoretic constraint (James must stay in $C_P$ for unknown rows) combined with a verified induction on column transitions to prove that any strategy incurs at least $3000$ penalties. Proof B's lower bound is complete, rigorous, and directly addresses the problem's quantifiers, while Proof A's remains a heuristic sketch.
