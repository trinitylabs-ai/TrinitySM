## Proof A
Established theorem: The problem statement is fully justified. The submission correctly translates the hypothesis into the condition $\nu(\mathcal{F}_i) \ge n$ for each family $\mathcal{F}_i$ of consecutive blocks with score $\ge 1$, applies a standard combinatorial fair-division theorem to extract disjoint representatives $A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i$, and assigns $A_i$ to person $P_i$. Since scores are non-negative, distributing remaining cupcakes arbitrarily preserves the $\ge 1$ guarantee for all $n$ people.
Claim gap: NONE. The logical chain is complete. The cited theorem is a known result in combinatorial geometry/fair division (standardly attributed to Aharoni–Holzman or Alon–Friedland–Kalai) whose statement exactly matches the required implication.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The reference to Aharoni and Holzman (1998) is standard for this circular arc packing result. No additional assumptions, domain restrictions, or repairs were needed; the application to the discrete cupcake setting is direct and valid.
Decisive checks: 
- Lines 3–5: Verified that the hypothesis (each person can partition the circle into $n$ consecutive groups of score $\ge 1$) directly implies $\mathcal{F}_i$ contains $n$ pairwise disjoint blocks, hence $\nu(\mathcal{F}_i) \ge n$. Quantifiers and domains align perfectly with the problem statement.
- Lines 7–9: The cited theorem guarantees disjoint arcs $A_1, \dots, A_n$ with $A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i$. This is a verified combinatorial fact for circular interval families. No boundary or exceptional cases break the implication.
- Lines 11–14: Assignment of $A_i$ to $P_i$ yields score $\ge 1$ by definition of $\mathcal{F}_i$. Non-negativity of scores ensures leftover distribution cannot violate the condition. The derivation is arithmetically and logically sound.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The continuous relaxation and existence of a fractional partition into $n$ intervals $I_1, \dots, I_n$ with $\mu_i(I_i) \ge 1$ are plausible, but the discretization via greedy rounding is not rigorously justified. The argument establishes a fractional solution but fails to prove the existence of an integral assignment satisfying all constraints simultaneously.
Claim gap: Load-bearing gap in the greedy rounding procedure (Lines 18–21). The sequential assignment of split cupcakes does not guarantee that $P_n$ receives score $\ge 1$. The claim that "abundance of total score... ensures that the greedy rounding does not fail" is heuristic and mathematically insufficient. Additionally, the reference to "Woodall's Theorem (1980)" is misattributed; the continuous result is standardly due to Alon–Friedland–Kalai or Stein, not Woodall.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The continuous step requires a correct reference (e.g., Alon–Friedland–Kalai, 1993). The rounding step requires a substantive repair: sequential greedy rounding on shared resources can violate the last constraint unless supported by a matroid structure, network flow argument, or pipage rounding technique. The submission provides none of these. I supplied no repair; the gap remains unresolved in the text.
Decisive checks:
- Lines 1–5: The measure-theoretic setup correctly translates the discrete problem to a continuous one. The continuous partition existence is a known result, though the citation is incorrect.
- Lines 18–20 (Greedy rounding): Demonstrated defect. The proof claims $1-y_{k-1} \ge 1-\delta_{k-1}$ "whenever $y_{k-1}=0$", but ignores the case $y_{k-1}=1$, where $1-y_{k-1}=0$ may be strictly less than $1-\delta_{k-1}$. If the greedy choice for $P_{k-1}$ takes the split piece ($y_{k-1}=1$), $P_k$ loses that fractional contribution, and the proof offers no invariant to compensate. 
- Line 21 ($P_n$ constraint): Falsification check. Consider $n=2$, $m=3$. Suppose the continuous solution splits cupcake 2 with $\delta_1=0.9$. Greedy sets $y_1=1$ to satisfy $P_1$. $P_2$ then receives only the $0.1$ fraction of cupcake 2 plus other whole cupcakes. If $P_2$'s scores are concentrated on the split piece, $S_2$ can drop below 1. The appeal to "total score $\ge n$" does not prevent this localized starvation. The argument is mathematically incomplete.

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Proof A provides a complete, logically airtight argument by correctly reducing the problem to a known combinatorial theorem on circular arc families and applying it directly. Every step is verified, quantifiers are handled correctly, and the non-negativity of scores cleanly resolves the leftover distribution. Proof B attempts a constructive continuous-to-discrete rounding approach but contains a load-bearing defect in the greedy rounding step: it fails to guarantee the last person's constraint and incorrectly handles the dependency between sequential binary choices. The misattributed reference further undermines its rigor. Proof A's reliance on a standard, correctly stated theorem yields a fully justified solution, whereas Proof B's rounding argument remains heuristic and mathematically insufficient.
