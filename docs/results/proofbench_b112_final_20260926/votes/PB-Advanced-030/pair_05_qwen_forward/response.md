# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The continuous relaxation correctly translates discrete scores to non-negative measures on a circle of length $m$ (Lines 1-2). The hypothesis is accurately reformulated as the existence of integer-endpoint partitions with measure $\ge 1$ (Lines 3-4). The pigeonhole principle correctly guarantees an unsplit cupcake when $m>n$, enabling a valid linearization of the assignment structure (Lines 11-12).
Claim gap: The greedy rounding argument (Lines 18-21) fails to guarantee $S_k \ge 1$ when the preceding boundary choice forces $y_{k-1}=1$. Line 20 explicitly conditions the inequality $1-y_{k-1} \ge 1-\delta_{k-1}$ on $y_{k-1}=0$, but provides no justification for the $y_{k-1}=1$ case, where the left boundary term drops to $0$. The proof incorrectly assumes the gain from setting $y_k=1$ (collecting the full right cupcake) automatically compensates for the unbounded loss on the left, leaving the score guarantee for $P_k$ and $P_n$ unverified.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: Treated Woodall's Theorem as a stated premise without independent verification; accepted the continuous fractional solution as internally consistent. No repairs were supplied for the greedy rounding gap; it remains an unresolved technical step requiring a more rigorous error-propagation analysis or alternative discretization scheme.
Decisive checks: VERIFIED: Lines 1-8 correctly establish the continuous measure model and fractional partition. VERIFIED: Line 11 correctly uses $m>n$ to break circular symmetry. DEMONSTRATED DEFECT: Line 20's implication chain breaks when $y_{k-1}=1$. The fractional bound $(1-\delta_{k-1})L + M + \delta_k R \ge 1$ does not imply the integer bound $M + R \ge 1$ under the stated greedy rule, as the loss of the left term $(1-\delta_{k-1})L$ is unaccounted for. UNRESOLVED: Whether a modified rounding order or global score accounting can close this gap without introducing new lemmas.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The problem is correctly reformulated as finding a rainbow matching of size $n$ across families $\mathcal{F}_1, \dots, \mathcal{F}_n$ of circular intervals, where each family satisfies $\nu(\mathcal{F}_i) \ge n$ (Lines 1-7). The Aharoni-Berger condition for linear interval hypergraphs is accurately stated (Line 9).
Claim gap: The proof asserts a "known extension" to circular interval hypergraphs claiming $\nu_{\text{rainbow}} \ge \min_i \nu(\mathcal{F}_i)$ (Line 11). This claim is mathematically false for circular intervals and is not a standard result. The reduction to a rainbow matching also unnecessarily restricts each person's allocation to a single contiguous block, which is stronger than the problem requires but would be acceptable if the theorem were true.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: None supplied. The cited extension is treated as a black box; no verification of its applicability to circular topology is provided. The proof contains no constructive or analytical steps to bridge the gap between the hypothesis and the conclusion.
Decisive checks: VERIFIED: Lines 1-7 correctly define $\mathcal{F}_i$ and translate the hypothesis to $\nu(\mathcal{F}_i) \ge n$. VERIFIED: Line 9 correctly states the Aharoni-Berger condition for linear intervals. DEMONSTRATED DEFECT: Line 11's claim is false. Counterexample: Let $n=2$ and the circle have 4 cupcakes. Let $\mathcal{F}_1$ contain intervals $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$, and $\mathcal{F}_2$ contain $\{2,3\}$ and $\{4,1\}$. Both families have $\nu=2$, but every pair $(S_1, S_2)$ with $S_1 \in \mathcal{F}_1, S_2 \in \mathcal{F}_2$ intersects. Thus $\nu_{\text{rainbow}} = 1 < 2$, contradicting the claimed bound. The proof's central implication collapses on this false premise.

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Both submissions contain fatal gaps, but Proof A's framework is mathematically coherent and its defect is localized to a standard discretization step (greedy rounding) that fails to account for cumulative boundary losses. This is a technical deficiency that can be addressed with refined rounding or flow arguments. Proof B, by contrast, reduces the entire problem to a cited theorem that is demonstrably false for circular intervals (as verified by the $n=2$ counterexample). B's gap is foundational and invalidates the core implication, whereas A's continuous relaxation and linearization contain verified substantive progress. A's mathematical structure is significantly more rigorous and transparent.
