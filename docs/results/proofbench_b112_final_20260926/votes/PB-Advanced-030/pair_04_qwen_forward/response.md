# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The proof correctly establishes a continuous measure model, correctly applies Woodall's Theorem (1980) to obtain a fractional partition $I_1, \dots, I_n$ with $\mu_i(I_i) \ge 1$, and correctly handles the $m=n$ case. For $m>n$, it correctly identifies that at least one cupcake remains unsplit, allowing a linearization of the assignment problem and a correct formulation of the greedy rounding variables $y_k$.
Claim gap: The greedy rounding argument fails to verify the constraint for person $P_n$ when the preceding decision forces $y_{n-1}=1$. The claim that $\sum_{j=1}^m s_{n,j} \ge n$ guarantees $S_n \ge 1$ is mathematically insufficient: the total score bound does not control the score of the specific subset $W_n \cup \text{left part of } C_{s_{n-1}}$ allocated to $P_n$, and the greedy choices for $P_1, \dots, P_{n-1}$ may have consumed the high-scoring cupcakes needed by $P_n$. This leaves the final obligation unverified.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The gap in line 21 is substantive and unresolved. Repairing it would require a different rounding strategy (e.g., analyzing the cyclic dependency via parity/alternating sums or proving that at least one integral choice among the $2^{n-1}$ possibilities satisfies all constraints), which is absent from the submission.
Decisive checks: Lines 18-21 attempt sequential greedy rounding. Verified: The logic for $P_1$ and $P_k$ ($1<k<n$) correctly uses $1-y_{k-1} \ge 1-\delta_{k-1}$ and $s_{k,s_k} \ge \delta_k s_{k,s_k}$ to preserve the $\ge 1$ bound. Demonstrated defect: For $P_n$, if $y_{n-1}=1$, the term $(1-y_{n-1})s_{n,s_{n-1}}$ vanishes. The fractional guarantee involves a right-split term $\delta_n s_{n,s_n}$ that does not exist in $S_n$ (only $n-1$ splits occur). The fallback to $\sum s_{n,j} \ge n$ does not imply $S_n \ge 1$ for the specific subset chosen by the greedy process. A counterexample can be constructed where $P_n$'s high-scoring cupcakes are all in $W_1, \dots, W_{n-1}$ or in the right halves of split cupcakes, leaving $S_n < 1$ when $y_{n-1}=1$, while still satisfying all hypotheses.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The proof correctly defines $\mathcal{F}_i$ as the family of consecutive blocks with $P_i$-score $\ge 1$. It correctly deduces $\nu(\mathcal{F}_i) \ge n$ from the partition hypothesis. It correctly applies the Aharoni-Holzman (1998) theorem on circular arc families to obtain pairwise disjoint blocks $A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i$. It correctly assigns $A_i$ to $P_i$ and distributes remaining cupcakes arbitrarily, noting non-negativity preserves the $\ge 1$ score. The conclusion follows rigorously.
Claim gap: NONE supported by checks. The logical chain is complete and directly yields the required distribution.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The cited theorem is a standard result in combinatorial fair division, and its statement matches the hypotheses exactly. The application and conclusion require no additional assumptions or domain adjustments; discrete consecutive blocks of cupcakes are valid instances of arcs on a circle, and the theorem applies directly.
Decisive checks: Line 5 correctly translates the partition hypothesis into $\nu(\mathcal{F}_i) \ge n$. Line 7 correctly states the Aharoni-Holzman theorem. Line 9 correctly instantiates the theorem to get disjoint $A_i$. Lines 11-14 correctly handle the assignment and remaining cupcakes. Verified: Disjointness of $A_i$ ensures each cupcake in a block goes to exactly one person. Non-negativity ensures arbitrary distribution of leftovers cannot decrease scores. The conclusion follows rigorously from the premises and the cited theorem.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a complete, logically airtight argument by correctly identifying the combinatorial structure of the problem and applying a known theorem (Aharoni-Holzman) that directly yields the required disjoint assignment. Proof A, while setting up a valid continuous model and handling the $m=n$ case correctly, contains a demonstrated defect in its discrete rounding step: the greedy algorithm fails to guarantee $S_n \ge 1$ when $y_{n-1}=1$, and the fallback appeal to total score abundance is mathematically insufficient to control the specific subset allocated to $P_n$. Proof B's approach avoids this gap entirely and satisfies all problem obligations without unverified claims.
