# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The continuous relaxation correctly establishes that the hypothesis implies $\mu_i(\text{Circle}) \ge n$ for each person. By invoking a continuous fair division result (Stromquist-Woodall/Woodall), it correctly deduces the existence of a common partition of the circle into $n$ intervals $I_1, \dots, I_n$ such that $\mu_i(I_i) \ge 1$ for all $i$. It also correctly identifies that since $m > n$, at least one cupcake remains unsplit by the $n$ boundaries, allowing the circular dependency to be broken and the problem linearized.
Claim gap: The greedy rounding procedure (lines 18-21) fails to guarantee $S_k \ge 1$ when the previous person's greedy choice forces $y_{k-1}=1$. The inequality $1-y_{k-1} \ge 1-\delta_{k-1}$ used in line 20 is false when $y_{k-1}=1$ and $\delta_{k-1} < 1$, meaning $P_k$ may lose more score on the left boundary than it gains by taking the full right boundary. The hand-waving in line 21 about "abundance of total score" does not mathematically bridge this deficit or provide a rigorous exchange mechanism.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The continuous reduction, measure definition, and linearization are credited as valid. The greedy step's inequality direction is flagged as a demonstrated defect. No external lemmas or rounding corrections are supplied.
Decisive checks: 
- Line 5: Woodall's theorem statement is slightly non-standard but mathematically sound here because the premise implies $\mu_i(\text{Circle}) \ge n$, so equal division yields $\mu_i(I_i) \ge 1$. (VERIFIED)
- Line 11: Linearization via an unsplit cupcake is correct for $m > n$. (VERIFIED)
- Line 20: Falsification check: Let $\delta_{k-1} = 0.9$, $\delta_k = 0.1$, $s_{k, s_{k-1}} = 10$, $s_{k, s_k} = 1$, and interior sum $= 0$. Fractional score $= 0.1(10) + 0 + 0.1(1) = 1.1 \ge 1$. If $y_{k-1}=1$ (forced by $P_{k-1}$), $P_k$ gets $0$ from left. Greedy sets $y_k=1$, giving $P_k$ score $0 + 0 + 1 = 1$. This works, but if interior sum $= 0.05$, fractional $= 1.15$, discrete $= 1.05 \ge 1$. However, if $s_{k, s_{k-1}} = 100$, $\delta_{k-1}=0.99$, $\delta_k=0.01$, interior $= 0$, fractional $= 1.01$. If $y_{k-1}=1$, $P_k$ gets $0$ from left, takes full right ($0.01 \times 100 = 1$), total $= 1$. The claim holds in these cases, but the proof's justification "$1-y_{k-1} \ge 1-\delta_{k-1}$" is logically invalid for $y_{k-1}=1$. The argument lacks a rigorous exchange or compensation mechanism for the case where the left loss exceeds the right gain. (DEMONSTRATED DEFECT in justification; the claim may be salvageable with a more careful argument, but as written it is incomplete.)

## Proof B
Established theorem: Correctly models the problem with measures and notes $\mu_i(\text{Circle}) \ge n$. Correctly applies the Stromquist-Woodall theorem to obtain a continuous partition $J_1, \dots, J_n$ with $\mu_i(J_i) \ge 1$. Correctly sets up the shifting discretization $S_k(\theta)$ and defines the score functions $f_{i,k}(\theta)$.
Claim gap: Two independent gaps undermine the conclusion. First, the integral identity in line 10 is mathematically incorrect: $\int_0^1 \mathbb{I}(j-1 \in J_k + \theta) d\theta$ does not equal $\text{length}(J_k \cap [j-2, j-1))$ (or even $[j-1, j)$); the measure of shifts covering a fixed point depends on the global position of $J_k$ modulo $m$, not just local overlap. Second, the core existence claim in line 17 is unjustified. The proof asserts that because $\int_0^1 f_{i,i}(\theta) d\theta \ge 1$ and $\sum_k f_{i,k}(\theta) \ge n$, there must exist a $\theta$ where the bipartite graph $G_\theta$ has a perfect matching. Averaging properties do not guarantee the existence of a perfect matching in any single instance; the sets of $\theta$ where individual edges exist may be disjoint or fail Hall's condition simultaneously. The appeal to "fair division context" and "average density" is a non-sequitur without a supporting combinatorial or topological lemma.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: NONE. The continuous reduction and shifting setup are credited as valid. The integral calculation and matching existence claim are flagged as demonstrated defects. No external lemmas or averaging corrections are supplied.
Decisive checks:
- Line 5: Stromquist-Woodall application is correct and yields $\mu_i(J_i) \ge 1$. (VERIFIED)
- Line 10: Falsification check: Let $m=10$, $J_k=[0, 2.5)$, $j=3$ (cupcake $[2,3)$). Point $2$ falls in $J_k+\theta$ iff $\theta \in (-0.5, 2]$. Intersecting with $\theta \in [0,1)$ gives measure $1$. But $\text{length}(J_k \cap [2,3)) = 0.5$. The claimed equality fails. (DEMONSTRATED DEFECT)
- Line 17: Falsification check: Even if the integral were correct, $\int f_{i,i} \ge 1$ only bounds the average. One can construct score distributions where $f_{i,i}(\theta) < 1$ for all $\theta$, while cross-terms compensate in the sum, yet no single $\theta$ supports a perfect matching due to misaligned high-score blocks. The proof provides no argument (e.g., via Hall's condition, intermediate value theorem, or measure of bad sets) to rule this out. (DEMONSTRATED DEFECT: logical leap from average to existence.)

## Decision
Winner: A
Reason: Both proofs correctly reduce the problem to a continuous fair division setting and identify the need for discretization. Proof A's gap is localized to the greedy rounding step, where the inequality justification fails when a previous person claims a boundary cupcake; however, the overall strategy (continuous partition $\to$ linearization $\to$ rounding) is structurally sound and the defect is a technical inequality that could be repaired with a standard exchange argument. Proof B contains two independent defects: a mathematically incorrect integral identity in the averaging step, and a fundamental logical leap asserting that favorable averages guarantee a perfect matching for some shift. Proof A provides more substantive, verifiable progress and a clearer path to the conclusion, making it the stronger submission despite its local flaw.
