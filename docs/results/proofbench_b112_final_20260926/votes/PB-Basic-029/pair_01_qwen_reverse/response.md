# Proof comparison

## Proof A
Established theorem: The number of red points is at least $\binom{p+2}{2}$, derived from the cited lower bound $v_k \ge k+1$ for vertices of level $k$ in a line arrangement. The submission claims this bound is tight via a tangent-circle construction.
Claim gap: The lower bound relies entirely on an external citation without derivation. The tightness construction contains a demonstrated geometric impossibility: it places $O$ at the center of a circle tangent to $p+2$ lines, forming a convex polygon containing $O$. It then claims new intersections $X_{ik}$ can be placed so that segment $OX_{ik}$ crosses at least $p+1$ lines of $S_0$. However, any ray from an interior point of a convex polygon intersects the boundary exactly once, meaning $OX_{ik}$ can cross at most 1 line of $S_0$. Thus, the construction fails to ensure $d(X,O) \ge p+1$ for the added intersections, leaving the tightness claim unverified and geometrically inconsistent as written.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The level bound $v_k \ge k+1$ is a standard theorem and is accepted as correct. The construction's feasibility is plausible with a different arrangement (e.g., placing $O$ outside the convex hull of $S_0$), but the submission's specific geometric setup contradicts its own crossing claim. No repair is supplied; the defect remains in the tightness argument.
Decisive checks: Step 5 correctly identifies red points as vertices of levels $\le p$. Step 6's summation is arithmetically correct. Step 9 correctly computes $d_{S_0}(X_{ij}, O) = j-i-1 \le p$ for the initial set. Step 11's claim that $OX_{ik}$ crosses $\ge p+1$ lines of $S_0$ is falsified by convexity: $O$ is strictly inside the polygon formed by $S_0$, so any segment from $O$ to an exterior point crosses exactly one $S_0$ line. This is a demonstrated defect.

## Proof B
Established theorem: The minimum number of red points is exactly $\binom{p+2}{2}$. The lower bound is established via induction on the dual point set, and tightness is proven with an explicit algebraic construction.
Claim gap: The inductive step (Step 15) asserts that a hull point $P$ lies in the upper half-plane of at most $p+1$ edges of the $p$-level chain. This is a standard geometric lemma in $k$-set theory but is stated without proof. Aside from this omitted justification, the inductive structure and arithmetic are complete.
Qualifications and supplied repairs: The duality mapping and half-plane correspondence are routine and correct. The induction base case is verified. The loss bound $\le p+1$ is a known property of levels (the $p$-level is a convex chain, and a point outside the hull can be above at most $p+1$ of its edges); accepting this standard lemma closes the induction. The explicit construction (Steps 17-20) is fully verified algebraically and requires no repair.
Decisive checks: Step 5-6 correctly translates the problem to counting edges with $\le p$ points in a half-plane. Step 12 correctly notes that for a hull point, half-plane counts take all values $0$ to $n-1$, yielding exactly $p+1$ new red edges. Step 13-14 correctly accounts for potential loss of red status. Step 17-20's construction $L_i: y=ix+i^2$ is rigorously verified: intersection coordinates are correct, the condition $(k-i)(k-j)>0$ correctly identifies lines above $X_{ij}$, the count $(i-1)+(n-j)$ is exact, and the summation $\sum_{k=1}^{p+1} k = \binom{p+2}{2}$ is arithmetically sound. The construction independently confirms tightness without geometric hand-waving.

## Decision
Winner: B
Reason: Proof B provides a logically sound inductive framework for the lower bound and a fully verified explicit algebraic construction for tightness. While it omits a brief justification for a standard level-chain lemma, its argument is complete and its construction is rigorously checked. Proof A relies on an uncited theorem and contains a demonstrated geometric impossibility in its construction (claiming a ray from an interior point crosses $\ge p+1$ boundary lines of a convex polygon). B's explicit verification and correct geometric reasoning make it mathematically stronger and complete as written.
